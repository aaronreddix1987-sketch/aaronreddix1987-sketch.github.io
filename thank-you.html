Cold Call Code: A Field Guide to the First Sixty Seconds

This guide teaches one skill: how to call someone who is not expecting you, earn a fair hearing in under a minute, and leave with a clear next step. It is written for owners and salespeople at small service businesses. It takes about 30 minutes to read and 10 days to practice.

How to use this guide

  1. Read Parts 1 and 2 once. They set the goal and the preparation.
  2. Learn the five rules in Part 3. Say the example lines out loud.
  3. Copy the sample call in Part 4 and rewrite it in your own words.
  4. Read Part 6 before you dial anyone. The law matters.
  5. Follow the 10-day plan in Part 7 and keep the scorecard.

Part 1. What a cold call is for

A cold call is not for selling. It is for earning the next conversation. When researchers at Gong studied cold calls, they counted a call as a success only if it led to a follow-up meeting that actually took place. Use the same test. Your goal on every call is one small yes: a callback time, a short meeting, or permission to send something.

Expect most calls to end without that yes. Gong reports that the average success rate can be as low as 1.48 percent. That is not a reason to quit. It is a reason to prepare, because small improvements in the first minute change the result a great deal.

Practice: Write the one outcome you want from your calls in ten words or fewer. Example: "A 15-minute call on their calendar this week."

Part 2. Before you dial

Build a short, honest list

Twenty well-chosen names beat two hundred random ones. Choose businesses that clearly have the problem you solve. If you help shops that miss calls, look for shops with one phone line, busy hours and no online booking.

Do three minutes of homework

Before each call, find three facts in three minutes: what they sell, who answers their phone, and one recent thing about them, such as a new location, a review or a job posting. One true detail in your first sentence proves you did not dial at random.

Write your reason in one sentence

Finish this sentence before you call: "The reason for my call is ___." If you cannot finish it in one breath, you are not ready to dial.

Know your numbers

Work backward from your goal. If you want 2 new meetings a week and 1 conversation in 10 turns into a meeting, you need 20 real conversations. If 1 dial in 5 reaches a person, that is 100 dials a week, or 20 each working day. These rates are examples. Replace them with your own after your first week.

Practice: Fill in your own line. "I need ___ meetings a week. That takes about ___ conversations and ___ dials a day."

Part 3. The five rules

Rule 1. The script is a skeleton, not a cage

A script gives you the order of the call. It does not give you the words. People can hear reading. Learn the order so well that you can respond to what the other person actually says and still find your way back.

  • Do: memorize your first two sentences and your closing ask. Improvise the middle.
  • Avoid: reading a paragraph word for word. Plan what you will say, then say it in your own voice.

Drill: Record yourself saying your opening three times. Keep the version that sounds like you talking to a neighbor.

Rule 2. You have about seven seconds

Seven seconds is a working rule, not a measured fact. It reminds you that the opening has one job: to show you are a real person with a real reason. Research on openings is useful here.

  • In Gong's study of 90,380 cold calls, saying the reason for the call early was linked to a success rate 2.1 times higher.
  • Opening with "Did I catch you at a bad time?" was linked to 40 percent fewer meetings.
  • A direct, permission-based opening, where the caller admits it is a cold call and asks for 30 seconds, was among the strongest openers Gong tested.

Those numbers come from one company's recordings, mostly of software sales. Treat them as a starting point and test them in your own market.

A simple opening that follows the evidence:

"Hi Maria, this is Aaron with Total Transformation in North Hollywood. I'll be honest, this is a cold call. Can I take 30 seconds to tell you why I picked your shop, and then you decide if we keep talking?"

  • Do: say your name, your business and your town. Then give the reason or ask for 30 seconds.
  • Avoid: pretending to know them, hiding that it is a sales call, or starting with an apology.

Rule 3. Qualify before you pitch

Ask one question that shows whether they have the problem you solve. If they do not, thank them and end the call. If they do, you have earned a short explanation.

Keep it to one or two questions. Gong found that successful cold calls did not involve more questions than unsuccessful ones, and that the caller did more of the talking. A cold call is for earning the meeting. The long questions belong in the meeting itself.

A good qualifying question has three features. It is about their business, not your product. It can be answered in a few words. And a person with no need would honestly answer "no."

Example: "When you're with a customer and the phone rings, what happens to that call?"

  • Do: stay quiet after you ask. Let them finish.
  • Avoid: questions that only have one polite answer, such as "Would you like more customers?"

Rule 4. Never argue with a no

A first "no" is often a reflex, not a decision. You may ask one respectful follow-up. If the answer is still no, leave cleanly. Use three steps: acknowledge, ask once, accept.

  1. Acknowledge. "That's fair. I called out of nowhere."
  2. Ask once. "Before I go, is it that missed calls aren't a problem, or just that now is a bad time?"
  3. Accept. "Understood. Thanks for picking up. I won't call again."

Then keep your word. Write the number on your own do-not-call list. Part 6 explains why this is also a legal duty for many calls.

Check yourself: Am I listening, or waiting for my turn to talk?

Rule 5. End with one clear ask

Every call ends with a single, specific next step. Offer a time, not a favor.

Weak: "Can I send you some information?"
Strong: "Can I call you Thursday at 10 for fifteen minutes and show you what it caught for a shop like yours?"

  • Do: repeat the day and time back, and send a short text to confirm.
  • Avoid: two asks at once. One call, one next step.

Part 4. A full sample call

This example is for a business that helps shops with missed calls. Change the details to fit what you sell.

  1. Open. "Hi Maria, this is Aaron with Total Transformation in North Hollywood. I'll be honest, this is a cold call. Can I take 30 seconds to tell you why I picked your shop?"
  2. Reason. "I called yesterday at lunch and got voicemail. I help shops make sure a missed call gets answered right away, so the customer doesn't call the next place."
  3. Qualify. "When you're with a customer and the phone rings, what happens to that call?"
  4. Listen, then reflect. "So it goes to voicemail, and you call back when you can. Do most of them pick up when you call back?"
  5. Short pitch. "That's the gap we cover. For seven days we catch those calls, text them back and give you a list of everyone it caught. Then you decide."
  6. Ask. "Can I call you Thursday at 10 for fifteen minutes to show you how it would work for your shop?"
  7. Confirm. "Thursday at 10. I'll text you my name and number now so you know it's me."

If you reach voicemail

"Hi Maria, Aaron with Total Transformation in North Hollywood, 747-301-8586. I called your shop at lunch and got voicemail, which is exactly what I help with. I'll try you once more Thursday morning. 747-301-8586."

Keep it under 20 seconds. Say your number twice. Do not pitch.

The follow-up text

"Maria, this is Aaron from Total Transformation. Thanks for your time today. Confirming Thursday at 10:00 for 15 minutes. Reply STOP if you'd rather I not text."

Part 6. The rules of the road

This section is general information for callers in the United States. It is not legal advice. Rules differ by state and change over time. If calling is a large part of your business, speak with an attorney.

  • Calling hours. Under the Federal Trade Commission's Telemarketing Sales Rule, sales calls to a person's home are limited to between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. in the local time of the person you are calling, unless they agreed otherwise.
  • Calls to businesses. The FTC says most phone calls between a telemarketer and a business are exempt from the Telemarketing Sales Rule. One exception is selling office or cleaning supplies that get used up, such as paper and toner. State laws can be stricter.
  • Calls to consumers. The rule prohibits sales calls to numbers that consumers have placed on the National Do Not Call Registry. Check the registry before you call people at home.
  • "Do not call me again." Under the rule it is a violation to call a consumer who has asked not to be called again. Treat every such request the same way, business or home. Keep your own do-not-call list and honor it every time.
  • Be truthful. Say who you are and that it is a sales call. Do not mislead anyone about price or terms.
  • Recording. California requires the consent of everyone on the call before a confidential call is recorded, and before any call involving a cell or cordless phone is recorded (Penal Code sections 632 and 632.7). Ask first, or do not record.
  • Automated and AI voices. The FTC rule prohibits cold calls that deliver prerecorded messages. In February 2024 the Federal Communications Commission confirmed that calls using an AI-generated voice count as "artificial" voice calls and require the prior express consent of the person called. Make cold calls yourself, with your own voice.

The code on one page

  1. Open. Name, business, town. Be honest that it is a cold call.
  2. Reason. One sentence on why you chose them.
  3. Qualify. One real question. Then listen.
  4. Pitch. Two sentences, only after they describe the problem.
  5. Objection. Acknowledge, ask once, accept.
  6. Close. One ask with a day and a time. Confirm by text.
