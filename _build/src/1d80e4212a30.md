# How to Use This Book

This book is written for people who want to start a residential or small commercial cleaning business, and for owners in their first two years who want to put their operation on firmer ground. It assumes no business training. It does assume you are willing to time your work, write things down, read chemical labels, and price jobs from evidence rather than from guesses about what competitors charge.

Cleaning is often described as an easy business to enter, and in one sense it is: the equipment is inexpensive and no degree is required. But low barriers to entry cut both ways. They mean you will compete with many other operators, that customers can switch easily, and that the margin between a profitable job and an unprofitable one is often a single hour of underestimated labor or a few miles of unplanned driving. The businesses that last tend to be the ones that treat cleaning as a system: a defined service, a written scope, a costed price, a safe routine, a trained team, and a reliable schedule.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this book you should be able to:

- Describe the cleaning market using public labor data and choose one service lane to start.
- Register the business, understand licensing and insurance questions, and evaluate a franchise offer.
- Calculate the full cost of a cleaning job, including labor burden, travel, supplies, and overhead.
- Write a quote with a room-by-room scope, exclusions, and clear terms.
- Run a chemical safety program based on federal guidance, and never mix incompatible products.
- Build checklists and a quality control routine that a new team member can follow.
- Plan routes and schedules, and pay employees correctly for travel between jobs.
- Decide whether workers are employees or contractors, and keep the records labor laws require.
- Market honestly, collect genuine reviews, and convert one-time jobs into recurring service.
- Know when to hire help for your own role and how to protect customer keys and data.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, defines key terms, and presents a step-by-step framework. Every chapter includes a worked example with real arithmetic, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Each chapter also contains a case study drawn from public sources: government data and enforcement actions, court decisions, company filings, and founders' own published accounts. The cases include successes and failures, because both teach. The results in those cases belong to the people and organizations involved, not to Total Transformation Inc. (TTI).

The worked examples follow one fictional business, Brightline Cleaning Co., owned by a fictional entrepreneur named Keisha Monroe in Columbus, Ohio. Brightline begins with recurring residential cleaning. Its signature service is a biweekly standard clean of a three-bedroom, two-bath home of about 1,800 square feet, priced at $165 per visit. The numbers stay consistent from chapter to chapter so you can see how a single service moves through costing, quoting, scheduling, staffing, and growth. Brightline and Keisha are illustrative only; they are not TTI customers, and their figures are not benchmarks or promises.

# Chapter 1: The Cleaning Market

:::objectives
- Describe the size and structure of the cleaning workforce using public data.
- Distinguish residential, commercial, and specialty cleaning.
- Explain why low entry barriers raise the importance of systems.
- Assess your own starting position before choosing a lane.
:::

Every business plan begins with the market: who buys, what they buy, how often, and who else is selling. For cleaning, public labor statistics give a useful outline, even though they describe workers rather than businesses.

## What the data shows

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reports that janitors and building cleaners held about 2,432,600 jobs in 2025, with a median pay of $17.71 per hour, or $36,840 per year, in May 2025 [1]. BLS projects employment in the occupation to grow 2 percent from 2025 to 2035, slower than average, yet it still projects about 321,800 openings each year, mostly to replace workers who change occupations or leave the labor force [1]. In other words, turnover, not growth, drives most hiring in this field.

Residential cleaning is measured separately. BLS occupational data for maids and housekeeping cleaners, which covers workers who clean private homes as well as hotels and other establishments, estimated employment of 836,230 and a mean hourly wage of $16.66 in May 2023 [2]. Across the broader group of building and grounds cleaning occupations, BLS reports a median annual wage of $37,680 in May 2025, compared with $50,980 for all occupations, and about 730,600 openings projected each year [3].

These figures have three practical implications. First, labor is the main cost of cleaning, and local wage levels set a floor under your prices. Second, high turnover means hiring and training will be a permanent part of your job once you grow beyond yourself. Third, customers buy cleaning largely to save time and avoid an unpleasant chore, so reliability and trust matter as much as technique.

## Market segments

Cleaning work falls into three broad segments. Residential cleaning includes recurring home cleaning, one-time deep cleans, and move-in or move-out cleaning. Commercial cleaning includes offices, medical and dental suites, retail stores, and common areas in residential buildings, usually cleaned after hours under a monthly contract. Specialty cleaning includes carpet and upholstery, window washing, post-construction cleanup, and remediation work such as mold, biohazard, or fire damage, which often requires additional training, licensing, equipment, and insurance.

| Segment | Typical buyer | Pricing basis | Main risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recurring residential | Busy households | Per visit or flat recurring rate | Scope creep, cancellations, key security |
| Move-in/move-out | Tenants, landlords, agents | Per job, by size and condition | Unpredictable condition, deadline pressure |
| Small commercial | Office managers, property managers | Monthly contract | Bidding pressure, after-hours staffing |
| Specialty | Varies | Per job or per square foot | Licensing, equipment cost, liability |

## Why low barriers raise the stakes

Business survival is difficult in every industry. BLS research found that 34.7 percent of business establishments born in 2013 were still operating in 2023 [4]. In a business with low entry costs, the competitive edge comes from execution: accurate pricing, clear scope, consistent quality, safe practice, and dependable people. That is why most of this book is about systems rather than cleaning tips.

## A framework for assessing your starting position

1. List the cleaning work you have actually done, paid or unpaid, and how long each task took.
2. Note your constraints: hours available, transportation, physical limits, childcare, and startup cash.
3. Identify the segments you can serve with the equipment and skills you already have.
4. Talk with at least ten potential customers in one segment about what they buy now and what frustrates them.
5. Write down the price range they mention and the reasons they would switch.
6. Choose one segment to test first, and set a review date after thirty days.

:::case Clean My Space: learning the work before building the system (real, cited)
Melissa Maker founded Clean My Space, a home cleaning service in Toronto, in 2006 [6]. In her own account, her first job after university was unsatisfying, and although she says she disliked cleaning, she saw an opportunity: many people wanted a clean space but lacked the time or skills to do it themselves [5]. She writes that she had to learn to clean "from the ground up," and that after months of doing all the cleaning herself she used what she learned to develop a training system, hire staff, and grow the business [5]. In 2011 she began posting cleaning tips on YouTube to promote the service, which grew into a large media channel and later a microfiber products company [6]. The facts are from her published accounts, and the results are her own, not TTI's. For a new operator, the lesson is the sequence: do the work, measure it, write it down, and only then train others.
:::

:::example Keisha's market notes (illustrative)
Keisha interviews twelve households in two Columbus neighborhoods. Eight already pay for cleaning or have in the past. Six mention reliability ("the cleaner stopped showing up") as their main frustration, and four mention unclear pricing. Prices quoted for a standard biweekly clean of a three-bedroom home range from $140 to $190. Keisha times herself cleaning three homes of roughly 1,800 square feet: 3 hours 20 minutes, 3 hours 5 minutes, and 2 hours 50 minutes, an average of 3 hours 5 minutes. She records that the first visit to each home took longer because she was learning the layout. Her notes show that the market will bear a price in the $160 to $170 range if she can be reliable and clear, and that her own time per visit, not competitors' prices, should anchor her costing.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choosing Your Service Lane

:::objectives
- Define a service lane and explain why starting narrow helps.
- Compare residential and commercial lanes on cash flow, scheduling, and risk.
- Identify work to decline until you have specialist training and insurance.
- Write a one-sentence service definition.
:::

A service lane is a specific, repeatable service sold to a specific type of customer: for example, biweekly standard cleaning for occupied single-family homes, or three-night-per-week cleaning for offices under 5,000 square feet. Starting with one lane makes every other system easier. Your checklist is shorter, your time estimates become accurate faster, your supply kit is smaller, and your marketing message is clearer.

## Comparing lanes

Residential recurring work usually offers steady weekday daytime hours, frequent customer contact, and payment per visit. Move-out work pays well per job but arrives irregularly and often under deadline pressure, and the condition of the home can be hard to predict. Small commercial work is often scheduled in the evening, paid monthly under a contract, and less dependent on individual customer preferences, but it may require bidding, proof of insurance, and sometimes a bond. The right first lane depends on your schedule, your transportation, and the customers you can reach.

| Factor | Recurring residential | Move-in/move-out | Small commercial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Schedule | Weekday daytime | Irregular, deadline-driven | Evenings and weekends |
| Payment timing | At each visit | At job completion | Monthly, sometimes net 30 |
| Estimating difficulty | Low after first visit | High | Moderate, depends on walkthrough |
| Paperwork | Simple agreement | Job quote and photos | Contract, insurance certificate, possibly bond |
| Revenue stability | High if retained | Low | High if contract renews |

## Work to decline at the start

Some work carries risks that a new operator should not accept without specialist training, licensing, equipment, and insurance. Examples include mold remediation, biohazard or crime-scene cleanup, hoarding situations with structural or pest hazards, work at heights such as high exterior windows, pressure washing near electrical equipment, and cleaning after fire or flood damage. Declining politely and referring to a specialist protects your workers and your business.

## A framework for choosing a lane

1. List two or three candidate lanes from your Chapter 1 assessment.
2. For each, write the buyer, the frequency, the typical job size, and the payment timing.
3. Estimate how many hours per week each lane would require to reach your first income goal.
4. Check whether each lane requires insurance certificates, bonds, or licenses in your area.
5. Pick the lane with the best fit between your constraints and the buyer's needs.
6. Write a one-sentence service definition and a list of what is excluded.
7. Commit to that lane for at least ninety days before adding another.

:::case Quality Touch Cleaning Systems: a commercial niche built after prison (real, cited)
CalMatters reported in 2024 on Timothy Jackson, who spent 12 years in prison and never thought about becoming an entrepreneur until he met formerly incarcerated people who had started businesses [7]. He enrolled in a training program run by Defy Ventures, a national nonprofit, placed second in its business-pitch competition, and received a $7,000 grant to help start his business, along with a mentor who, he said, "was there every step of the way" [7]. Jackson was released in 2017 and started Quality Touch Cleaning Systems, a San Diego-area business, a year later; at the time of the report he had five employees plus a couple of independent contractors, and clients in biotech, health care, and other industries [7]. "Five, six years later and I'm signing checks," he told the publication [7]. The facts are from CalMatters' reporting, and the results are his own, not TTI's. His business illustrates a focused commercial lane serving specific industries, supported by training, seed capital, and mentorship.
:::

:::example Keisha chooses a lane (illustrative)
Keisha compares recurring residential cleaning with small-office cleaning. She has school-age children and a car available during the day, so evening office work would require childcare. Working alone, she can complete two homes per day, five days per week, or 10 visits; once she hires one cleaner, the two of them could complete about 16 visits per week. At $165 per visit, that is 16 × $165 = $2,640 in weekly revenue before costs. Office work would require a walkthrough and bid for each building, proof of insurance, and payment at month-end. She chooses recurring residential cleaning and writes her definition: "Biweekly and weekly standard cleaning for occupied homes up to 2,500 square feet in northeast Columbus." Her exclusions list includes inside ovens and refrigerators (available as add-ons), exterior windows, carpet shampooing, mold, biohazards, and anything above a two-step stool.
:::

:::checklist Lane decision
- Two or three candidate lanes listed.
- Buyer, frequency, job size, and payment timing written for each.
- Weekly hours needed for first income goal estimated.
- Insurance, bond, and license needs checked.
- One lane chosen and written as a single sentence.
- Exclusions and declined work listed.
- Ninety-day commitment date set.
:::

:::summary
A service lane pairs one repeatable service with one type of customer. Recurring residential, move-out, and small commercial lanes differ in schedule, payment timing, estimating difficulty, paperwork, and stability. Decline specialist work until you have the training, licensing, equipment, and insurance it demands. Timothy Jackson's Quality Touch Cleaning shows how a focused commercial lane, combined with training and mentorship, can become a stable business.
:::

# Chapter 3: Legal Setup and Insurance

:::objectives
- Choose a business structure with professional guidance.
- Identify registrations, licenses, and permits to verify locally.
- Distinguish general liability, workers' compensation, and bonds.
- Evaluate a franchise offer using the disclosure document.
:::

Legal setup is not glamorous, but it determines who is responsible when something goes wrong. A cleaning business enters private homes and offices, handles chemicals, and often holds keys and alarm codes. Mistakes and accidents are inevitable over time; the question is whether your structure, contracts, and insurance are ready for them.

## Business structure

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) explains that the structure you choose affects day-to-day operations, taxes, and how much of your personal assets are at risk [8]. Common choices are the sole proprietorship, the limited liability company (LLC), and corporations. A sole proprietorship is simple but does not separate personal and business liability. An LLC can offer liability protection while keeping tax filing relatively simple. Ask an accountant or attorney which structure suits your situation; the choice also affects how you pay yourself and how you file taxes.

## Registrations and licenses

The SBA notes that licensing and permit requirements depend on your business activity and location, and that federal, state, and local agencies may each have requirements [9]. For cleaning, typical items to check include state business registration, a federal employer identification number (EIN) if you will have employees or form an LLC or corporation, a local business license, sales tax registration if your state taxes cleaning services, and any city requirements for home-based businesses. Some states also have rules for specific services such as pest control or biohazard work. Call or visit your city and state offices; do not rely on another operator's experience in a different city.

## Insurance and bonds

The SBA explains that business insurance protects against unexpected costs, and that businesses with employees are generally required to carry workers' compensation, unemployment, and, in some states, disability insurance [10]. General liability insurance covers claims such as property damage or injury to third parties; for a cleaner, that might include a broken heirloom, a slip on a freshly mopped floor, or water damage. Commercial auto coverage may be needed if you use a vehicle for business. Many commercial clients ask for a certificate of insurance before you start work.

Bonds work differently from insurance. The SBA describes surety bonds as guarantees to a customer that work will be completed, and notes that many public and private contracts require them; it distinguishes contract bonds, which ensure the terms of a specific contract are fulfilled, from commercial bonds, which ensure that laws and regulations are followed [11]. In the cleaning industry, owners often buy a janitorial or "dishonesty" bond to reassure customers about theft. Ask an insurance agent to explain exactly what a bond pays, to whom, and when.

| Protection | What it typically covers | Who usually requires it |
|---|---|---|
| General liability | Third-party injury and property damage | Commercial clients, landlords, some homeowners |
| Workers' compensation | Employee work injuries | State law, once you have employees |
| Commercial auto | Vehicle accidents during business use | Insurers; required if business-owned vehicle |
| Janitorial bond | Theft by employees, as defined in the bond | Some customers and contracts |
| Contract surety bond | Completion of a specific contract | Public and some private contracts |

## Franchises

Some new owners consider buying a cleaning franchise. A franchise can provide a brand, training, and systems, but it also brings fees and contractual obligations. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) explains that under its Franchise Rule, you must receive the franchisor's Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) at least 14 days before you are asked to sign any contract or pay any money [12]. The FTC guide suggests reviewing the document carefully, talking with current and former franchisees, and getting independent professional advice before investing [12].

## A framework for legal setup

1. Choose a business structure with advice from an accountant or attorney.
2. Register the business name and entity with your state.
3. Obtain an EIN and check state tax registrations.
4. Check city and county business license and home-occupation rules.
5. Get quotes for general liability and, before hiring, workers' compensation.
6. Decide whether a janitorial bond is expected by your customers.
7. Open a separate business bank account.
8. If considering a franchise, obtain the FDD, interview franchisees, and get independent advice.

:::case Awuah v. Coverall: when franchise terms meet wage law (real, cited)
Coverall North America sold commercial cleaning franchises in Massachusetts. A federal judge ruled that Coverall had misclassified as independent contractors the plaintiffs who were Massachusetts residents, and certified questions about damages to the state's highest court [13]. In 2011, the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court held that Coverall's system of paying workers through customer accounts-receivable financing, under which they were paid when the customer paid rather than when the work was performed, improperly deferred earned wages; that an employer could not deduct the cost of workers' compensation and other insurance from an employee's earned wages; and that the state Wage Act forbade deducting "franchise fees" from such wages [13]. The facts are from the court's published decision, and the outcome concerns those parties, not TTI. The case shows why anyone buying or selling a cleaning franchise should understand how state employment law treats the relationship, and why the FDD review the FTC recommends deserves independent legal advice.
:::

:::example Keisha's setup budget (illustrative)
Keisha forms a single-member LLC after talking with an accountant. Her setup costs are: state LLC filing $99, city business license $50, a general liability policy quoted at $65 per month, and a janitorial bond at $125 per year. The first-year cost of these items is $99 + $50 + (12 × $65) + $125 = $99 + $50 + $780 + $125 = $1,054. She records each item as a business expense, opens a business checking account, and stores her certificate of insurance as a PDF so she can send it to any client who asks. She notes that workers' compensation will be added before her first hire, and that she must confirm Ohio's specific requirements at that time.
:::

Your structure, registrations, insurance, and bonds determine how well your business absorbs mistakes and accidents. Follow SBA guidance, verify local requirements directly, and understand the difference between insurance and bonds. If you consider a franchise, use the FTC's 14-day disclosure period to review the FDD and seek independent advice. # Chapter 4: Costing a Cleaning Job

:::objectives
- Identify every cost that belongs in a cleaning job.
- Calculate burdened labor cost and travel cost.
- Allocate overhead per job.
- Set a price that covers full cost and a target margin.
:::

Pricing by copying competitors is risky because you do not know their costs, their speed, or whether they are profitable. Costing means building a price from the bottom up: what the job actually costs you, plus the margin you need to reinvest, save, and survive slow months.

## Cost categories

Direct labor is the time spent cleaning, multiplied by the hourly wage. Labor burden is the additional cost of employing that labor: employer payroll taxes, workers' compensation premiums, and any benefits. Travel cost includes vehicle expense and, for employees, paid time driving between jobs (discussed in Chapter 8). Supplies include chemicals, microfiber, bags, and replacement pads, usually estimated per job. Overhead includes insurance, software, phone, marketing, equipment replacement, and the owner's administrative time, spread across all jobs.

## Contract types

Large cleaning firms use more than one pricing structure. ABM Industries, a large building services company, explained in its 2007 annual report that its janitorial segment operated under thousands of individually negotiated contracts, nearly all obtained by competitive bidding, and that these contracts were either fixed-price agreements or "cost-plus," under which the customer reimburses agreed wages, benefits, payroll taxes, insurance, and other expenses plus a profit percentage [14]. Small operators almost always quote fixed prices, which means the risk of underestimating time falls entirely on the cleaner.

## Vehicle costs

The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) publishes standard mileage rates; its rates page lists a business rate of 76 cents per mile for July through December 2026 [15]. Whether you deduct actual expenses or the standard rate for taxes is a question for your tax professional, but the standard rate is a reasonable planning estimate for what driving really costs. IRS Publication 583 explains the records small businesses should keep to support deductions, including vehicle expenses [16].

## The costing framework

1. Measure cleaning time for the service, using your own timed visits.
2. Multiply hours by the hourly wage and add labor burden.
3. Estimate average miles and drive time per job; cost both.
4. Estimate supplies per job from a month of purchases divided by jobs completed.
5. Divide monthly overhead by expected monthly jobs to get overhead per job.
6. Add all costs to find full cost per job.
7. Set price to achieve your target margin, and compare it with the market range.
8. Recalculate quarterly or whenever wages, rates, or routes change.

:::example Costing Brightline's signature clean (illustrative)
Keisha plans for an employee cleaner paid $20 per hour. She estimates labor burden (employer payroll taxes and workers' compensation) at 15 percent, so burdened labor costs $20 × 1.15 = $23 per hour. The standard clean takes 3.0 hours, so labor is 3.0 × $23 = $69.00. Supplies average $6.00 per visit. Each visit adds about 12 miles at the IRS planning rate of $0.76, or 12 × $0.76 = $9.12, plus about 0.4 hours of paid drive time between jobs, or 0.4 × $23 = $9.20. Monthly overhead of $2,400 spread across 120 expected visits is $2,400 ÷ 120 = $20.00 per visit.

| Cost item | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Burdened labor | 3.0 hours × $23 | $69.00 |
| Supplies | Monthly average | $6.00 |
| Vehicle | 12 miles × $0.76 | $9.12 |
| Drive time | 0.4 hours × $23 | $9.20 |
| Overhead | $2,400 ÷ 120 visits | $20.00 |
| Full cost | Sum | $113.32 |
| Price | Biweekly standard | $165.00 |
| Profit | $165.00 − $113.32 | $51.68 |
| Margin | $51.68 ÷ $165.00 | 31.3% |

If the clean actually takes 3.5 hours, labor rises to 3.5 × $23 = $80.50, full cost becomes $124.82, and margin falls to ($165.00 − $124.82) ÷ $165.00 = 24.4%. Half an hour of underestimated time costs nearly seven points of margin.
:::

:::case ABM Industries: fixed price versus cost-plus (real, cited)
ABM Industries' annual report for fiscal 2007 described how its janitorial segment priced work [14]. Nearly all of its building maintenance contracts were obtained by competitive bidding, and they were structured either as fixed-price agreements or as cost-plus arrangements in which the customer reimbursed agreed labor, payroll tax, insurance, and other expenses plus a profit percentage [14]. The report also noted that profit margins on contracts tended to be inversely related to contract size, and described low costs of entry in the facility services business [14]. The facts are from the company's filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and the figures and outcomes are ABM's, not TTI's. For a small operator, the lesson is that a fixed price transfers estimating risk to you, which makes timing your work and costing every component essential, and that larger contracts may carry thinner margins.
:::

# Chapter 5: Quotes, Scope, and Terms

:::objectives
- Decide when to quote remotely and when to require a walkthrough.
- Write a room-by-room scope with explicit exclusions.
- Set clear terms for payment, cancellation, and access.
- Present prices and offers honestly.
:::

Most disputes in cleaning are not about effort; they are about expectations. A customer who hears "deep clean" may picture the inside of the oven, the blinds, and the baseboards in every room, while the cleaner priced a three-hour surface clean. A written scope converts a vague promise into a list both sides can check.

## Intake and walkthroughs

An intake call or form gathers the facts needed to estimate time: square footage, bedrooms and bathrooms, number of occupants, pets, the date of the last professional cleaning, flooring types, special requests, and access instructions. For a recurring clean of a typical, well-maintained home, a remote quote based on photos and intake answers may be enough. For move-out jobs, homes that have not been cleaned professionally in months, homes with heavy pet hair, and any commercial space, a walkthrough is safer. Many operators quote the first visit as a longer "initial clean" at a higher price and the recurring visits at the standard rate.

## Writing scope

Scope is a room-by-room list of tasks included in the price. Exclusions are tasks not included. Add-ons are tasks available for an additional, stated price. A good scope uses observable language: "wipe exterior of kitchen appliances" rather than "clean kitchen," and "vacuum and damp-mop all hard floors" rather than "do floors."

| Room | Included | Excluded | Add-on price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | Counters, sink, exterior of appliances, microwave interior, floor | Inside oven, inside refrigerator, cabinet interiors | Oven $35; refrigerator $35 |
| Bathrooms | Toilet, tub/shower, sink, mirror, floor | Grout restoration, mold treatment | Not offered |
| Bedrooms | Dust reachable surfaces, make beds with clean linens provided, vacuum | Laundry, closet organizing | Laundry $20 per load |
| Living areas | Dust, vacuum, damp-mop, empty trash | Wall washing, upholstery shampoo | Not offered |
| Whole home | Baseboards reachable from floor | Exterior windows, anything above step-stool height | Interior windows $5 each |

## Terms

Terms explain how the relationship works. Include the price per visit and what it covers; when payment is due and how; the cancellation and lockout policy (for example, a fee if the cleaner arrives and cannot enter); what happens with damage or breakage and how to report it; how keys, codes, and pets are handled; and how either party can end recurring service. Keep the language plain and the document short enough that customers actually read it.

## Honest offers

Introductory discounts are common, but every material term must be disclosed clearly. The FTC's advertising guidance for small businesses explains that advertising must be truthful and not misleading, and that claims must be backed up with evidence [18]. If a discounted first clean comes with a required membership or minimum commitment, say so plainly and before the customer agrees.

## The quoting framework

1. Collect intake facts on every inquiry, using the same form.
2. Decide remote quote or walkthrough using written criteria.
3. Estimate hours from your timing data and calculate price from your cost model.
4. Write the room-by-room scope, exclusions, and add-ons.
5. Attach terms covering payment, cancellation, access, damage, and ending service.
6. Send the quote in writing and obtain written acceptance before the first visit.
7. After the first visit, compare actual time with the estimate and adjust the recurring price if needed, with notice.

# Chapter 6: Chemical and Worker Safety

:::objectives
- Explain the main requirements of OSHA's Hazard Communication Standard.
- Identify dangerous chemical combinations and safe use practices.
- Distinguish cleaning, sanitizing, and disinfecting.
- Build a written safety routine for a small team.
:::

Cleaning is physical work with real hazards: chemical exposure, slips and falls, lifting strain, and repetitive motion. A safety program protects your workers, your customers, and your business. It also signals professionalism to clients who are trusting you inside their homes and offices.

## Hazard communication

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) Hazard Communication Standard, 29 CFR 1910.1200, exists to ensure that the hazards of chemicals are classified and that information about those hazards is transmitted to employers and employees through container labeling and other warnings, safety data sheets, and employee training [19]. OSHA publishes a small entity compliance guide to help small employers meet the standard; the guide describes it as advisory and notes that the standard addresses the information needs of employers and workers with regard to chemicals [20]. In practice, employers with employees who use hazardous chemicals need a written hazard communication program, a list of the chemicals used, a safety data sheet (SDS) for each, properly labeled containers including any secondary spray bottles, and training before workers handle the chemicals.

## Never mix

OSHA's guidance on protecting workers who use cleaning chemicals warns that mixing cleaning products containing bleach and ammonia can cause severe lung damage or death [21]. OSHA's worker fact sheet adds that employers are required to provide a safe workplace including sufficient ventilation, protective clothing, gloves and safety goggles when needed, and labels on containers of cleaning chemicals [38]. A simple rule for a cleaning business: use one product per task, never combine products, and never pour one product into another product's container.

## Cleaning, sanitizing, and disinfecting

Cleaning removes dirt and many germs from surfaces. Sanitizing reduces germs to levels considered safe. Disinfecting kills germs listed on the product label when used as directed. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) notes that disinfectants are pesticides, can be harmful, and that users should read and understand the label's precautions and directions for use, which include how long the surface must stay wet (often called contact or dwell time) [24]. A disinfectant wiped off immediately may not achieve what the label claims. Where full disinfection is not required, EPA's Safer Choice program identifies products whose ingredients meet its safety criteria [23].

| Hazard | Example | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical reaction | Bleach plus ammonia or acids | One product per task; never mix |
| Skin and eye exposure | Splashes from concentrates | Gloves, goggles, pre-diluted products |
| Inhalation | Sprays in small bathrooms | Ventilation, spray onto cloth not into air |
| Slips | Wet floors | Wet-floor signs, mop in sections, proper shoes |
| Lifting strain | Vacuums on stairs, buckets | Lightweight tools, two-handed carries, partial buckets |
| Unknown substances | Customer's products, spills | Use only your own labeled products; decline unknown hazards |

## A framework for a safety routine

1. List every chemical your business uses and download its SDS.
2. Reduce the list to the fewest products that do the job; prefer ready-to-use or pre-diluted formulas.
3. Label every container, including spray bottles, with product name and hazards.
4. Write a one-page hazard communication program and keep SDS copies in each vehicle and on your phone.
5. Train every worker before first use, and record the date and topics.
6. Supply gloves, eye protection, and wet-floor signs; set a rule for ventilation.
7. Prohibit mixing and the use of customers' chemicals.
8. Review incidents and near misses monthly and update the routine.

:::case Poison center calls in early 2020 (real, cited)
In April 2020, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) published a report on calls to U.S. poison centers [22]. From January through March 2020, poison centers received 45,550 exposure calls related to cleaners (28,158) and disinfectants (17,392), increases of 20.4 percent over the same period in 2019 and 16.4 percent over 2018 [22]. Among cleaners, bleaches accounted for the largest share of the increase, and inhalation exposures to disinfectants rose 108.8 percent [22]. The report described an adult who mixed a bleach solution with vinegar and hot water to soak produce, noticed a chlorine smell, and developed difficulty breathing [22]. The authors noted that the data did not prove a definite link with pandemic cleaning but showed a clear temporal association with increased product use [22]. The facts are from the CDC's published report and concern the public, not TTI. The lesson for professionals is that more cleaning, done urgently and without label discipline, produces more injuries; written routines and a no-mixing rule are basic protections.
:::

:::example Brightline's chemical list (illustrative)
Keisha starts with nine products she has collected over the years. She reduces the list to five: a neutral all-purpose cleaner, a glass cleaner, a bathroom cleaner, a registered disinfectant for high-touch surfaces when customers request it, and a neutral floor cleaner. The disinfectant label lists a 4-minute contact time, so her bathroom checklist puts disinfectant on the toilet and faucet handles first and wipes them last, about 10 minutes later. Five products at an average of $9 per refill bottle, replaced about every 25 visits, cost 5 × $9 ÷ 25 = $1.80 per visit, well within her $6 supply estimate. She keeps SDS sheets in a folder in the car and as PDFs on her phone, and trains her first hire for one hour on the program before any visit.
:::

# Chapter 7: Systems and Quality Control

:::objectives
- Explain why written systems produce consistent quality.
- Build a room-by-room checklist with a set work order.
- Design a quality control inspection and feedback loop.
- Use outside standards as a reference point.
:::

A system is a documented way of doing work that produces the same result regardless of who does it. In cleaning, systems take three main forms: checklists that say what to clean, sequences that say in what order, and inspections that confirm the result. Systems make training faster, make quality predictable, and let you measure time accurately.

## Checklists and sequences

A good checklist is specific, observable, and ordered. Professional cleaners commonly work top to bottom (so dust falls onto areas not yet cleaned) and dry to wet (dusting before mopping). They also work in a consistent direction around each room so nothing is missed. Assign a standard time to each room based on your measurements, so you can see where a visit ran long.

## Quality control

Quality control (QC) is checking work against the standard. Methods include self-inspection by the cleaner at the end of each room, spot inspections by the owner or lead on a sample of visits, photographs of key areas when appropriate and permitted, and a follow-up message to the customer within 24 hours. Record any issue, its cause, and the fix. Patterns, not individual complaints, show where your training or checklist needs to change.

## Outside standards

Formal standards can guide your own. Green Seal's GS-42 standard for commercial and institutional cleaning services sets requirements for cleaning service providers, including building-specific operating procedures, sustainable purchasing, and employee training [27]. A small residential company will not usually seek certification, but reading such a standard shows what a mature quality system includes.

| QC method | When | What it catches | Record kept |
|---|---|---|---|
| Room self-check | End of each room | Missed tasks | Checklist tick |
| Owner spot inspection | 1 in 10 visits | Training gaps, drift | Inspection score |
| Customer follow-up | Within 24 hours | Expectation gaps | Response log |
| Re-clean | Within 48 hours of a valid complaint | Service failure | Issue log with cause |
| Monthly review | Once a month | Patterns | Summary and checklist changes |

## A framework for building systems

1. Write a checklist for each room in your service, in the order you clean it.
2. Time each room over at least five visits and set a standard time.
3. Train new workers by doing, then watching, then checking.
4. Inspect a sample of visits using the same checklist and a simple score.
5. Follow up with each customer after the first three visits and periodically after.
6. Offer a re-clean for valid complaints within a set window.
7. Review issues monthly and change the checklist or training, not just the individual.

:::case The Maids: time and motion study and team cleaning (real, cited)
According to the company's published history, Daniel J. Bishop, who had run a janitorial company before age 21, founded The Maids in Omaha, Nebraska, in 1979 [25]. Before starting, he researched the home cleaning process; through time and motion studies, he concluded that team cleaning was the most efficient approach, and trained workers in a system built on four functions: dusting, bathroom cleaning, kitchen cleaning, and vacuuming [25]. The company began franchising in 1980 [26]. In 1995 it adopted environmentally preferable products and a backpack vacuum and developed a 22-step cleaning process [25]. Bishop was quoted in 2019 as saying the company brought "a system to an industry many didn't give a second look" [26]. The facts come from the company's own published materials, and the outcomes are the company's, not TTI's. The lesson for a small operator is that measurement comes before method: time the work, find the most efficient division of tasks, and write it down so others can repeat it.
:::

:::example Brightline's room standards (illustrative)
Keisha times her standard clean over five visits and sets standard times: kitchen 45 minutes, two bathrooms 30 minutes each, three bedrooms 15 minutes each, living areas 30 minutes, and floors and final walk-through 15 minutes. The total is 45 + 60 + 45 + 30 + 15 = 195 minutes, or 3.25 hours for one cleaner. With a two-person team splitting tasks, the visit takes about 1.75 hours on site, or 3.5 labor hours, slightly more than one cleaner because of coordination. She decides solo cleaners will handle standard visits and teams will handle initial and move-out cleans, where faster completion matters more. Her first ten inspections score an average of 92 out of 100; the most common miss is baseboards behind doors, so she adds "behind doors" to the bedroom checklist.
:::

:::

:::summary
Systems turn individual skill into repeatable results. Write ordered room checklists, set standard times, train by demonstration, inspect a sample of work, follow up with customers, and fix patterns through checklist and training changes. # Chapter 8: Routes, Schedules, and Pay

:::objectives
- Cluster customers geographically to reduce drive time.
- Build schedules with realistic buffers.
- Apply federal rules on paid travel time between job sites.
- Track hours accurately for overtime purposes.
:::

In a mobile service business, the road is a cost center. Every minute between homes is paid time (for employees) and vehicle expense that no customer pays for directly. Route density, the number of customers within a small area, is one of the strongest levers on cleaning profitability.

## Route density and scheduling

Cluster customers by neighborhood or ZIP code and assign each cluster to particular days. When a new inquiry comes from outside your service area, either decline, charge a travel fee, or wait until you have enough customers there to justify a new route. Schedule with buffers: an arrival window (for example, 9:00 to 9:30) rather than an exact time, and fifteen minutes between jobs for traffic, parking, and unloading. Leave one open slot per week for re-cleans and rescheduling.

## Paid travel time

Federal wage law affects how you pay employees for time on the road. The U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) explains that ordinary home-to-work travel is generally not work time, but that time spent traveling as part of an employee's principal activity, "such as travel from job site to job site during the workday," is work time and must be counted as hours worked [28]. For a cleaning business, that means the drive from the first home to the second, and from the second to the third, is paid time. It also counts toward overtime.

## Overtime and recordkeeping

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), covered nonexempt employees must receive overtime pay for hours worked over 40 in a workweek. The workweek is the unit: you cannot average hours across two weeks to avoid overtime. Accurate records of hours worked each day and each week are required. Some states have additional rules, including daily overtime, so check your state labor department as well.

| Time category | Paid under federal rules? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cleaning at a customer's home | Yes | Principal activity |
| Driving between job sites during the day | Yes | Counts toward overtime |
| Ordinary commute from home to first job | Generally no | State rules and special circumstances can differ |
| Picking up supplies at the office before the first job | Usually yes | Workday may begin at the office |
| Waiting at a home when locked out, if required to wait | Often yes | "Engaged to wait" can be work time |

## A framework for routes and pay

1. Map all customers and group them into clusters by area.
2. Assign each cluster to fixed days.
3. Set arrival windows and fifteen-minute buffers.
4. Track start, end, and travel times daily for every employee.
5. Total hours by workweek and pay overtime for hours over 40.
6. Review route density monthly and adjust service-area boundaries.

:::case Shoreline Building Services: travel time and overtime records (real, cited)
In December 2020, the DOL announced that a federal court consent judgment required Shoreline Building Services LLC, a janitorial company in St. Joseph, Michigan, and its owner to pay 11 employees $10,000 in back wages and liquidated damages [29]. The Wage and Hour Division found that the company paid overtime based on a two-week or longer pay period rather than for hours over 40 in a workweek, calculated overtime incorrectly for employees who worked in multiple positions at different rates, and failed to record or pay for time employees spent traveling between work sites during the workday [29]. It also cited recordkeeping violations for failing to keep accurate daily and weekly hours [29]. The facts are from the DOL announcement and concern those parties, not TTI. For a cleaning business, the case is a checklist of common errors: averaging across pay periods, ignoring blended rates, and leaving travel between sites off the timesheet.
:::

:::example Pay for a three-home day (illustrative)
Brightline's first employee, Marcus, earns $20 per hour. On Tuesday he cleans three homes for 3.0, 3.25, and 2.75 hours, a total of 9.0 cleaning hours, and drives 20 and 25 minutes between them, or 0.75 hours. His paid time for the day is 9.0 + 0.75 = 9.75 hours. Over the week he works four such days, for 4 × 9.75 = 39.0 hours, plus a 2.5-hour Friday re-clean with 0.5 hours of travel between sites, for a total of 42.0 hours. He is owed 40 × $20 = $800 in straight time and 2 × $20 × 1.5 = $60 in overtime, or $860. If Keisha had omitted the travel between sites (3.0 + 0.5 = 3.5 hours), she would have recorded 38.5 hours and underpaid him both straight time and overtime.

# Chapter 9: Hiring and Classification

:::objectives
- Distinguish employees from independent contractors.
- Understand the risks of misclassification.
- Build a lawful, practical hiring process.
- Keep payroll and pay records accurate.
:::

The first hire changes a cleaning business more than any other step. It frees the owner's time, but it adds payroll obligations, training responsibility, and legal exposure. The first decision is classification: is this person an employee or an independent contractor?

## Employee or contractor

The IRS explains that the answer depends on the degree of control and independence in the relationship, and groups the evidence into three categories: behavioral control (whether the business controls or has the right to control what the worker does and how), financial control (how the worker is paid, whether expenses are reimbursed, who provides tools and supplies), and the type of relationship (written contracts, benefits, permanence, and whether the work is a key aspect of the business) [30]. The IRS advises looking at the entire relationship and documenting the factors used [30]. State laws may use different and sometimes stricter tests.

In a typical cleaning company, the owner sets the schedule, assigns the homes, provides the checklist, supplies the chemicals, sets the price, and requires the company's way of working. Those facts point toward employment. Labeling a worker a contractor in an agreement does not change the underlying relationship.

## Hiring process

A practical process includes a clear job description with pay, hours, and physical requirements; a short phone screen; a working interview or paid trial shift; reference checks; any background checks you choose to run, conducted in compliance with applicable law and with the candidate's consent; completion of required employment forms; and documented training before solo work.

| Step | Purpose | Record kept |
|---|---|---|
| Job description | Set expectations | Posted version |
| Phone screen | Check availability and transport | Notes |
| Paid trial shift | Observe work and attitude | Trial checklist score |
| References | Confirm reliability | Notes |
| Onboarding forms | Payroll and eligibility | Employee file |
| Training | Safety and checklists | Training log with dates |

## A framework for the first hire

1. Calculate whether current demand supports a part-time hire (see example).
2. Decide classification using IRS and state guidance, with professional advice.
3. Set up payroll, tax withholding, and workers' compensation before the first shift.
4. Write the job description and screen candidates.
5. Run a paid trial shift with the standard checklist.
6. Train on safety, checklists, customer conduct, and key security.
7. Shadow, then solo with inspection, then routine inspection.
8. Keep accurate time and pay records from day one.

:::case Handy and Hawaii: two classification and overtime cases (real, cited)
In May 2023, the San Francisco District Attorney announced that Handy Technologies, a platform that dispatches workers for cleaning and handyman services, agreed to pay $6 million and enter a permanent injunction to settle a lawsuit brought with the Los Angeles District Attorney alleging it misclassified workers as independent contractors under California law [31]. The judgment required $4.8 million in restitution covering more than 25,000 California workers and a $1.2 million civil penalty, and Handy changed its practices so workers could set their own rates and negotiate terms without penalty [31]. Separately, in May 2025 the DOL announced that a consent judgment required a Hawaii staffing agency and a Lihue cleaning contractor, Hawaii Care and Cleaning, along with their owners, to pay $1.9 million in back wages and an equal amount in damages to 1,133 employees, plus $50,000 in civil penalties; investigators found the employers had frequently falsified pay records to hide overtime violations [32]. The facts are from the government announcements and concern those parties, not TTI. Together they show that classification and timekeeping errors can grow into large liabilities as a business scales.
:::

:::example Is Brightline ready to hire? (illustrative)
Keisha has 22 recurring customers, 18 biweekly and 4 weekly. Biweekly customers average 18 ÷ 2 = 9 visits per week, and weekly customers add 4, for 13 visits per week. Working alone, she can complete about 10 visits per week while still handling sales and administration, so 3 visits per week, about 9 labor hours plus travel, are unserved, and she has a waiting list of 5 households. A part-time employee working 20 hours per week at $20 per hour costs 20 × $23 = $460 per week burdened. Those 20 hours could cover about 5 visits plus travel, or 5 × $165 = $825 in weekly revenue. After labor, vehicle, and supplies of roughly 5 × ($9.12 + $6.00) = $75.60, the remaining $825 − $460 − $75.60 = $289.40 contributes to overhead and profit. Keisha decides the hire is justified and sets up payroll and workers' compensation before the first shift.
:::

# Chapter 10: Marketing and Reviews

:::objectives
- Choose a small set of marketing channels for your lane.
- Write marketing claims that are truthful and supported.
- Collect and display reviews in compliance with FTC rules.
- Track the cost of acquiring a customer.
:::

Marketing for a cleaning business is mostly local and mostly about trust. Customers want to know that you will show up, do what you promised, and treat their home with respect. The most effective channels are usually those that carry the voice of other customers: referrals, reviews, and neighborhood networks.

## Channels

Common channels include a business listing on major maps and search platforms, a simple website with service area, scope, and a quote form, referrals from existing customers, neighborhood groups and community boards, partnerships with real estate agents and property managers for move-out work, and door hangers in clusters where you already clean. Choose two or three channels that fit your lane and track results for each.

## Truthful claims

The FTC's advertising guidance explains that claims must be truthful, not misleading, and supported by evidence [18]. Words such as "insured," "bonded," "background-checked," "eco-friendly," or "guaranteed" each carry meaning. Use them only if they are true and you can explain exactly what they mean. If you say "green," say which products you use and why.

## Reviews

In August 2024, the FTC announced a final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials. The rule prohibits creating, selling, or buying reviews that misrepresent who wrote them or their experience, including AI-generated fake reviews; prohibits offering compensation or incentives conditioned on reviews expressing a particular sentiment; and restricts reviews by company insiders that do not clearly disclose their connection [33]. In practice, ask every customer for an honest review, never only the happy ones; never offer a discount for a five-star rating; never post reviews written by employees or family without disclosure; and never hide negative reviews.

| Channel | Best for | Cost type | Metric to track |
|---|---|---|---|
| Map and search listing | Recurring residential | Time | Calls and quote requests |
| Customer referrals | All lanes | Referral credit | New customers per month |
| Agent and manager partnerships | Move-out | Time | Jobs per partner |
| Door hangers in clusters | Route density | Printing | Inquiries per 100 hangers |
| Neighborhood groups | Recurring residential | Time | Inquiries |

## A framework for marketing

1. Pick two or three channels that fit your lane.
2. Write a short message stating service, area, and what makes you reliable.
3. Check every claim for truth and support.
4. Set up an honest review request sent after the second or third visit to all customers.
5. Record the source of every inquiry.
6. Calculate customer acquisition cost for each channel monthly.
7. Drop channels that do not produce customers after a fair test.

:::case Fashion Nova and suppressed reviews (real, cited)
In 2022 the FTC finalized an order against online retailer Fashion Nova, which agreed to pay $4.2 million to settle allegations that it misrepresented that reviews on its website reflected the views of all purchasers who submitted reviews, when it in fact suppressed reviews rated lower than four stars out of five [34]. The order prohibits the company from suppressing customer reviews and from misrepresenting reviews or endorsements [34]. Fashion Nova is a clothing retailer, not a cleaning company, but the principle applies to any business that displays reviews: showing only positive reviews while blocking negative ones misleads customers. The facts are from the FTC announcement and concern those parties, not TTI. For a cleaning business, the lesson is to request reviews from every customer, display them honestly, and respond to criticism rather than hiding it.
:::

:::example Customer acquisition cost (illustrative)
In March, Keisha spends $180 printing 600 door hangers placed in two neighborhoods where she already cleans, and offers existing customers a $25 credit for each referral who books. The door hangers produce 9 inquiries and 3 new recurring customers, a cost of $180 ÷ 3 = $60 per customer. Referrals produce 4 new customers at $25 each, or $100, which is $25 per customer. A biweekly customer at $165 per visit who stays for a year generates about 26 visits, or 26 × $165 = $4,290 in revenue. At a 31.3 percent margin, that is roughly $1,343 in profit before marketing, so both channels are affordable, and referrals are the more efficient.

The FTC's 2024 rule bans fake reviews, sentiment-conditioned incentives, and undisclosed insider reviews. The Fashion Nova order shows that hiding negative reviews is itself deceptive.

# Chapter 11: Retention and Growth

:::objectives
- Measure customer retention and its value.
- Convert one-time jobs to recurring service.
- Protect customer keys, codes, and personal information.
- Decide when to delegate your own tasks.
:::

A recurring cleaning business grows in two ways: by adding customers and by keeping them. Keeping them is usually cheaper. Each customer who stays a year generates many visits from a single acquisition cost, and dense routes of long-term customers are the most profitable routes you will run.

## Retention

Retention rate is the percentage of customers at the start of a period who are still customers at the end. Track it monthly and record the reason each customer leaves. Common reasons include moving, budget changes, a quality failure, a change in cleaner, and scheduling conflicts. Some are beyond your control; others point directly to your systems. Assigning the same cleaner to a home when possible, communicating schedule changes early, and resolving complaints quickly all improve retention.

## Converting one-time jobs

Move-out and deep cleaning customers can become recurring customers if you ask. After a one-time job, follow up within 24 hours, confirm satisfaction, and offer a recurring schedule with the price and scope stated clearly. Do not make the offer a hidden condition of the one-time job.

## Protecting keys and information

Customers trust cleaners with keys, garage codes, alarm codes, and sometimes Wi-Fi passwords and personal schedules. The FTC's guide to protecting personal information advises businesses to know what personal information they have, keep only what they need, protect it, properly dispose of what they no longer need, and plan for responding to incidents [37]. For cleaning, that means coding keys with numbers rather than addresses, storing codes in a password-protected system rather than on paper, limiting who can see them, changing access when an employee leaves, and returning or destroying keys when service ends.

| Measure | Formula | Brightline example |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly retention | Customers retained ÷ customers at start | 21 ÷ 22 = 95.5% |
| Annual visits per biweekly customer | 26 visits | 26 |
| Annual revenue per biweekly customer | Visits × price | 26 × $165 = $4,290 |
| Conversion rate, one-time to recurring | Converted ÷ one-time jobs | 3 ÷ 10 = 30% |

## Delegating your own role

As the business grows, the owner's time becomes the constraint. Dan Martell, a software entrepreneur and author of Buy Back Your Time, describes a "buyback rate": take your annual income, divide it by 2,000 hours to get an effective hourly rate, then divide by four, and consider paying others to do tasks that cost less than that amount so you can spend your time on higher-value work [35]. In a 2023 interview he explained that someone earning $100,000 a year would have a buyback rate of $12.50 per hour [35].

## A framework for retention and growth

1. Track retention monthly and record reasons for every cancellation.
2. Assign consistent cleaners to homes where possible.
3. Follow up on every one-time job and offer recurring service clearly.
4. Secure keys and codes and limit access.
5. Calculate your buyback rate and list tasks below it.
6. Delegate one task at a time, with a written procedure.
7. Review route density, pricing, and margin every quarter before adding a new lane.

:::case Dan Martell and buying back time (real, cited)
Dan Martell is a software entrepreneur and author. In an interview with business publication TheStreet, he described his path from addiction treatment, where he learned to code, to building software companies, and said that it took seven years before he found success [36]. In a 2023 Entrepreneur interview, he explained his buyback rate: annual income divided by 2,000 hours, then divided by four, so that delegated tasks return roughly four times their cost in freed time [35]. He framed hiring as buying back time from your calendar rather than simply adding capacity [35]. The facts are from published interviews, and his experience is his own, not TTI's. For a cleaning business owner, the framework helps decide when to hire an office assistant, a bookkeeper, or a lead cleaner, rather than continuing to do every task personally.
:::

:::example Keisha's buyback rate (illustrative)
After eighteen months, Keisha pays herself $52,000 a year. Her effective hourly rate is $52,000 ÷ 2,000 = $26, and her buyback rate is $26 ÷ 4 = $6.50 per hour. That is below the wage she would need to pay anyone in her market, so she uses the idea differently: she lists the tasks she does each week and their hours. Scheduling and customer messages take 6 hours per week; bookkeeping takes 3 hours; cleaning takes 20 hours; sales calls and walkthroughs take 5 hours. She decides to hire a part-time scheduler at $18 per hour for 6 hours ($108 per week) and moves those 6 hours into sales calls. If those calls bring in two new biweekly customers per month, each worth about $4,290 per year, the added revenue far exceeds the scheduler's cost of about $108 × 52 = $5,616 per year. She also notes that this is an estimate, not a promise, and sets a ninety-day review.
:::

:::checklist Retention and security
- Monthly retention rate tracked with reasons.
- Consistent cleaner assignments where possible.
- Follow-up within 24 hours of every one-time job.
- Keys coded by number; codes stored securely.
- Access changed when employees leave.
- Task list with hours reviewed quarterly.
- One task delegated with a written procedure.
:::

:::summary
Retention multiplies the value of each customer, and dense routes of long-term customers are the most profitable. Track retention and reasons, convert one-time jobs with clear offers, and protect keys and personal information as FTC guidance advises. When your time becomes the constraint, list your tasks and delegate those that others can do, using frameworks such as Dan Martell's buyback rate as a guide rather than a rule.
:::

# Glossary

Add-on: A task available for an additional, stated price beyond the standard scope.

Arrival window: A time range, rather than an exact time, during which the cleaner will arrive.

Burdened labor rate: Hourly wage plus employer payroll taxes, workers' compensation, and benefits.

Contact time: The length of time a disinfectant must remain wet on a surface to work as the label claims; also called dwell time.

Cost-plus contract: A contract in which the customer reimburses agreed costs plus a profit percentage.

Customer acquisition cost: Marketing spending divided by the number of new customers it produces.

Disinfecting: Using a registered product as directed to kill the germs listed on its label.

Exclusion: A task specifically not included in the quoted price.

Fixed-price contract: A contract in which the price is set in advance regardless of actual time or cost.

Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD): The disclosure document a franchisor must provide under the FTC's Franchise Rule at least 14 days before signing or payment.

General liability insurance: Coverage for third-party injury and property damage claims.

Hazard communication program: A written program covering chemical lists, labels, safety data sheets, and training.

Independent contractor: A worker who controls how the work is done and operates an independent business, as determined by the full relationship.

Janitorial bond: A bond that may pay customers for losses from employee theft, as defined in the bond.

Labor burden: Employment costs added to wages, such as payroll taxes and workers' compensation.

Overhead: Business costs not tied to a single job, such as insurance, software, and marketing.

Retention rate: The percentage of customers at the start of a period who remain at the end.

Route density: The number of customers within a small geographic area served on the same day.

Safety data sheet (SDS): A document describing a chemical's hazards, safe handling, and emergency measures.

Sanitizing: Reducing germs on a surface to levels considered safe.

Scope: The written list of tasks included in a quoted price.

Service lane: One repeatable service sold to one type of customer.

Surety bond: A guarantee, issued by a surety company, that contract terms or legal obligations will be met.

Travel time between sites: Time an employee spends traveling from one job site to another during the workday, which federal rules count as work time.

Walkthrough: An in-person visit to assess a property before quoting.

Workers' compensation: Insurance covering employee work injuries, required by state law for most employers.

Workweek: A fixed, recurring period of 168 hours (seven consecutive days) used to calculate overtime.
