# How to Use This Book

A business plan is one of the oldest tools in entrepreneurship and one of the most misunderstood. Some founders treat it as a formality for a bank. Others dismiss it as fiction that no one reads. The research literature, and the experience of companies that have used plans well, suggest a more useful view: a business plan is a disciplined way of thinking, a set of testable assumptions, and a communication device for the people whose help you need.

This book teaches you to write that kind of plan. It is designed for first-time founders, owners of very small businesses, side-business builders, and people preparing plans for lenders, partners, landlords, grant programs, or themselves. It assumes no business degree. It does assume a willingness to be honest about what you know and what you are guessing.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this book you should be able to:

- Explain what research says about the value of business planning, and its limits.
- Choose between a traditional plan, a lean plan, and a one-page plan.
- Describe a business, its customers, and its value proposition in plain language.
- Conduct practical market research and test demand before spending heavily.
- Write a marketing and sales plan tied to real customer behavior.
- Describe operations, staffing, and a startup budget.
- Set prices and build honest monthly financial projections with stated assumptions.
- Identify risks and plan responses.
- Prepare a plan for lenders and investors, including what an SBA lender reviews.
- Write an executive summary that a busy reader can absorb in a minute.
- Keep the plan alive as a management tool after launch.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives and an explanation of the topic, followed by a step-by-step framework, an illustrative worked example, a real case study with sources, at least one table, a worksheet or checklist, a summary, and review questions. Worked examples are hypothetical and their figures are invented for teaching. Case studies describe real companies and research from public sources listed in the References chapter; their results are their own, not TTI's.

:::warning Scope and limits
This book is education only. It is not legal, tax, accounting, securities, lending, or investment advice. Lenders, investors, and grant makers make their own decisions, and no loan, investment, grant, revenue, or profit is promised. Securities laws govern how you may raise money from investors; consult a licensed attorney before offering ownership in your business. Consult a CPA or enrolled agent on taxes and financial statements.
:::

# Chapter 1: Why Write a Plan? What the Evidence Says

:::objectives
- Summarize major research findings on business planning and new-venture outcomes.
- Distinguish the planning process from the planning document.
- Identify the audiences a plan may serve.
- Decide what your plan must accomplish.
:::

## The debate

Few topics in entrepreneurship have produced as much disagreement as business planning. Critics argue that plans consume time founders should spend selling and building, and that forecasts for unproven businesses are guesses dressed as numbers. Supporters argue that planning forces founders to confront unknowns, allocate resources, and communicate clearly. Both camps have a point, and the research helps clarify when planning helps.

## What the research found

In a widely cited study published in the Strategic Management Journal, Frédéric Delmar and Scott Shane followed 223 new ventures started by a random sample of Swedish founders in the first nine months of 1998 [4]. They argued that business planning helps founders make decisions, balance resource supply and demand, and turn abstract goals into concrete operational steps, and they found support for the hypotheses that planning reduces the likelihood of venture disbanding and accelerates product development and organizing activity [4]. In a related study of the same ventures, Shane and Delmar reported that completing a business plan before talking to customers reduced the hazard of termination by 46 percent, and completing one before beginning marketing and promotion reduced it by 41 percent [5].

A later meta-analysis by Jan Brinckmann, Dietmar Grichnik, and Diana Kapsa combined 46 studies covering 11,046 organizations and found that business planning was positively related to performance overall, with a mean effect size of 0.20, but that the relationship was weaker for new firms (0.13) than for established small firms (0.24) [6]. The authors proposed a "concomitant and dynamic approach that combines planning and learning" [6].

## Interpreting the evidence

Three cautions apply. First, these studies show associations; founders who plan may differ in other ways from those who do not. Second, effect sizes of 0.13 to 0.24 are modest; planning is helpful, not magical. Third, the meta-analysis suggests that planning pays off more when a business has information to plan with, which is a strong argument for combining a plan with fast learning from customers, the theme of Chapter 2.

:::case Delmar and Shane's study of Swedish new ventures
Delmar and Shane's research is valuable because it followed ventures over time rather than asking survivors to recall whether they had planned, a method that would bias results toward success [4][5]. Their sample of 223 ventures was drawn from a random sample of Swedish founders and tracked for 30 months [5]. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's, and were obtained in one country during one period. Their central insight, that planning functions as a precursor to action by helping founders decide, sequence, and coordinate, is the view of planning this book adopts.
:::

**Step 6: Schedule a review** with a knowledgeable outsider.

# Chapter 2: Choosing the Right Format

:::objectives
- Compare traditional, lean, and one-page plans.
- Explain the lean startup critique of traditional plans.
- Select a format for your purpose and stage.
- Outline your plan's sections.
:::

## Traditional and lean plans

The U.S. Small Business Administration describes two common categories of business plans. Traditional plans use a standard structure, go into detail in each section, require more work upfront, and can be dozens of pages long; lenders and investors commonly request them [1]. Lean startup plans summarize only the most important points, can take as little as an hour to make, and are typically one page [1]. The SBA emphasizes that there is no right or wrong way to write a plan; what matters is that the plan meets your needs [1].

## The lean startup critique

In a 2013 Harvard Business Review cover article, Steve Blank described the traditional approach, in which founders write a plan with a five-year forecast, raise money, and develop products in isolation, and argued that "business plans rarely survive first contact with customers" [7]. Blank wrote that five-year plans forecasting complete unknowns "are generally fiction" and described an alternative: summarize hypotheses in a business model canvas, test them with customers through "customer development," and iterate quickly with minimum viable products [7].

Blank's critique applies most strongly to new ventures with high uncertainty. For a business with known models, such as a franchise, an established trade, or a business acquisition, a detailed plan is often appropriate because there is real information to plan with. The two approaches are complementary: start lean, test quickly, and write the detailed plan when you have facts.

| Format | Length | Best for | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| One-page plan | 1 page | Clarifying thinking; early tests | Oversimplifying |
| Lean plan or business model canvas | 1–3 pages | High-uncertainty ventures | Under-documenting for lenders |
| Traditional plan | 15–40 pages | Lenders, investors, partners | Spending months on guesses |
| Operating plan | Ongoing | Running an existing business | Neglect after writing |

## The traditional plan's sections

The SBA lists common sections for a traditional plan, including an executive summary, company description, market analysis, organization and management, service or product line, marketing and sales, funding request, financial projections, and an appendix [1]. This book follows a similar structure, adding a dedicated chapter on risks.

:::case Steve Blank and the lean startup
Steve Blank, a serial entrepreneur and educator, developed the "customer development" process that became one of the foundations of the lean startup method, and his 2013 HBR article brought the approach to a wide business audience [7]. Blank describes a startup as an organization searching for a repeatable and scalable business model and argues that founders should begin with hypotheses rather than a detailed plan [7]. Blank's results and views are his own, not TTI's. His critique does not reject planning; it rejects planning in isolation from customers. This book adopts that lesson by pairing every planning chapter with ways to test assumptions.
:::

## The format framework

**Step 1: Assess uncertainty**: how much do you actually know about customers, prices, and costs?

**Step 2: Identify the audience's requirements**: a lender may require a traditional plan.

**Step 3: Start with a one-page plan** regardless of the final format.

**Step 4: List your riskiest assumptions** and plan tests for them.

**Step 5: Expand** into a traditional plan when the audience requires it or the facts justify it.

**Step 6: Keep both** a short version and a long version current.

:::example Illustrative: choosing a format
A hypothetical mobile dog-grooming founder has no customers yet. He writes a one-page plan, lists three risky assumptions (demand at $75 per groom, ability to book four grooms a day, van costs), and tests them over six weeks with a borrowed van. He books 52 grooms, averaging 3.1 per day. With this information he writes a traditional plan for a van loan. Figures are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 3: Describing the Business and Its Strategy

:::objectives
- Write a clear company description and value proposition.
- Describe strategy in a few memorable steps.
- Choose and explain a legal structure at a high level.
- State a mission and milestones.
:::

## Plain words first

A company description answers simple questions: what you sell, to whom, why they need it, and why you are the right one to provide it. The SBA advises being specific about the problems the business solves and the customers it plans to serve, and explaining the competitive advantages that will make the business succeed [1]. Jargon is a warning sign; if you cannot explain your business to a twelve-year-old, you probably do not yet understand it well enough.

## The value proposition

A value proposition states the specific benefit a defined customer receives and why your offer is better than alternatives. A useful template is: "For [customer] who [need], [business] provides [solution] that [benefit], unlike [alternative], which [limitation]."

## Strategy as a sequence

A strategy describes how you will win over time. Many small businesses benefit from describing strategy as a short sequence of steps, each of which funds or enables the next.

:::case Tesla's 2006 "Secret Master Plan"
In August 2006, Elon Musk published "The Secret Tesla Motors Master Plan (just between you and me)" on Tesla's website [9]. Musk explained that Tesla's strategy was "to enter at the high end of the market, where customers are prepared to pay a premium, and then drive down market as fast as possible to higher unit volume and lower prices with each successive model," and that buyers of the Roadster sports car were "helping pay for development of the low cost family car" [9]. He summarized the plan in four steps: build a sports car; use that money to build an affordable car; use that money to build an even more affordable car; and, while doing so, provide zero-emission electric power generation options [9]. Tesla's results are its own, not TTI's, and Tesla's path involved enormous capital and risk. The document is useful for small-business planners as a model of clarity: a strategy explained in a few sentences, with a logic in which each step funds the next.
:::

## Legal structure

Plans typically state the business's legal structure, such as sole proprietorship, partnership, limited liability company, or corporation. The choice affects liability, taxes, and fundraising, and should be made with a qualified attorney and tax professional. The plan should simply state the structure and ownership.

| Element | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Description | What do you do? | "We install and service residential solar water heaters in Tucson." |
| Customer | Who buys? | "Homeowners aged 35–70 with high water-heating costs." |
| Value proposition | Why you? | "Installation in one day, with a 10-year labor warranty." |
| Strategy | How will you win over time? | "Start with installs; build a service base; add commercial." |
| Structure | Who owns it? | "Single-member LLC owned by the founder." |
| Milestones | What happens when? | "First 20 installs by June; first hire by September." |

## The business-description framework

**Step 1: Write one sentence** describing what you do and for whom.

**Step 2: Complete the value proposition** template.

**Step 3: Describe your strategy** as three to five steps.

**Step 4: State the legal structure** and ownership.

**Step 5: List milestones** for the next 12 months.

**Step 6: Test the description** on three people outside your industry.

:::example Illustrative: sharpening a description
A hypothetical founder's first description reads: "We leverage innovative solutions to optimize home comfort." After testing it on three friends, she rewrites it: "We install and service ductless heating and cooling systems for older homes in Portland that lack ductwork." Her strategy: (1) install for homeowners; (2) build a maintenance-plan base; (3) use maintenance revenue to fund a second crew. The example is illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 4: Market Research and Testing Demand

:::objectives
- Use secondary sources to estimate market size and conditions.
- Conduct primary research through interviews and surveys.
- Test demand with low-cost experiments before investing heavily.
- Analyze competitors honestly.
:::

## Secondary and primary research

Market research combines existing data with direct evidence from customers. The SBA suggests answering questions about demand, market size, economic indicators, location, market saturation, and pricing, and notes that existing sources save time but may be less specific, while direct research such as surveys, questionnaires, focus groups, and in-depth interviews gives a more nuanced understanding but can be time-consuming and expensive [1]. Free federal sources include the Census Bureau, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and the Bureau of Economic Analysis [1].

## Estimating market size

Market size is often described in three layers: the total market, the portion you could serve given your location and offering, and the share you can realistically obtain in the near term. For small local businesses, a bottom-up estimate is usually more credible than a top-down one. Instead of "the U.S. pet industry is worth billions," estimate "there are about 18,000 dog-owning households within a 15-minute drive; if 1 percent book four grooms a year, that is 720 grooms."

## Customer interviews

Interviews are the most underused research tool. Good interviews ask about past behavior rather than hypothetical intentions. "Would you buy this?" invites polite yeses. "Tell me about the last time you had this problem. What did you do? What did it cost?" reveals what people actually do.

## Testing demand

The strongest evidence of demand is a commitment: a preorder, a deposit, a signed letter of intent, or at minimum a sign-up from a real prospect. Low-cost tests include landing pages, presales, pop-up events, pilot projects, and demonstration videos.

:::case Dropbox's demonstration video
TechCrunch, publishing an excerpt from Eric Ries's book The Lean Startup, described how Dropbox founder Drew Houston, facing the difficulty of building file-synchronization technology before knowing whether people wanted it, made a simple three-minute video demonstrating how the product was meant to work, aimed at a community of technology early adopters [8]. Houston recounted that the video "drove hundreds of thousands of people to the website" and that the beta waiting list "went from 5,000 people to 75,000 people literally overnight" [8]. Ries described the video as the minimum viable product: it validated the assumption that customers wanted the product because they actually signed up [8]. Dropbox's results are its own, not TTI's, and a viral response is unusual. The transferable lesson is that a low-cost test can generate behavioral evidence of demand before major investment.
:::

## Competitive analysis

The SBA recommends identifying competitors by product line or service and market segment and assessing market share, strengths and weaknesses, your window of opportunity, the importance of your target market to competitors, barriers to entry, and indirect competitors [1]. Honest competitive analysis acknowledges where competitors are better.

| Competitor | Offering | Price | Strengths | Weaknesses | Our response |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |
| Do-nothing alternative | Customer handles it themselves | $0 | Free | Time, quality | Show time savings |

Always include the "do nothing" alternative; for many small businesses, inertia is the largest competitor.

## The market-research framework

**Step 1: Gather secondary data** on your market from free public sources.

**Step 2: Build a bottom-up market estimate.**

**Step 3: Interview at least 15 potential customers** about past behavior.

**Step 4: Run one demand test** that asks for a commitment.

**Step 5: Complete a competitor table**, including the do-nothing option.

**Step 6: Write the market section** with sources and test results.

:::example Illustrative: a demand test
A hypothetical meal-prep founder creates a simple web page offering weekly meal kits for $89, shares it in three neighborhood groups, and asks for $20 deposits for the first delivery. Over two weeks, 410 people visit, 61 join the waitlist, and 23 pay deposits. She uses the 5.6 percent deposit rate from visitors and the cost of reaching them to build her first marketing assumption. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Market research
- Secondary sources cited.
- Bottom-up market estimate completed.
- Fifteen or more customer interviews conducted.
- At least one demand test requiring commitment.
- Competitor table including do-nothing alternative.
- Findings written with sources and dates.
:::

:::summary
Market research combines secondary data with direct customer evidence. Bottom-up estimates and interviews about past behavior are more credible than top-down figures and hypothetical questions. Demand tests that ask for commitment, as Dropbox's video did with sign-ups, provide stronger evidence. Competitive analysis should be honest and include the do-nothing alternative.
:::

# Chapter 5: The Marketing and Sales Plan

:::objectives
- Define the customer journey from awareness to repeat purchase.
- Choose marketing channels based on where customers decide.
- Set measurable marketing assumptions.
- Make honest, substantiated advertising claims.
:::

## From awareness to repeat purchase

A marketing and sales plan explains how customers will learn about you, decide to buy, purchase, and return. A simple customer journey has five stages: awareness, consideration, purchase, experience, and loyalty or referral. The plan should name the actions and channels for each stage.

## Choosing channels

The best channels are those where your customers already look for solutions. A plumber's customers search online and ask neighbors; a wedding photographer's customers browse portfolios and ask venues; a business-to-business consultant's customers rely on referrals and professional networks. A plan should choose a small number of channels and explain why.

| Stage | Customer question | Example actions |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | "Who can help with this?" | Search listing, referrals, local events |
| Consideration | "Are they right for me?" | Reviews, portfolio, clear pricing, FAQ |
| Purchase | "Is it easy and safe to buy?" | Simple booking, guarantees, clear terms |
| Experience | "Did they deliver?" | Quality service, communication |
| Loyalty | "Will I come back or refer?" | Follow-up, maintenance plans, referral requests |

## Reducing the buyer's risk

Many purchases stall because buyers fear making a mistake. A marketing plan should identify the main risk a buyer perceives and describe how the business will reduce it, through trials, demonstrations, guarantees, transparent pricing, or social proof that complies with FTC rules.

:::case Warby Parker's home try-on program
Inc. reported that the founders of Warby Parker, four Wharton MBA students who began exploring the idea in 2008, used feedback from potential customers to develop a program allowing customers to try on five pairs of frames at home for free before buying, and that this was "a hit with virtually everyone"; co-founder Dave Gilboa said, "That's when we felt really confident that this idea was going to work" [12]. Wharton Magazine reported that, after launching in February 2010, the company had a waitlist of 20,000 people within four weeks [11]. Warby Parker's results are its own, not TTI's, and the launch also benefited from national press coverage. The case shows how a marketing plan can address the specific risk that stops buyers, in this case buying glasses without trying them on.
:::

## Honest claims

Marketing claims in a plan and in advertising must be truthful. The FTC's advertising guidance for small businesses explains that ads must be truthful, not misleading, and supported by a reasonable basis before they run [15]. Avoid claims such as "guaranteed results" or "best in town" unless you can substantiate them.

## The marketing framework

**Step 1: Map the customer journey** for your business.

**Step 2: Identify the buyer's main perceived risk** and how you will reduce it.

**Step 3: Choose two or three channels** and explain why.

**Step 4: Set measurable assumptions**: reach, conversion rate, cost per customer.

**Step 5: Plan a sales process** with clear steps and follow-up.

**Step 6: Review claims** for truthfulness and substantiation.

:::example Illustrative: marketing assumptions
A hypothetical bookkeeping firm plans to reach small businesses through two channels: referrals from three local accountants and a monthly workshop at the library. It assumes each workshop draws 12 attendees, 25 percent book a consultation, and 40 percent of consultations become clients, yielding about 1.2 clients per workshop. After four workshops, actual results are 9 attendees on average, 33 percent booking, and 50 percent converting, about 1.5 clients per workshop. Figures are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 6: Operations, People, and the Startup Budget

:::objectives
- Describe how the work gets done, step by step.
- Plan staffing, suppliers, and facilities.
- Build a startup budget.
- Anticipate operational bottlenecks before launch.
:::

## The operations section

The operations section describes how the business delivers what it sells: location and facilities, equipment, suppliers, processes, technology, staffing, quality control, and regulatory requirements such as licenses and permits. Lenders read this section to judge whether the founder understands the work; partners read it to understand their roles.

## Mapping the process

A simple process map lists each step from order to delivery. For each step, note who does it, how long it takes, what it costs, and what could go wrong. Bottlenecks usually appear at hand-offs and at steps that only the founder can perform.

## People and management

The SBA's guidance on traditional plans includes an organization and management section describing the company's structure and the people running it [1]. For small businesses, this section should be honest about gaps: if no one on the team has bookkeeping experience, say how that function will be covered.

## The startup budget

A startup budget lists one-time costs to open and the operating cash needed until revenue covers expenses. One-time costs may include equipment, deposits, licenses, initial inventory, website, signage, and professional fees. Operating reserves cover rent, payroll, utilities, insurance, marketing, and the owner's minimum living costs during ramp-up.

| Category | Item | Amount | Source of estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| One-time | Equipment | ___ | Vendor quote |
| One-time | Deposits and licenses | ___ | Landlord; city |
| One-time | Initial inventory | ___ | Supplier quote |
| One-time | Website and signage | ___ | Quotes |
| One-time | Legal and accounting | ___ | Professional quotes |
| Reserve | Operating expenses for ramp-up months | ___ | Monthly budget × months |
| Reserve | Contingency (10–20%) | ___ | Percentage of total |

:::case Warby Parker's launch operations
In an interview with the Wharton Club of New York, Warby Parker co-founder Neil Blumenthal explained that the founders invested their savings first in getting a website built, because none of them were technical, and second in an initial set of inventory, which they had to pay for before knowing they could sell it [13]. He recalled that in the first 24 hours after launch, orders surpassed inventory; the website had no sold-out function, so the founders held an emergency meeting about whether to take the site down, received 12 more orders during that 10-minute meeting, and had their developer add a waitlist [13]. Inc. reported that within 48 hours of press coverage the company temporarily suspended its home try-on program because of the volume of orders [12]. Warby Parker's results are its own, not TTI's. For planners, the case shows that operations sections should anticipate success scenarios, not only failure scenarios, including inventory limits and customer communication.
:::

## The operations framework

**Step 1: Map the process** from order to delivery.

**Step 2: Identify bottlenecks** and single points of failure.

**Step 3: List suppliers**, with a backup for each critical one.

**Step 4: Describe the team** and how gaps will be covered.

**Step 5: Build the startup budget** with sourced estimates.

**Step 6: Add a contingency** and plan for both slow and fast growth.

:::example Illustrative: a startup budget
A hypothetical coffee cart founder estimates one-time costs of $18,400 (cart $11,000, espresso equipment $4,200, permits $900, initial supplies $1,300, branding $1,000) and monthly operating costs of $3,100. Expecting four months before the cart covers its costs, she plans a reserve of $12,400 and a 15 percent contingency of $4,620, for a total need of $35,420. She also identifies a backup bean supplier and a plan for running out of cups on a busy day. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Operations and budget
- Process mapped from order to delivery.
- Bottlenecks and single points of failure identified.
- Backup suppliers listed.
- Team roles and gaps described.
- Licenses and permits identified.
- Startup budget with sourced estimates.
- Contingency included.
- Plan for faster-than-expected demand.
:::

:::summary
The operations section explains how the work gets done, who does it, and what it costs. A startup budget combines one-time costs with operating reserves and a contingency. Warby Parker's launch shows that operations plans should anticipate success as well as failure.

# Chapter 7: Pricing and Honest Financial Projections

:::objectives
- Set prices using costs, customer value, and competitor benchmarks.
- Calculate contribution margin and break-even.
- Build a 12-month cash flow projection with stated assumptions.
- Prepare a projected income statement and understand the balance sheet.
- Account for taxes and the owner's pay.
:::

## Three views of price

Prices can be set from three perspectives. Cost-based pricing starts with what it costs to deliver and adds a margin. Value-based pricing starts with what the result is worth to the customer. Competitor-based pricing benchmarks against alternatives. A sound price is consistent with all three: it covers costs with a healthy margin, is justified by customer value, and makes sense relative to alternatives.

:::case Warby Parker revisited: pricing
Wharton Magazine reported that in Warby Parker's initial business plan the founders wanted to price their glasses at $45 per pair, but advice from Wharton marketing professor Jagmohan Raju changed their minds: as Raju anticipated, their cost of goods doubled, making the $45 price impossible, and market research indicated that $100 was the point at which people thought the product was not too expensive but was still made with quality [11]. Inc. reported that the company launched selling glasses for $95 [12]. Warby Parker's results are its own, not TTI's. The case illustrates two lessons: initial cost estimates are often too low, and the lowest price is not always the best price, because price also signals quality.
:::

## Contribution margin and break-even

Contribution margin is the revenue from a sale minus the variable costs of that sale. It is the amount each sale contributes toward fixed costs and profit.

**Contribution margin per unit = Price − Variable cost per unit**

Break-even is the volume at which total contribution margin equals fixed costs:

**Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin per unit**

For example, a service priced at $150 with variable costs of $45 has a contribution margin of $105. If monthly fixed costs are $6,300, the business must sell 60 services a month to break even.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Price per service | $150 |
| Variable cost per service | $45 |
| Contribution margin | $105 |
| Monthly fixed costs | $6,300 |
| Break-even services per month | 60 |

## Building the 12-month cash flow projection

Cash flow, not profit, determines whether a small business survives its first year. A monthly cash flow projection shows cash in (sales collected, loans, owner investment) and cash out (variable costs, fixed costs, loan payments, owner draws, taxes) and the ending cash balance each month.

Good projections have five properties:

1. **Monthly detail** for the first year.
2. **Stated assumptions** for every major number.
3. **Realistic ramp-up**: sales start slowly.
4. **Timing of collections**: invoices may take 30 to 60 days to collect.
5. **Three scenarios**: conservative, expected, and optimistic.

| Assumption | Conservative | Expected | Optimistic | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customers in month 1 | ___ | ___ | ___ | Demand test |
| Monthly growth | ___ | ___ | ___ | Comparable businesses |
| Average sale | ___ | ___ | ___ | Pricing research |
| Variable cost % | ___ | ___ | ___ | Supplier quotes |
| Collection delay | ___ | ___ | ___ | Industry norms |

## The income statement and balance sheet

A projected income statement (profit and loss) summarizes revenue, costs, and profit for a period. A balance sheet lists assets, liabilities, and owner's equity at a point in time. Lenders often request both. A CPA can help prepare them; your role is to supply honest assumptions and understand what the statements mean.

## Taxes and the owner's pay

Founders often forget two costs: taxes and their own pay. The IRS explains that self-employment tax, which covers Social Security and Medicare for self-employed individuals, is 15.3 percent, consisting of 12.4 percent for Social Security and 2.9 percent for Medicare [16]. The IRS also explains that people who expect to owe tax of $1,000 or more generally must make estimated tax payments [17]. A plan should include an owner's draw sufficient for minimum living costs and set aside cash for taxes.

## The financial-projection framework

**Step 1: Set prices** using cost, value, and competitor perspectives.

**Step 2: Calculate contribution margin** and break-even volume.

**Step 3: List assumptions** with their sources.

**Step 4: Build a monthly cash flow projection** for 12 months, in three scenarios.

**Step 5: Prepare a projected income statement** and, if needed, a balance sheet with professional help.

**Step 6: Include taxes and the owner's pay**, and identify the lowest cash month.

A hypothetical landscaping founder projects revenue starting at $4,000 in March and peaking at $14,000 in June, then falling to $1,500 in December. Fixed costs are $3,800 a month, and he plans an owner's draw of $2,500. His projection shows cash peaking in August and falling below zero in February of the following year. He adjusts the plan to sell prepaid winter snow-removal contracts and to build a reserve during summer. Figures are illustrative.

# Chapter 8: Risks and Contingency Plans

:::objectives
- Interpret business survival data realistically.
- Identify the major categories of business risk.
- Assess risks by likelihood and impact.
- Write contingency plans and early warning signals.
:::

## Survival in context

Most new businesses face real risk of closing. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that 34.7 percent of private-sector establishments born in March 2013 were still operating in March 2023, and that the survival rate fell most in the first year, dropping 20.4 percentage points from 2013 to 2014 [14]. Survival varied by industry; construction showed the sharpest first-year decline, 24.0 percentage points [14]. Closures are not always failures, since owners close businesses for many reasons, including retirement and sale, but the data argue for taking risk seriously.

:::case Ten years of BLS establishment survival data
The BLS Business Employment Dynamics program tracks establishments from birth. For the cohort born in 2013, roughly 80 percent survived one year, about half survived five years, and 34.7 percent survived ten years [14]. Industries differed: ten-year survival was 50.5 percent in agriculture, forestry, fishing, and hunting, 42.2 percent in real estate and rental and leasing, and 34.2 percent in administrative and waste services [14]. These are government statistics, and the BLS's results are its own, not TTI's. They describe establishments in aggregate, not any particular business. For planners, they underscore the importance of the first year and of planning cash reserves and contingencies.
:::

## Categories of risk

| Category | Examples | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Market | Lower demand than expected; new competitor | Test demand; diversify customers |
| Financial | Cash shortfall; slow collections; rising costs | Reserves; deposits; credit line |
| Operational | Supplier failure; equipment breakdown | Backup suppliers; maintenance |
| People | Founder illness; key employee leaves | Documentation; cross-training; insurance |
| Legal and regulatory | Licensing; contracts; liability | Professional advice; insurance |
| Reputational | Poor reviews; service failure | Quality control; responsive service |
| Technology | Data loss; cyber attack | Backups; security practices |

## Assessing and responding

A simple risk register rates each risk's likelihood and impact from 1 to 5 and multiplies them for a score. High-scoring risks need a contingency plan and an early warning signal, a measurable indicator that the risk is materializing.

## The risk framework

**Step 1: List risks** in each category.

**Step 2: Rate likelihood and impact** from 1 to 5.

**Step 3: Rank risks** by score.

**Step 4: Write a response** for each of the top five: avoid, reduce, transfer, or accept.

**Step 5: Define early warning signals** and the threshold that triggers action.

**Step 6: Review the register** quarterly.

:::example Illustrative: a risk register entry
A hypothetical catering business rates "largest client cancels" at likelihood 3 and impact 5, for a score of 15. Its response is to cap any one client at 25 percent of revenue within 12 months and to require deposits. The early warning signal is a client's order volume falling 30 percent below its three-month average. The example is illustrative.

# Chapter 9: Funding the Plan: Lenders, Investors, and Your Own Money

:::objectives
- Distinguish debt, equity, and self-funding.
- Explain what SBA lenders typically review.
- Write a clear funding request and use of funds.
- Prepare for rejection and persistence.
:::

## Sources of funding

Small businesses are funded by owner savings, revenue, loans, investors, grants, and sometimes friends and family. Debt must be repaid with interest but leaves ownership intact. Equity gives investors a share of ownership and, typically, expectations of growth. Raising equity from investors is regulated by securities laws and requires legal advice.

## What lenders look for

The SBA's Lender Match page explains that most lenders expect a business plan when you apply for startup funding, along with clarity on the amount and use of funds, credit history, financial projections showing how you will repay the loan, and, in many cases, collateral [2]. The SBA's 7(a) page states that eligible businesses must, among other requirements, operate for profit, be creditworthy, and demonstrate a reasonable ability to repay [3].

| Lender question | Where the plan answers it |
|---|---|
| How much, and for what? | Funding request and use of funds |
| Can the business repay? | Cash flow projection and assumptions |
| What if things go wrong? | Risks, reserves, collateral |
| Who is behind this? | Management section; owner investment |
| Is the owner's credit sound? | Credit history (outside the plan) |

## The funding request

A funding request states the amount needed, its uses, the type of funding sought, and the terms requested, and shows how the funds connect to milestones and repayment. A use-of-funds table makes this concrete.

| Use | Amount | Milestone it supports |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment | ___ | Production capacity |
| Working capital | ___ | First six months of operations |
| Marketing launch | ___ | First 100 customers |
| Contingency | ___ | Risk buffer |
| Total | ___ | |

:::case Howard Schultz and 242 conversations
Business Insider, drawing on Howard Schultz's book Pour Your Heart Into It, reported that after leaving Starbucks in 1985, Schultz started his own coffee company, Il Giornale, and needed to raise more than $1.6 million; he wrote, "In the course of the year I spent trying to raise money, I spoke to 242 people, and 217 of them said no" [10]. Schultz's results are his own, not TTI's, and very few businesses become Starbucks. The case is instructive for an ordinary reason: rejection is a normal part of raising money, and a plan should be revised with each round of feedback rather than abandoned after the first refusals.
:::

## The funding framework

**Step 1: Calculate the total need** from the startup budget and cash flow projection.

**Step 2: Decide the mix** of owner money, debt, and other sources.

**Step 3: Prepare lender documents**: plan, projections, personal financial information, and credit history.

**Step 4: Write a funding request** with a use-of-funds table.

**Step 5: Practice the conversation** with a mentor or SBA resource partner.

**Step 6: Record feedback** from every rejection and revise.

:::example Illustrative: a funding request
A hypothetical auto-detailing founder needs $48,000. She will invest $8,000 of her savings and requests a $40,000 term loan. Uses: equipment $22,000, van modifications $9,000, working capital $11,000, contingency $6,000. Her expected-case projection shows monthly cash flow after expenses and owner's draw of about $1,350 by month six, compared with a projected loan payment of about $800. The first lender declines; she records the reasons (limited operating history) and reapplies after six months of operations. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Funding readiness
- Total need calculated from budget and projections.
- Owner investment stated.
- Use-of-funds table completed.
- Repayment shown in cash flow projection.
- Personal financial information and credit history ready.
- Mentor review completed.
- Feedback log started.
:::

:::summary
Funding may come from owner savings, revenue, debt, equity, or grants, each with trade-offs. SBA guidance indicates lenders expect a business plan, clear use of funds, credit history, projections showing repayment, and often collateral. Howard Schultz's experience of 217 refusals in 242 conversations shows that persistence and revision are part of fundraising.
:::

# Chapter 10: Writing the Executive Summary Last

:::objectives
- Explain the purpose of an executive summary.
- Write a summary that a busy reader can absorb in about a minute.
- Avoid common summary mistakes.
- Tailor the summary to each audience.
:::

## Why last

The executive summary appears first but should be written last, because it distills everything else. The SBA advises that the executive summary briefly tell readers what the company is and why it will be successful, including the mission statement, product or service, and basic information about leadership, employees, and location, and, if seeking financing, financial information and high-level growth plans [1].

## A one-page structure

1. **The business in one sentence.**
2. **The problem and the customer.**
3. **The solution and why it is better.**
4. **Evidence**: demand tests, early sales, letters of intent.
5. **The team.**
6. **Key numbers**: price, margin, break-even, projected revenue range.
7. **The request**: amount, use, and what the reader gets.
8. **Milestones** for the next 12 months.

:::case Tesla revisited: a strategy in 31 words
Business Insider observed that at the bottom of Elon Musk's 2006 master plan, Musk summed up Tesla's strategy "in just 31 words": build a sports car; use that money to build an affordable car; use that money to build an even more affordable car; while doing so, provide zero-emission electric power generation options; and, jokingly, "Don't tell anyone" [18][9]. Tesla's results are its own, not TTI's. The relevance for an executive summary is the discipline of compression: after explaining the reasoning in detail, Musk gave readers a version they could remember and repeat. A small-business summary should aim for the same clarity, even if the subject is a bakery rather than a car company.
:::

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Starting with history | Reader doesn't know why to care | Start with what you do and for whom |
| Vague claims ("huge market") | Signals lack of research | Use specific, sourced numbers |
| Hiding the request | Reader can't act | State amount and use clearly |
| Over two pages | Not read | One page, two at most |
| Hype ("guaranteed success") | Undermines credibility | State evidence and risks honestly |

## The executive-summary framework

**Step 1: Finish every other section** first.

**Step 2: Draft each of the eight elements** in one to three sentences.

**Step 3: Cut to one page.**

**Step 4: Read it aloud** in under two minutes.

**Step 5: Ask someone unfamiliar** with the business to explain it back to you.

**Step 6: Tailor** the evidence and request for each audience.

:::example Illustrative: an executive summary opening
"Rivera Mobile Bikes repairs bicycles at customers' homes and workplaces in Denver. Many commuters cannot spare a day without their bike; we fix most problems on-site in under an hour. In a 10-week pilot we completed 214 repairs at an average ticket of $68, and two employers signed letters of intent for monthly on-site service. We request a $35,000 loan for a second van and tools, which our projections show can be repaid from operating cash flow within four years." The business and figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Executive summary
- Written after all other sections.
- Business described in one sentence.
- Problem, customer, and solution stated.
- Evidence included.
- Key numbers sourced and consistent with financials.
- Request clear.
- One page.
- Tested on an outsider.
:::

:::summary
The executive summary distills the plan into a page that tells readers what the company is, why it will succeed, what evidence supports it, and what you are asking for. Write it last, use specific numbers, and compress your strategy into a memorable form, as Tesla's 31-word summary did.
:::

# Chapter 11: Review, Test, and Keep the Plan Alive

:::objectives
- Review a plan for consistency and credibility.
- Turn the plan into an operating tool.
- Compare actual results with projections monthly.
- Revise the plan as you learn.
:::

## Review before you send

Before sharing a plan, check that the numbers in the executive summary match the financial projections, that assumptions are sourced, that the risks section addresses the biggest weaknesses a reader will notice, and that the document is free of errors. Ask at least one knowledgeable outsider, such as an accountant, banker, industry veteran, or SBA resource partner counselor, to review it. The SBA notes that free business counseling is available through its resource partner network [1].

## From plan to operating tool

After launch, a plan becomes a management tool. The most useful habit is a monthly comparison of actual results with projections, often called variance analysis.

| Line | Projected | Actual | Variance | Explanation | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |
| Customers | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |
| Variable costs | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |
| Fixed costs | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ | ___ |

:::case The meta-analytic case for planning and learning
Brinckmann, Grichnik, and Kapsa's meta-analysis found that planning's relationship with performance was stronger for established small firms than for new ones, and the authors suggested that uncertainty, limited prior information, and an absence of planning structures can limit the return on planning for new firms [6]. They proposed an approach that combines planning with learning [6]. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's. For a founder, the practical implication is that the value of a plan grows as real information accumulates; a plan reviewed and revised monthly against actual results becomes more useful over time.
:::

## When to revise

Revise the plan when a key assumption proves wrong, when results differ from projections by more than an agreed threshold (for example 20 percent for two consecutive months), when you seek new funding, or at least annually.

## The keep-it-alive framework

**Step 1: Run a consistency check** before sharing the plan.

**Step 2: Obtain an outside review.**

**Step 3: Set up a monthly variance review** in a recurring calendar slot.

**Step 4: Track your riskiest assumptions** with real data.

**Step 5: Revise the plan** when thresholds are crossed.

**Step 6: Write an annual update** with a new 12-month projection.

:::example Illustrative: a variance review
A hypothetical tutoring business projected 120 sessions in month four; it delivered 94, a 22 percent shortfall, for the second consecutive month. Revenue per session matched projections, but referrals from schools were slower than assumed. The owner revises her marketing plan to add parent workshops, lowers the projection for months five to eight, and confirms that cash reserves still cover the lowest cash month. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Plan review
- Summary numbers match financials.
- Assumptions sourced.
- Major weaknesses addressed in risks.
- Proofread.
- Outside review completed.
- Monthly variance review scheduled.
- Revision thresholds set.
- Annual update scheduled.
:::

:::summary
Review a plan for consistency and credibility, and obtain outside feedback before sharing it. After launch, compare actual results with projections monthly and revise when assumptions fail. Meta-analytic evidence suggests planning becomes more valuable as information accumulates, which supports an approach that combines planning with learning.
:::

# Appendix E: A Worked Cash Flow Example

The following example continues the hypothetical Harbor Street Laundry Pickup from Appendix B. All figures are invented for teaching, and they show how the assumptions log, break-even analysis, and monthly cash flow fit together.

**Assumptions.** Price $32 per bag; variable cost $11 per bag; fixed costs $4,200 per month, including rent of $1,400, insurance of $250, van loan payment of $520, marketing of $600, software and phone of $180, and part-time wages of $1,250 from month three (before month three, fixed costs are $2,950). Owner's draw is $1,500 per month from month four. Tax set-aside is 20 percent of positive monthly operating cash flow. Opening cash is $7,000 owner investment plus $5,000 of working capital from the loan.

**Expected-case volume.** Bags per month: 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 200, 210, 220, 230, 240, 240, 250.

| Month | Bags | Contribution ($21/bag) | Fixed costs | Draw | Operating cash flow | Ending cash (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60 | $1,260 | $2,950 | $0 | −$1,690 | $10,310 |
| 2 | 90 | $1,890 | $2,950 | $0 | −$1,060 | $9,250 |
| 3 | 120 | $2,520 | $4,200 | $0 | −$1,680 | $7,570 |
| 4 | 150 | $3,150 | $4,200 | $1,500 | −$2,550 | $5,020 |
| 5 | 180 | $3,780 | $4,200 | $1,500 | −$1,920 | $3,100 |
| 6 | 200 | $4,200 | $4,200 | $1,500 | −$1,500 | $1,600 |
| 7 | 210 | $4,410 | $4,200 | $1,500 | −$1,290 | $310 |

**What the example reveals.** On a pure break-even basis, the business covers its fixed costs at 200 bags in month six. But once the owner's draw is included, cash continues to fall, and by month seven the reserve is nearly gone. The founder has three choices: increase the working-capital portion of the loan, delay the owner's draw, or find ways to reach about 272 bags per month (the volume that covers fixed costs plus the draw) sooner. This is the kind of insight a monthly projection produces and an annual summary hides. Notice that the sample executive summary refers to fixed-cost break-even, not cash break-even; a consistency check should make sure readers cannot confuse the two.

**Scenario test.** In a conservative case with volumes 25 percent lower, cash runs out in month five. A lender reviewing the plan would reasonably ask how the founder would respond, and the risks section should answer that question before it is asked.

**Contribution margin.** Price minus variable cost per unit.

**Customer development.** Steve Blank's process for testing business hypotheses with customers.

**Equity.** Ownership in a business; also, investment in exchange for ownership.

**Executive summary.** A one- to two-page distillation of a business plan.

**Fixed costs.** Costs that do not vary with sales volume in the short term, such as rent.

**Income statement.** A statement of revenue, expenses, and profit for a period.

**Lean plan.** A short plan summarizing key elements and hypotheses.

**Minimum viable product.** The smallest offering that allows a team to learn whether customers want a product.

**Use of funds.** A breakdown of how requested money will be spent.

**Variance analysis.** Comparison of actual results with projections.

**Variable costs.** Costs that rise and fall with each sale.
