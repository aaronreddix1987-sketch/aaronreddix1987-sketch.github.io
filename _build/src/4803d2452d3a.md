# How to Use This Book

## Who this book is for

The First 365 is for adults in their first year of recovery from alcohol or drug problems, including people preparing to leave treatment, jail, or prison and begin or continue recovery in the community. Family members and other supportive people may find it useful, too. Read on your own, with someone you trust, or alongside treatment and other support.

People begin recovery with different histories, resources, cultures, responsibilities, and hopes. There is no single starting point or timetable. Recovery can include improving health and wellness, directing your own life, and working toward your potential. It may also involve building stability at home, finding meaningful daily activities, and strengthening supportive relationships [1] [2].

## What this book is and is not

This educational guide offers plain-language explanations, planning frameworks, and exercises to adapt to your circumstances. Use what helps and set aside what does not. It is not medical advice or a substitute for treatment or speaking with a doctor or clinician. Discuss symptoms, withdrawal, medication, and other health concerns with a qualified health care professional. Nor does the book replace ongoing support from a sponsor, peer specialist, or someone who knows you; you can decide which kinds of support to seek.

Recovery has many pathways, including clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, and self-care, in different combinations. Progress can include setbacks, and choices may change over time [1]. You need not adopt a particular belief, group, or recovery identity to use this guide.

:::warning If you are in danger right now
If you are in crisis, call or text **988** for free, confidential support at any time. In Spanish, call 988 and press 2; veterans can press 1 [3]. For an immediate emergency, call **911**.

For treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at **1-800-662-4357**. It is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day in English and Spanish. TTY: **1-800-487-4889** [4]. **FindTreatment.gov** is a confidential, anonymous treatment-finding resource for the United States and its territories, available in Spanish [5].
:::

## How to use the chapters

Read from beginning to end, turn to a current concern, or revisit an exercise later. If you are leaving a structured setting, start with immediate practical questions and return to broader topics when useful. If you are already months into recovery, begin with what feels most relevant.

A typical chapter uses the following elements. Front matter, including this section, may not include every element.

| Element | How it can help |
|---|---|
| Objectives | See what the chapter aims to cover |
| Explanation | Learn key ideas and plain-language definitions |
| Framework | Work through a topic in numbered steps |
| Illustrative example | Consider a fictional situation |
| Real case | Read a cited public story, when included |
| Worksheet | Record ideas or make a plan |
| Summary | Review the main points |
| Review | Check your understanding and consider next steps |

Fictional examples clarify ideas; they do not predict what will happen in your life. A cited real case draws on a public story. The person’s or organization’s experience and results are their own, not a promise of your outcome or results of TTI.

Worksheets and review questions are invitations, not tests. Talk through an exercise, make a brief note, or reflect without writing if that works better.

## Go at your own pace

Recovery topics can bring up difficult memories, feelings, or questions. Pause, skip an exercise, or return later. If safe and helpful, work through difficult material with a trusted person or clinician; you choose whether to share what you write.

This reflects a trauma-informed principle: support should take safety, trust, collaboration, and personal choice seriously [6]. You decide how to use the book. If a section feels overwhelming, set it aside and focus on feeling grounded and supported in the present.

The first year brings planned changes and unexpected demands. Treat the book as a resource, not a scorecard. Return to chapters as circumstances change, or complete only part of an exercise. Progress looks different for each person.

## Language and respect

This guide uses person-first language, such as “person with a substance use disorder” and “person in recovery.” A person is more than a diagnosis, past event, or period of substance use. You may use different words for your experience; no single label fits everyone.

People may draw on mutual-help groups, treatment, medication, therapy, faith communities, secular supports, family relationships, and personal practices. Approaches can change over time. If a suggestion does not fit, consider another option.

## Suggested reading paths

These are starting points, not rules.

**If you are in your first week out:** Focus on immediate needs and a short, realistic plan. Consider who you can contact, where to find support, and what questions need a clinician’s attention. If leaving treatment or incarceration, use your transition plan as a starting point. Return to other chapters as the first days settle.

**If you are already a few months in:** Start with the pressing topic, such as routines, relationships, purpose, support, or responding to a lapse or difficult moment. Revisit exercises and adjust plans as your needs change.

**If you are a family member:** Begin with chapters **1, 2, and 9**. Consider the person’s choices and needs alongside your own well-being and boundaries. Offer support without taking responsibility for another person’s recovery. A qualified clinician or family support resource can help you consider appropriate support.

## A final note before you begin

Use this guide as a companion, not an authority over your life. Keep what is practical, question what does not fit, and seek professional help when a concern needs more than a book can offer. Move at your own pace and seek support that respects your safety, values, and goals.

# Chapter 1: What Recovery Really Means

:::objectives
- Explain a working definition of recovery and describe its four dimensions.
- Recognize that recovery can follow many pathways and that setbacks do not erase progress.
- Identify personal, social, community, and cultural resources that may support recovery.
- Use five starting questions and a brief self-assessment to choose a next step.
:::

## Recovery is a process, not a label

The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) describes recovery as a process of change in which people improve health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and work toward their full potential [1]. This definition allows for different goals and routes. It does not require one label, program, or pace.

For someone with a substance use disorder, recovery may involve reducing or stopping substance use, addressing health needs, rebuilding trust, finding stable housing, or returning to school or work. What matters is whether a person is moving toward a life they value and can sustain. You may use the word recovery, or choose different language.

Recovery does not promise an easy life. It involves responding to circumstances, making informed choices, and seeking support when needed. A lapse or return to use can be a reason to review support and treatment, not proof of failure [7]. You deserve care and practical help, not shame.

This guide is educational, not a substitute for treatment or advice from a clinician. Discuss health concerns, withdrawal, and treatment decisions with a qualified professional. In crisis, call or text 988; for emergency help, call 911. For treatment referrals, call the free, confidential SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357, available at all times [3] [4].

## Four dimensions of recovery

SAMHSA describes four connected dimensions of recovery. They are not a checklist or a required sequence. Progress in one area may support another, while needs may arise in several areas at once [2].

| Dimension | What it can mean | A starting question |
|---|---|---|
| Health | Managing health needs and making informed choices | What health support would help me now? |
| Home | A stable and safe place to live | Where can I feel safer and more settled? |
| Purpose | Meaningful activities and the resources to take part | What gives my days meaning or direction? |
| Community | Relationships that offer support, friendship, love, or hope | Who could be part of my support network? |

These dimensions help name needs without reducing you to a problem. You may be making progress with health while still needing safer housing, or have stable housing but feel isolated. Both belong in the recovery picture; progress does not have to wait until every area is settled.

## Ten principles to guide the process

SAMHSA’s principles offer a guide, not a set of rules. Here they are in everyday language [1].

| Principle | In everyday language |
|---|---|
| Hope | Change can begin with a small step. |
| Person-driven | Your goals and choices matter. |
| Many pathways | Different supports may fit different people and times. |
| Shared responsibility | People and systems can work together to support recovery. |
| Safe healing | Support should protect safety, trust, choice, and dignity. |
| Relationships | Connection with others can be part of recovery. |
| Culture | Culture and lived experience shape what support feels right. |
| Holistic | Health, home, purpose, and community are connected. |
| Respect | People deserve respect at every stage. |
| Peer support | Helping and learning from others can strengthen recovery. |

A trauma-informed approach attends to safety, trust, peer support, collaboration, choice, and cultural and historical experience, aiming to avoid further harm [6]. You should be able to ask questions, understand options, and say when a plan feels unsafe or unworkable. If it feels safe, involve someone you trust; you can also skip a question and return to it later.

## Many pathways can lead forward

No single route fits everyone. Support may include clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, cultural practices, or self-care, alone or in combination [1]. Needs and preferences can change. A clinician can help with medical questions and treatment decisions; a peer group may offer connection and shared experience. The person receiving support remains central to choosing goals.

In a national probability survey, 9.1% of US adults reported that they used to have a problem with alcohol or drugs but no longer did, often summarized as about 22 million adults. Of this group, 46% identified as being in recovery and 53.9% reported using an assisted pathway. Reported supports included mutual help (45.1%), treatment (27.6%), recovery support services (21.8%), and recovery community centers (6.2%) [8]. These are survey responses, not predictions about an individual.

The findings show that many people resolve alcohol or drug problems through varied routes, and do not all use the same label [8]. Your path need not look like someone else’s. Stay open to support while deciding what fits your values, needs, and circumstances.

## Recovery capital: resources you can build on

Recovery capital refers to the personal and social resources available to address substance dependence and strengthen the chances and opportunities for recovery [9]. This strengths-based view considers both challenges and existing or developable resources. Even modest resources matter.

Four kinds of recovery capital can help organize this picture [9]:

- **Personal capital:** health, skills, hope, coping strategies, and the ability to ask for help.
- **Social capital:** supportive relationships with family, friends, peers, or others.
- **Community capital:** useful local places, services, groups, and opportunities.
- **Cultural capital:** values, traditions, language, identity, and connections that help you feel understood and grounded.

Resources need not be perfect. A relative may help in one way but not another; a service may meet some needs but not all. Notice what is available, what is missing, and what you might strengthen. Complete the worksheet privately, leave items blank, or return to it with someone you trust.

## Five starting questions

Use these questions as a framework, not an exam. Write a few words, talk them through, or leave an answer open. The aim is one useful next step, not a solution to every problem.

1. **What do I want recovery to make possible?** Name something that matters, such as stability, connection, health, or greater choice.
2. **What would support my health right now?** Consider health concerns, professional support, or routines to discuss with a clinician. If withdrawal or another medical issue may be a concern, seek advice from a health care provider rather than managing it alone.
3. **Where can I be safer and more settled?** Consider where you will sleep, who will be there, and what could make the setting more stable. If the situation feels unsafe, discuss options with a trusted professional or support person.
4. **What can give my days purpose?** Work, school, caring for family, volunteering, or creative activity can provide meaning [2]. Notice one activity worth exploring; you need not choose a lifelong direction today.
5. **Who and what can help me stay connected?** Consider supportive people, peers, community resources, and cultural connections. If one option does not fit, consider another.

Choose one specific, manageable action, such as asking a clinician a question, contacting someone supportive, or learning about nearby groups or services. Before leaving treatment, jail, or prison, it may help to identify a contact and a place to go. You can ask for help before every resource is arranged.

:::example Illustrative example: Malik maps his starting point
Malik is a fictional person preparing to leave a residential program. He thinks of recovery as having more say in his days and fewer crises. He plans to ask a clinician how to continue discussing care after discharge. For housing, he lists two places to ask about. For purpose, he chooses to explore a short training course without making it a test of his worth. He names a cousin and a peer from the program as possible contacts. His plan has gaps, but he also sees resources to build on. He decides to ask his discharge worker about the next housing contact and call his cousin. His steps are his own; another person may choose different supports.
:::

The questions can guide a conversation with a clinician, peer, family member, or community worker. A changing answer is useful information: recovery involves adjustment, and plans can change as needs and choices change [7].

## Putting the chapter to work

A first-year recovery plan can be simple: identify what matters, consider the four dimensions, take stock of resources, and choose one next step. Decide when to check whether it helped. If a support is not a good fit, use that information to consider another option. Asking for help is one way to use the resources around you.

:::summary
- Recovery is a self-directed process of change, not one label or fixed route [1].
- Health, home, purpose, and community are connected dimensions for identifying needs and progress [2].
- Recovery pathways vary; survey findings describe people resolving alcohol or drug problems through different supports and labels [8].
- Recovery capital includes personal, social, community, and cultural resources. Taking stock can reveal strengths and gaps [9].
- Use the five starting questions to choose one manageable action. Revise plans and seek help as needs change.
:::

# Chapter 2: Understanding Addiction Without Shame

:::objectives
- Describe addiction as a treatable condition that may need ongoing care and support.
- Understand what relapse-rate comparisons can and cannot tell you.
- Distinguish shame, guilt, and responsibility without reducing yourself to a past action.
- Choose how much of your recovery story to share in different settings.
- Practice rewriting a self-critical story in a more accurate and useful way.
:::

## A condition, not a character verdict

A substance use disorder is a health condition, not proof that someone is weak, selfish, or beyond help. Treatment can support change, though it may take time and may need to adapt as a person’s needs change.

The National Institute on Drug Abuse describes addiction as a treatable disorder. Like other chronic diseases, such as heart disease or asthma, it is managed rather than cured by treatment. A return to use does not mean treatment has failed; a lapse may signal that care should be restarted or adjusted [7].

Here, **chronic** means that a condition may need attention over time. It does not mean progress is impossible or that one plan fits every stage of life. Recovery involves improving health and wellness, living a self-directed life, and working toward one’s potential. Pathways may include clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, and self-care [1].

Involve a doctor or other clinician in treatment decisions and health concerns. This guide is educational, not a substitute for treatment. If stopping alcohol may cause withdrawal, talk with a health care provider; alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous and may involve seizures or confusion [10].

## What relapse-rate comparisons mean

NIDA reports relapse-rate ranges of 40 to 60 percent for drug addiction, 30 to 50 percent for type I diabetes, 50 to 70 percent for hypertension, and 50 to 70 percent for asthma [11]. The comparison supports treating addiction as a health condition and a return to use as a reason to renew support, not as a moral failing [11].

| Condition | Reported relapse rate |
|---|---:|
| Drug addiction | 40 to 60 percent |
| Type I diabetes | 30 to 50 percent |
| Hypertension | 50 to 70 percent |
| Asthma | 50 to 70 percent |

These broad rates do not predict an individual’s outcome, show that the conditions are identical, or identify the best care for a particular person. They measure neither effort nor worth. Their practical message is that setbacks can occur and support may need to be renewed or adjusted.

If you return to use, treat the event as information rather than a verdict. Consider what happened beforehand and whether changes in stress, routine, housing, relationships, or access to care played a part. A clinician or trusted recovery support can help identify ways to make the next stretch safer.

## Shame, guilt, and responsibility

**Shame** turns an action or struggle into a judgment of the whole person: I made a mistake becomes I am a mistake. It can prompt hiding or withdrawal, but does not offer a reliable plan.

**Guilt** focuses on a particular action or its effects. It may point toward repair, such as listening, apologizing, or making a practical change. You can take regret seriously without using it to punish yourself.

**Responsibility** means recognizing choices and effects, then deciding what you can do now. It does not mean claiming total control over a complicated situation. It may involve asking for help, keeping an appointment, setting a boundary, or making amends when appropriate and safe. You can take responsibility without deciding you are hopeless.

Ask: **What is one next action that supports safety, repair, or recovery?** This does not erase harm; it directs attention toward a choice you can make now.

## Language can open or close a door

Person-first language puts the person before the condition. It does not require you to hide your history or use a label you do not choose. You can describe what happened while recognizing that your life is larger than that experience.

| Stigmatizing framing | Person-first alternative |
|---|---|
| A person reduced to a substance-use label | A person with a substance use disorder |
| A person described as morally failing | A person dealing with a health condition |
| A person defined by a lapse | A person who returned to use or had a lapse |
| A person judged by a recovery status score | A person in recovery, with their own path |

If someone’s language hurts, you can correct it if safe: I prefer person in recovery, or I am working on my health and would rather not use that label. You can also choose not to correct them. Language is one part of respect, not a test.

## Sharing your history is your choice

There is no required amount to disclose. Your choice may depend on safety, the relationship, and what you want from the conversation. You do not owe everyone your full story.

With family, you might ask for specific support: I am working on recovery, and I would appreciate a check-in this week. If a conversation becomes unsafe or blaming, pause it or seek support before deciding what to share.

At work, you may keep details private and focus on what you need: I have a health matter I am managing and need to discuss a schedule change. For questions about workplace requests or rights, consult a qualified professional or appropriate workplace resource; this chapter cannot determine your legal rights.

With new friends, you might start small: I do not drink, and I am comfortable meeting somewhere that does not center on alcohol. A boundary can be simple; you do not need to defend it.

## A step-by-step way to tell your story

Use these steps before a conversation, or afterward. Skip any that do not help.

1. **Name your purpose.** Decide whether you want understanding, practical help, a boundary, or an honest answer.
2. **Check the setting.** If the person or place does not feel safe, wait, choose another setting, or share less.
3. **Choose a level of detail.** You can be truthful without sharing private details.
4. **Leave room for change.** Describe yourself as a person with a history and a present, not a permanent label.
5. **Make a request or set a boundary.** State what would help or what you are not ready to discuss.
6. **Notice the response.** Continue, change the subject, end the conversation, or seek support. You can revise your choice.

:::example Illustrative example: Maya chooses what to share
Maya is preparing to visit relatives after leaving treatment. She expects questions but is not ready to describe every detail. She decides to ask for a calm place to stay and avoid a long conversation about the past. She plans to say, I am working on my recovery and would like to talk about what support looks like now. If questions feel too personal, she will say, I am not ready to go into that, but I appreciate your concern. Maya is choosing a manageable level of sharing and focusing on what she needs today. This is an illustrative example, not a report about a TTI client.
:::

## If a return to use happens

A lapse can bring fear, regret, or self-judgment. Focus first on immediate safety. If someone may be in immediate danger, call 911. In a crisis, call or text 988 for free, confidential support at any time. For treatment referrals, the SAMHSA National Helpline is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day in English and Spanish at 1-800-662-4357 [3] [4].

When immediate safety is addressed, contact a clinician or recovery support. NIDA describes a lapse as a signal that treatment may need to be reinstated or adjusted [7]. If you have had a period without opioids, returning to amounts your body can no longer tolerate increases the risk of overdose and death [12]. Discuss overdose safety and available supports with a clinician. Do not use this chapter to make medication decisions.

# Chapter 3: Safety First

:::objectives
- Explain why safety is the starting point for early recovery.
- Identify warning signs and practical steps for a personal safety plan.
- Choose people and professional services to contact in a crisis.
- Adapt a plan for transitions such as leaving treatment or incarceration.
:::

## Why safety comes first

Early recovery can bring hope, uncertainty, and practical change. You may be returning to an unsettled home, job, or relationship, leaving treatment or incarceration, or learning to respond to difficult feelings and urges in new ways. A safety plan does not predict that something bad will happen; it gives you options for a hard moment.

Safety includes more than protection from immediate physical danger. It can mean having a place to rest, a way to ask for help, and the choice to take a next step without feeling pushed or shamed. Recovery includes health, home, purpose, and community, each of which can affect how supported and secure you feel [2].

SAMHSA describes recovery as a process of change in which people improve health and wellness, live self-directed lives, and work toward their potential. Recovery is person-driven, follows many pathways, and may include setbacks [1]. A safety plan should fit your circumstances and can change as they do.

A trauma-informed approach recognizes the prevalence of trauma and seeks to avoid causing further harm. Its principles include safety, trust, peer support, collaboration, empowerment and choice, and attention to cultural, historical, and gender issues [6]. You deserve clear information, respectful treatment, and choices where possible. You do not have to tell your whole story to make a plan. Skip questions that feel unsafe or too personal, and return to them later with someone you trust if you wish.

This chapter is educational, not a substitute for treatment or emergency care. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. For emotional crisis or urgent support, call or text 988; the service is free, confidential, available around the clock, and offers Spanish-language support [3]. For treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357; it is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day [4].

## Make a plan you can use

Keep your plan short enough to find and follow when concentration is difficult. Write it on paper, save it on your phone, or use both. If you share it, choose people likely to respect your privacy and choices. You can complete the steps with a counselor, peer, family member, or trusted person, or work through them on your own.

1. **Name situations you want to prepare for.** Consider moments that have felt risky or overwhelming: being alone after a conflict, passing a place connected with substance use, having an urge, or facing a housing change. Be specific and nonjudgmental. A warning sign is information, not a character flaw.

2. **Notice early warning signs.** These may be thoughts, feelings, actions, or circumstances that indicate you need support soon. Examples include pulling away from people, missing appointments, feeling unable to settle, or thinking that asking for help will not matter. Use signs that fit your experience; you need not wait for an emergency to respond.

3. **Choose immediate coping steps.** List actions that are safe and realistic where you are. You might move to a shared or public space, pause a difficult conversation, use a grounding activity, or contact a peer. When an urge arises, you might stay with it briefly and notice your breathing as it changes. This practice, called urge surfing, lets an urge rise and fall without acting on it [13]. It is a skill, not a test.

4. **Decide whom to contact.** Name one person for everyday support and another option if that person is unavailable. Ask whether they are willing to be part of your plan and what support they can offer. Make a specific request: “Can you stay on the phone with me?” or “Can you help me contact my counselor?” A trusted person can help you take a next step but does not replace professional care.

5. **Record professional options.** Include your treatment provider, clinician, local crisis service, or referral service. If you are unsure which service fits, describe what is happening and ask what to do next.

6. **Plan for substance-related health concerns with a professional.** If you think you may have alcohol withdrawal, contact a health care provider. Withdrawal can cause dangerous seizures and confusion, and severe untreated withdrawal can be fatal [10]. Do not rely only on this guide or a personal plan; a clinician can assess what care is needed.

7. **Review the plan after changes or difficult moments.** A move, schedule or relationship change, or return to use may affect available support. Update contacts and note what helped. A lapse does not mean treatment has failed; it can signal a need to renew or adjust support [7]. Respond with care rather than blame.

:::example Illustrative example: Jordan prepares for a difficult evening
Jordan is preparing to leave a residential treatment program. Jordan notices that evenings alone can bring strong urges and that shame makes it harder to reach out. With a counselor, Jordan writes down two early signs: canceling plans and ignoring messages. The plan lists taking a walk in a familiar, well-lit area, going to a supportive relative’s home if it feels safe, and calling a peer. Jordan also saves the crisis and treatment referral contacts. Jordan decides to review the plan with the counselor before leaving and update it after settling into the next living arrangement. The plan does not guarantee that every evening will be easy; it gives Jordan more than one next step.
:::

## Make the plan fit your situation

A plan works best when it reflects where you are and which help you can reach. If you are leaving treatment, ask which supports will continue, how to contact them, and what to do if your first choice is unavailable. If you are preparing to leave jail or prison, arrange support beforehand when possible. After time without opioids, tolerance can drop; returning to amounts the body can no longer tolerate raises the risk of overdose and death [12]. Discuss transition risks and available support with a clinician or re-entry provider.

Naloxone can reverse an opioid overdose, including overdoses involving fentanyl, heroin, or prescription opioids, when given in time. Prefilled nasal spray and injectable forms can be used by people without medical training [14]. On March 29, 2023, the FDA approved Narcan 4 mg naloxone nasal spray for over-the-counter, nonprescription use [15]. Ask a clinician, pharmacist, or local support provider about access or use. Keep naloxone where others know where it is, and still call 911 in an emergency.

| Situation | Possible warning sign | First step | Additional support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feeling isolated | Stopping replies | Move near safe company | Call a trusted person |
| A strong urge | Thinking you must act now | Pause and use a coping skill | Contact a peer or clinician |
| Possible alcohol withdrawal | Concern about stopping after heavy drinking | Contact a health care provider | Seek urgent help if in danger |
| Immediate danger | Someone may be seriously harmed | Call emergency services | Follow the responder’s guidance |

# Chapter 4: Building Your Support Team

:::objectives
- Explain why recovery support can include people with different roles and perspectives.
- Map clinical, peer, mutual-help, personal, and community supports.
- Compare ways to find care and connect with a group.
- Create a practical plan for reaching out, including after a transition from treatment or custody.
:::

## Recovery is supported by relationships

Recovery is not a test of willpower that you must pass alone. SAMHSA describes recovery as a process of change through which people improve health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and work toward their full potential. It recognizes many pathways, including clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, and self-care. Relationships and peer support are part of that picture, and a setback does not erase the work already done. [1]

A **support team** is the group of people and services you can turn to for different kinds of help. It does not need to be a formal team, and you do not have to tell everyone the same things. One person might help you find a counselor; another might join you at a meeting; someone else might be a trusted friend you call when a day feels difficult. You choose who is involved and what you share.

Support can also be practical. A stable place to live, meaningful daily activity, and relationships that offer care and hope are among SAMHSA’s four dimensions of recovery: Home, Purpose, Community, and Health. [2] If you are leaving treatment, jail, or prison, planning who you will contact and where you will go can help make the first days more organized. If a person or setting does not feel safe, you can look for another option.

A trauma-informed approach values safety, trust, collaboration, choice, and empowerment. [6] In practice, that means you can ask questions, set boundaries, and make decisions with the people supporting you. You can also change your support plan as your needs change.

## The parts of a support team

Think of support as a set of roles rather than one person who must do everything. Some people may fill more than one role. Others may be available only for a short time.

- **Clinical support** may include a doctor, counselor, therapist, or prescriber. These professionals can discuss treatment options and help you decide what care fits your situation. Questions about symptoms, treatment, or medication belong with a qualified health care professional. This guide is educational and does not replace treatment or medical advice.
- **Peer support** comes from someone with lived experience who offers encouragement and practical connection. A peer specialist or recovery coach may help you identify goals, connect with services, or make a plan for a difficult moment. A sponsor is a peer relationship used in some mutual-help groups. The role and expectations can differ, so it is reasonable to ask how the relationship works.
- **Mutual-help groups** bring people together to share support. AA and NA are examples of mutual-help pathways. Participation is a choice, and people may try a meeting to see whether its approach feels useful. For alcohol, a review found that clinically delivered, manualized Twelve-Step Facilitation designed to increase AA participation led to higher rates of continuous abstinence than other active treatments. About 42% of AA participants in the reviewed studies remained completely abstinent one year later, compared with about 35% receiving other treatments. The review included 27 studies and 10,565 people; these findings concern alcohol and a specific kind of treatment, not a guarantee for any individual. [16]
- **Family and friends** may offer companionship, transportation, help with daily tasks, or a steady check-in. You can choose what is helpful and set limits around what is not. Support does not require giving someone access to private information or making them responsible for your recovery.
- **Community support** can include a faith community, cultural community, recovery community center, or other place where you can build connection and purpose. Recovery Cafés are one model of recovery community center. Their membership agreements include being drug- and alcohol-free in the space, taking part in a weekly Recovery Circle, and contributing to the Café. The model describes its practices as loving accountability and radical hospitality. [17]

Your team can be small at first. A first step might be one clinician and one peer contact, with a plan to explore a group or community resource later. Having more than one kind of support can give you options when someone is unavailable or a particular approach does not fit.

## Treatment and medication are legitimate pathways

Treatment can include counseling and behavioral therapies, medication, or a combination decided with a clinician. Medications approved by the FDA, when combined with counseling and behavioral therapies, are part of a whole-person approach to substance use disorders. Research summarized by SAMHSA reports that medication plus therapy can successfully treat substance use disorders. For some medications, benefits include helping sustain recovery; evidence also shows improved survival and treatment retention, decreased illicit opioid use, greater ability to gain and keep employment, and prevention or reduction of opioid overdose. Which options are relevant depends on a person’s situation and should be discussed with a clinician. [18]

Using medication is not weakness, and it does not make recovery less real. It is one evidence-based pathway. A clinician can explain options, possible benefits, and concerns in a way that applies to you. Do not start, stop, or change medication based on this chapter; discuss those decisions with your prescriber or another qualified clinician.

Mutual help is another pathway, and it can sit alongside clinical treatment rather than replace it. SMART Recovery offers a secular mutual-support option grounded in cognitive behavioral therapy. Its four-point program focuses on building and maintaining motivation, coping with urges and cravings, managing thoughts, feelings, and behaviors, and living a balanced life. Groups are led by trained volunteer facilitators. [19] People may attend different kinds of groups before deciding what feels like a good fit.

## A step-by-step way to build your team

1. **Name what you need first.** Keep the list concrete. You might need help finding a clinician, a safe place to stay, a ride, a meeting, a way to handle unstructured time, or someone to call. You do not need to solve every part of life at once.
2. **Choose one starting contact.** This could be a current counselor, doctor, peer worker, trusted friend, or treatment referral service. If you are still in treatment, ask whether you can make a first appointment or contact before leaving. If you are leaving custody, ask what local services and practical arrangements are available.
3. **Ask about options and choice.** You can ask what a service offers, what the first contact involves, whether there is a cost, and whether there are other approaches. If you are considering medication or have health concerns, speak with a clinician. You can bring a written list of questions or ask someone you trust to join you if that feels safe.
4. **Try a connection, then assess the fit.** After a meeting, appointment, or conversation, notice whether you felt respected, listened to, and able to ask questions. A single difficult interaction does not mean support is impossible. You may try a different person, group, or service.
5. **Make a simple contact plan.** Write down whom to contact during ordinary hours, whom to contact after hours, and what you will do if your first choice is unavailable. Include practical details such as a phone number, meeting location, or transportation plan.
6. **Review the plan regularly.** Needs shift during the first year. Keep what is helping, adjust what is not, and add support when you are ready. If a lapse or return to use happens, it is a reason to reconnect with support and talk with a clinician about whether care should be adjusted, not a reason to hide. [7]

The number is 1-800-662-4357; TTY is 1-800-487-4889. [4]

FindTreatment.gov is a confidential and anonymous resource for finding treatment for mental health and substance use disorders in the United States and territories, and it is available in Spanish. [5] You can use one of these resources to ask about options, then contact a service to learn whether it meets your needs. If you are in immediate crisis, call or text 988 for free, confidential support available around the clock. [3]

# Chapter 5: Structure for the First 90 Days

:::objectives
- Explain how a simple routine can make early recovery more manageable.
- Use a HALT check-in and basic sleep habits to notice needs before they build.
- Create daily anchors and a flexible weekly plan.
- Use three 30-day phases as a guide, not a test or deadline.
:::

## Why structure helps

The first months of recovery can bring many decisions: where to go, who to call, how to fill time, and what to do when stress or an urge appears. A basic routine reduces the number of choices you have to make in the moment. It also helps you notice unplanned gaps that may leave you alone, hungry, exhausted, or near people and places connected with past use.

Structure is not the same as controlling every hour. A useful plan has a few dependable points in the day and enough room to respond to changing needs. It can support self-trust: each time you follow through on a small, realistic commitment, you get evidence that you can rely on yourself. If a plan does not work, that is information. You can adjust it rather than treat it as a personal failure.

Recovery is a process of change in which people improve health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and work toward their potential [1]. People may follow different pathways, and progress can include setbacks [1]. Your routine can include clinical care, mutual help, family or peer support, faith or cultural practices, self-care, or other supports that fit your situation. If you are leaving treatment, jail, or prison, consider asking a trusted support person or clinician to help you plan the transition. Choose what feels safe and useful; you can skip an exercise and return to it later.

This chapter is educational and is not a substitute for treatment. A doctor or clinician should address persistent sleep problems, withdrawal concerns, or other health questions. Do not use this chapter to make medication decisions. In a crisis, call or text 988. For treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. In an emergency, call 911.

## Start with a check-in

HALT is a common recovery check-in. The letters stand for **hungry, angry, lonely, and tired**. It is a quick way to ask whether a basic need or feeling may be making the next hour harder. It is not a diagnosis, and it does not explain every urge or difficult moment. It can help you pause before deciding what to do.

Try asking: “Am I hungry? Am I angry or tense? Am I lonely or disconnected? Am I tired?” You might notice more than one answer. If it feels safe, respond to the need with a small step: eat something, take a quiet pause, contact a supportive person, or make room for rest. If the feeling remains, consider another support, such as a meeting, counselor, or clinician. A check-in is an invitation to care for yourself, not a demand to fix everything at once.

## Build a daily rhythm

Daily anchors are a few repeatable actions that mark the start, middle, and end of a day. Anchors can be simple. They do not have to happen at an exact time, and they can change around work, caregiving, appointments, or health needs. The goal is to make the day easier to navigate, not to create a perfect schedule.

### Step-by-step framework

1. **Choose one morning anchor.** Pick a first action that helps you begin the day, such as getting dressed, eating, checking your written plan, or contacting a support person.
2. **Choose one midday anchor.** Add a pause to eat, check HALT, take a short walk if that is comfortable for you, or confirm where you need to be next.
3. **Choose one evening anchor.** Decide how you will mark the end of the day. This might be preparing for tomorrow, spending time with a supportive person, or following a calming bedtime routine.
4. **Put support on the calendar.** Add meetings, appointments, calls, or other supports that fit your needs. If you are in treatment, use the plan you develop with your care team.
5. **Leave breathing room.** Avoid filling every open space. Travel, meals, rest, and unexpected needs take time.
6. **Review and revise.** At the end of the day or week, ask what helped, what got in the way, and what one adjustment might make the plan more workable.

A rhythm template can help you begin:

| Part of day | Possible anchor | Check-in question |
|---|---|---|
| Morning | Get ready and review plan | What support do I need today? |
| Midday | Eat and pause | What is my HALT check-in? |
| Evening | Prepare for rest | What helped me get through today? |

## Sleep and recovery

Sleep can affect how manageable a day feels, though people’s experiences differ. In a study of 119 patients in a Mayo Clinic residential alcohol program, 69% had sleep disturbances at admission. Researchers examined links between sleep problems and return to use over 12 months; findings varied by subgroup, and sleep was a relevant factor for some people but not others [21]. This supports taking sleep seriously without assuming that every sleep problem has the same cause or meaning.

Basic habits may help create a steadier routine: keep a fairly consistent time for getting up, make time to wind down, and use a quiet, comfortable sleep space when possible. If you wake during the night, try a calm activity rather than turning the moment into a test of willpower. Keep expectations realistic; routines may take time to settle. Persistent insomnia or a major change in sleep deserves a conversation with a doctor or clinician. Medication choices need a doctor or clinician; this chapter does not offer medication advice.

## Plan the week, not just the day

A weekly plan can protect time for both responsibilities and recovery supports. Include meetings or appointments, movement that feels appropriate to you, connection with supportive people, chores, rest, and something enjoyable. Fun matters because a life in recovery needs more than tasks and problem-solving. If you are unsure which supports fit, you can talk it through with a clinician or another trusted support person.

Try to make plans specific enough to act on. “Connect with someone” is harder to follow than “call Maya after work on Tuesday.” At the same time, allow flexibility. A missed meeting or changed shift does not have to erase the whole week; identify the next useful step and update the plan.

## The first 90 days: three planning phases

The phrase “first 90 days” can sound like a deadline. Here, it is simply a way to divide a large task into smaller planning windows. The phases below are not a clinical rule, and your pace may differ. You can repeat a phase, change its focus, or work on several areas at once. If your treatment or re-entry plan sets different priorities, discuss how to adapt your schedule with the people supporting you.

| Phase | Main focus | One practical task |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–30 | Establish safety and basic rhythm | Identify daily anchors and key contacts |
| Days 31–60 | Strengthen support and follow-through | Review the weekly plan and fill support gaps |
| Days 61–90 | Build a sustainable life pattern | Keep what works and revise what does not |

**Days 1–30: make the next day workable.** Focus on immediate needs, appointments, a safe place to stay, and a few simple anchors. If you are transitioning from treatment or custody, confirm practical details such as transportation and where you are expected to be. You do not need to solve every long-term question at once.

**Days 31–60: notice patterns.** Review which times of day feel most difficult and which supports are actually useful. Look for unplanned high-risk gaps, such as long stretches alone or an evening with no plan. Consider one small adjustment: arranging a call, adding a meeting, or making a meal plan.

**Days 61–90: keep the plan livable.** Continue supports that help and make changes where the routine feels too crowded or fragile. Add personally meaningful activities when you are ready. A steady life is not a life without problems; it is a life with ways to respond and people or resources to turn to.

# Chapter 6: Cravings and High-Risk Situations

:::objectives
- Define a craving and explain why an urge is not an instruction.
- Use Marlatt’s relapse prevention model to understand how risk can build and how coping can interrupt it.
- Identify personal high-risk situations and create a practical coping menu.
- Practice urge surfing and write an if-then plan for a likely trigger.
:::

## Cravings are experiences, not commands

A craving is an urge or desire to use alcohol or another drug. It may come with thoughts, feelings, memories, or body sensations. A craving can feel powerful, but it does not make a decision for you. You can notice it, choose what to do next, and ask for support. The urge may rise and fall; it is not a reliable forecast of what you will do.

Cravings can be connected to a person, place, object, feeling, time of day, or situation. They can also seem to arrive without a clear reason. Learning your patterns is not about blaming yourself. It is about gathering useful information so you can plan for moments when your risk may be higher.

The first year of recovery may include new routines, changing relationships, and practical pressures. Someone leaving treatment, jail, or prison may also be returning to places and people connected with past use. You do not have to solve every risk at once. Start with the situations you can name and the next helpful action you can take.

This chapter is educational and is not a substitute for treatment. If you may have alcohol withdrawal when stopping or reducing heavy drinking, talk with a health care provider. Alcohol withdrawal can involve dangerous seizures and confusion, and severe untreated withdrawal can be fatal [10]. For a crisis, call or text 988. For treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. In an emergency, call 911.

## A plain-language relapse prevention model

Marlatt’s relapse prevention model looks at both immediate risks and conditions that can build over time. Immediate determinants include high-risk situations, coping responses, expectations about what using will do, and the abstinence violation effect. Covert antecedents include lifestyle imbalances, urges, and cravings [22]. These are terms for patterns to notice, not labels for a person.

A **high-risk situation** is a moment or setting where you may be more likely to use. A **coping response** is what you do to get through that moment without using. An **outcome expectancy** is what you predict alcohol or drugs will do for you, such as make a painful feeling go away or help you fit in. Those expectations can be incomplete: a plan can leave out the costs that may follow.

**Self-efficacy** means a person’s belief that they can carry out actions needed for a specific outcome [23]. Each time you use a coping response that fits, you have a chance to strengthen confidence. A difficult situation does not prove you cannot cope. It can show where more support or a different plan may help.

The **abstinence violation effect** is a pattern in which a lapse leads to guilt or a sense of failure, and those reactions can raise the risk of further use [22]. A lapse is a signal to pause and seek support, not a reason to give up. NIDA describes addiction as treatable and notes that a lapse can mean treatment should be reinstated or adjusted [7]. If a return to use happens, tell a trusted support person or clinician and focus on the next safe step.

| Model element | Plain meaning | Useful question | Possible response |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-risk situation | A person, place, feeling, or event that raises risk | What is happening around me? | Leave or contact support |
| Coping response | An action for getting through the moment | What can I do next? | Delay, move, or call |
| Outcome expectancy | What I think using will do | What might I be leaving out? | Recall the full picture |
| Lifestyle imbalance | Ongoing strain or unmet needs | What has been off balance? | Restore routine and support |
| Abstinence violation effect | Guilt after a lapse that may add risk | What is one helpful next step? | Reach out and revise the plan |

## A step-by-step framework for planning

Use these steps when you have a calm moment. You can skip a question that feels unsafe or overwhelming and return to it later. If you are currently at risk, begin with the safest immediate action, such as moving away from alcohol or drugs or contacting someone you trust.

1. **Name the situation.** Describe the setting without judging yourself. Is it a payday, a particular route home, an argument, a lonely evening, or contact with someone connected to past use?
2. **Notice the early signs.** Record thoughts, feelings, body sensations, or changes in routine that tend to show up. Early signs might include bargaining with yourself, isolating, or thinking that one use will solve a problem.
3. **Check the prediction.** Ask what you expect alcohol or drugs to do. Then ask what else could happen and what matters to you beyond the next few minutes. You are not required to argue with every thought; the aim is to see that a thought is not the whole situation.
4. **Choose a coping action.** Pick one action that is possible now and one backup. A short, specific action is often easier to use than a broad promise to stay strong.
5. **Connect with support.** Decide who you can contact and how. Support may come from a trusted person, a peer, a meeting, a counselor, or another resource that fits your preferences.
6. **Review without blame.** After the situation passes, note what helped, what was hard, and what you might change. Planning can be adjusted as your life changes.

### Identify your high-risk situations

Think across five areas: **people, places, things, feelings, and times**. A person might be an old using companion or someone with whom conflict is common. A place could be a former neighborhood, a bar, or a room where you used. Things might include cash, a phone contact, or objects associated with use. Feelings can include anger, grief, excitement, loneliness, or stress. Times may include payday, late evenings, weekends, or the period after a difficult appointment.

Not every item is avoidable, and not every reminder creates the same level of risk. Mark which situations you can avoid for now, which you can change, and which you may need to face with support. If your living situation or safety is involved, seek help from someone qualified to work through that with you. You deserve a plan that respects your circumstances and choices.

### Build a coping menu

A coping menu gives you options before a craving peaks. Choose actions that fit your health, beliefs, responsibilities, and setting. You may want to write them down or save them on your phone. It can help to make the first action small enough to begin right away.

- **Leave:** Move away from a person, place, or item linked with use, if it is safe to do so.
- **Call:** Contact a trusted person, peer, counselor, or support group.
- **Delay:** Set a short period before making any decision about using, and use the time to try another action.
- **Distract:** Choose a task that holds your attention, such as a household chore, a game, or a creative activity.
- **Move:** Walk or change rooms if that is comfortable and safe for you.
- **Eat:** If you have not eaten and it is appropriate for you, have food and notice whether that helps you take the next step.
- **Pray or meditate:** Use a spiritual or secular practice that feels supportive to you.
- **Go to a meeting:** Choose a mutual-help meeting or other recovery gathering that fits your pathway.

A coping menu is not a test of willpower. If one action does not help, try another or ask someone to help you choose. Recovery can include clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, and self-care; people use different pathways [1]. Questions about treatment or health concerns belong with a clinician.

## Urge surfing: staying with an urge without acting on it

Urge surfing is a way to notice a craving and let it change without treating it as an order. The practice described in the research uses attention to the breath as an urge rises and reaches a peak, with the expectation that it will subside without acting on it [13]. You can try the steps below if doing so feels safe. If focusing inward feels uncomfortable, choose another coping action instead.

1. **Pause and orient.** Notice where you are. If it helps, place both feet on the floor and look around the room. Remind yourself that you are having an urge, not receiving a command.
2. **Name what is present.** Use simple words: craving, worry, anger, or restlessness. You do not need to explain why it is happening.
3. **Notice the breath.** Let your attention rest on a few natural breaths. There is no need to force a deep breath or make the urge disappear.
4. **Observe the changing experience.** Notice whether the urge changes in intensity, location, or quality. You can picture it as a wave rising, reaching a peak, and easing. If it does not ease yet, that is information, not failure.
5. **Choose the next action.** When you are ready, use your coping menu. You may still need to leave, call someone, or go to a meeting. Urge surfing can be one tool among several.

In a trial of 286 people, both mindfulness-based relapse prevention and standard relapse prevention reduced relapse compared with treatment as usual at six months. At 12 months, the mindfulness-based group had 31% fewer drug-use days and a lower probability of heavy drinking than the treatment-as-usual group [13]. This finding supports practicing skills for tolerating cravings; it does not promise a particular result for any one person.

## Make an if-then plan

An if-then plan specifies when, where, and how you will respond: **If situation Y occurs, then I will do X**. A review of 94 independent tests involving more than 8,000 participants found that these plans had a medium-to-large positive effect on goal attainment beyond goal intentions alone [24]. A useful plan is concrete and realistic. Include a backup in case your first choice is not available.

:::example Illustrative example: Payday trigger
Maya has noticed that payday often brings a strong urge to meet an old friend who uses alcohol and drugs. She decides that, if the urge begins on payday, then she will take a different route home, call her sister before leaving work, and go to an evening meeting. She saves her sister’s number and the meeting details in her phone. If her sister cannot answer, Maya’s backup is to contact a peer from her recovery group. Maya’s plan does not assume that the urge will vanish. It gives her steps to follow while she decides what she needs next.
:::

You can make the plan more useful by naming a cue you are likely to notice and an action you can begin quickly. For example: “If I start thinking I can use just once, then I will read my reasons for recovery and call my support person before I go anywhere.” Try the plan in writing. If it relies on something you cannot control, revise it to focus on your own next action.

# Chapter 7: If a Lapse Happens

:::objectives
- Explain the working difference between a lapse and a relapse.
- Identify immediate safety steps after a return to use, including added risks after a period without opioids.
- Use a first-day plan and a blame-free learning review to reconnect with support.
- Practice a clear, respectful way to talk with family or another trusted person.
:::

## A lapse is a moment to respond, not a verdict

People use the words lapse and relapse in different ways. For this chapter, a **lapse** means a return to alcohol or drug use after a period of not using, or after a personal recovery goal has been set. A **relapse** means a more sustained return to a pattern of use or a loss of progress toward that goal. These are working definitions, not labels you have to accept. You can describe what happened in your own words.

A return to use does not erase the work you have done. The National Institute on Drug Abuse explains that addiction is treatable and that, for some people, relapse can be part of the process. A lapse can signal that treatment should be resumed or adjusted; it does not mean treatment has failed [7]. The useful question is not, What is wrong with me? It is, What support or change would help me stay safer and move toward recovery now?

Recovery can include clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, and self-care. People may choose different pathways, and progress can include setbacks [1]. If you are unsure what to do next, a clinician can help you consider options. This guide is educational, not a substitute for treatment or medical care.

## Safety comes before analysis

After any return to use, first check the immediate situation. Move away from traffic, unsafe people, or other danger if you can do so safely. If you may be at risk of overdose, get help now. Do not use alone. If opioids may be involved, having naloxone available and another person present can matter. Naloxone can reverse an overdose from opioids, including fentanyl, heroin, and prescription opioids, when given in time [14]. Call emergency services if overdose is possible; do not wait to see whether the person improves. This is guidance for staying alive, not an endorsement of using alcohol or drugs.

A period without opioids can lower tolerance. Returning to amounts the body could no longer tolerate raises the risk of overdose and death [12]. This can be relevant after treatment, jail or prison, or another time without use. If someone has used opioids after a break, avoid being alone and make safety the first priority. If naloxone is available, learn how to use it according to its instructions. Naloxone is the standard treatment for opioid overdose [15].

Alcohol can also require medical attention. Some people who drink heavily experience withdrawal if they stop. If withdrawal may be a concern, talk with a health care provider; severe alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous and needs medical care [10]. Do not try to manage a serious medical concern by relying on this chapter.

In a crisis, call or text 988 for free, confidential support at any time; Spanish-language support is also available [3]. For treatment referrals and information, call the free, confidential SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357 [4]. In an emergency, call 911.

## A first 24-hour plan

The first day is not a test of character. It is a short period in which to protect safety, reduce isolation, and reconnect with support. You do not have to solve every part of recovery today. Use the steps in order when possible, and adapt them to what feels safe.

1. **Get safe.** Check whether you or someone else needs urgent help. Move to a safer place, avoid being alone if overdose is a concern, and contact emergency help when needed. If alcohol withdrawal may be a concern, contact a health care provider.
2. **Tell one person.** Choose someone who is likely to respond with care and help you take the next step. You can share only what is needed for safety. If one person is not safe to tell, choose another support person or a professional.
3. **Reconnect with care or support.** Contact your clinician, treatment program, peer, or recovery group. Ask what the next available step is. If you are not connected to care, you can seek a referral. Asking for help is a practical action, not a confession of failure.
4. **Learn without blame.** When you are safe and able, make a brief record of what happened before, during, and after the lapse. You can skip this and come back later. The goal is to notice useful information, not to punish yourself.
5. **Recommit to the next action.** Choose one specific action for the next day, such as attending a planned appointment, asking someone to accompany you, or making your environment safer. A next action can be small and still matter.

| Step | First action | A useful question | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Get safe | Move toward help | Is anyone in immediate danger? | Waiting alone with a serious concern |
| Tell one person | Contact a trusted person | Who can help me take one next step? | Trying to carry this in silence |
| Reconnect | Contact care or a group | What support can I access today? | Deciding that support is no longer for me |
| Learn | Note the sequence | What happened before, during, and after? | Turning the review into self-attack |
| Recommit | Choose one action | What is my next manageable step? | Demanding a perfect long-term plan today |

## How shame can extend a lapse

Marlatt’s relapse-prevention model includes high-risk situations, coping skills, expectations about what use will do, and hidden build-up factors such as lifestyle imbalance, urges, and cravings. Effective coping can strengthen confidence and reduce the chance of further use. When coping does not work, a lapse may be followed by guilt and a sense of failure. Marlatt describes this reaction as the abstinence violation effect; it can raise the likelihood of continued use [22].

This is a pattern to understand, not a reason to blame yourself. Thoughts such as I ruined everything or I might as well keep going can make it harder to seek help. Try replacing them with a precise statement: I used, I want to understand what happened, and I can take a safety step now. If shame makes it hard to speak, you can start with a brief message: I need support today. Can you stay with me while I contact my clinician?

A lapse can also provide information about what needs attention. Perhaps a social situation felt difficult, a coping plan was missing, or a basic need had gone unmet. These are possibilities to explore, not assumptions. You decide what to share and what feels safe to examine.

## A learning review without blame

A chain-style review looks at what came before, what happened during the lapse, and what followed. It does not assume that one event caused everything. Several pressures or choices may have played a part. If writing about the event increases distress, pause and return to it with a trusted person or clinician.

Ask yourself:

- **Before:** Where was I, and who was present? What was happening in my day or relationships? What feelings, urges, or expectations did I notice? Had I been sleeping, eating, or connecting with support in ways that worked for me?
- **During:** What happened first? What choices or events came next? What did I expect alcohol or drugs to do for me in that moment? Was there a point when I could have reached someone or changed the situation?
- **After:** What happened to my safety, mood, and relationships? What helped, even a little? What made it harder to stop or ask for help? What needs attention now?
- **Next time:** What is one early sign I could notice? What is one response I could try? Who could help me practice or carry out that response?

The purpose is not to prove that you should have known better. It is to find a possible point of support for next time. You might discover that you need a different plan for a particular setting, more contact with a peer, or help addressing a health or housing concern. Recovery includes more than substance use alone; health, a stable home, purpose, and supportive community are all recognized dimensions of recovery [2].

:::example Illustrative example: At a wedding
Maya has been avoiding alcohol for several months. At a cousin’s wedding, she feels tense after an argument with a family member and accepts a drink. Later, she thinks that she has ruined her progress and considers continuing to drink. Instead, she steps outside with a trusted cousin, says she wants help getting home safely, and contacts her recovery group the next morning. When Maya later reviews the evening, she notices that the argument and feeling alone came before the drink. She and her clinician discuss a plan for future family events, including a person she can check in with and a way to leave if she feels overwhelmed. Maya’s next steps are hers to choose; the lapse does not define her or settle what her recovery will look like.
:::

## Talking with family or another support person

You can decide how much to disclose. A useful conversation is specific about what happened, what you need now, and what you are not ready to discuss. If talking face to face does not feel safe, consider a message or a conversation with a clinician or peer present.

You might say: I had a return to use. I am taking steps to be safe and reconnect with support. I am not asking you to fix everything. What would help today is for you to listen while I contact my clinician, or to help me get to an appointment.

If a family member reacts with fear or anger, you can pause the conversation and return to it later. You do not need to settle every disagreement on the first day. You can ask for practical support, such as a ride or company while making a call, while also setting limits on questions or comments that feel harmful. Family members may need support of their own; your recovery does not require you to manage everyone else’s feelings.

# Chapter 8: Thoughts, Feelings and Trauma

:::objectives

- Practice naming a feeling, checking a thought, grounding, and steady breathing.
- Understand trauma-informed principles and how they can guide choices in recovery.
- Identify when to seek professional mental health support and how to find it.

## When feelings come back into view

Early recovery can bring many emotions into sharper focus. You may feel relief, grief, anger, fear, hope, boredom, or several things at once. Some days may feel manageable; other days may feel intense or confusing. There is no single correct emotional pattern for a person in recovery, and a difficult feeling does not mean you are failing.

Alcohol or other drugs may have been part of how you responded to distress. When that response changes, feelings that were pushed aside may become easier to notice. You may also be adjusting to changes in relationships, daily routines, housing, work, or contact with family. These changes can affect mood and concentration. A feeling is information about what is happening inside you; it is not an order you must follow, and it is not a verdict about who you are.

A useful first step is to pause and name what is present. “I feel bad” may be true, but it does not tell you much about what you need. You might instead name sadness, shame, worry, loneliness, or frustration. If no word fits, “I feel unsettled” is enough. You can be curious without forcing an explanation.

Thoughts also deserve attention, but they are not always facts. A thought such as “I have ruined everything” may arrive with the force of certainty. Checking it does not mean pretending everything is fine. It means looking for evidence, noticing what the thought leaves out, and trying a more balanced statement. This can make room for a next step that is safer and more useful.

## Four practical skills

These skills are options, not tests. You can use one, change it to suit your needs, or skip it and come back later. If focusing inward feels uncomfortable, try looking around the room or talking with a trusted person instead. These practices do not replace care from a qualified clinician.

### 1. Name the feeling

Pause and ask, “What am I feeling right now?” Choose a word, or use a broad description such as “tense” or “numb.” Then ask, “What might this feeling be asking me to notice?” You do not need to solve the feeling immediately. Naming it can help separate the feeling from the action you choose next.

Try adding a body cue if that feels comfortable: “I feel worried, and my shoulders are tight.” This is an observation, not a diagnosis. You may find that the feeling changes, stays the same, or becomes clearer after a few minutes.

### 2. Check a thought

Write the thought as a sentence. Ask what evidence supports it and what evidence does not fit. Then create a balanced thought that makes room for both the difficulty and the possibility of a next step. A balanced thought is not forced optimism. It should sound believable to you.

For example, “I made a mistake, so there is no point trying” might become “I made a mistake. I can ask for help and decide what to do next.” If the thought concerns a serious decision, consider talking it through with someone you trust or a clinician rather than relying on a worksheet alone.

### 3. Ground with your senses

The 5-4-3-2-1 exercise directs attention to the present surroundings. Look around, and name five things you can see, four things you can feel through touch, three things you can hear, two things you can smell, and one thing you can taste. You can adapt the sequence or leave out a sense if it is uncomfortable or unavailable. There is no need to close your eyes.

Grounding is a way to orient to the room you are in. It may help you pause when thoughts or emotions feel consuming, but it does not have to make the feeling disappear. If the exercise increases distress, stop and choose a simpler action, such as placing both feet on the floor or naming the date and where you are.

### 4. Breathe at a comfortable pace

If it feels safe, notice one breath as it comes in and goes out. Let the breath be ordinary; there is no need to take a very deep breath or hold it. You could count a few comfortable breaths, then return attention to what is around you. If focusing on breathing feels unsettling, stop and use another grounding option.

## A step-by-step pause

Use this sequence when a feeling or thought is making it hard to decide what to do. It can take less than a minute, or you can spend longer. You do not need to complete every step perfectly.

1. **Pause and orient.** Notice where you are. If you are in immediate danger, move toward safety if you can and contact emergency help.
2. **Name what is happening.** Identify a feeling, a thought, or both. Keep the description simple: “I feel scared” or “I am thinking that I cannot cope.”
3. **Check the thought.** Ask, “What do I know for sure? What might I be missing?” Look for a balanced statement rather than arguing with yourself.
4. **Choose a grounding option.** Try 5-4-3-2-1, notice your feet on the floor, or use comfortable breathing. Stop if the practice does not feel safe or useful.
5. **Choose one next action.** That might be contacting a supportive person, stepping into a safer setting, writing down a question for a clinician, or waiting until you feel steadier before responding to a message.
6. **Review without blame.** Ask what helped even a little and what you might change next time. If the distress keeps returning or affects daily life, consider professional support.

:::example Illustrative example: Maya checks a thought
Maya is in her first months of recovery and receives a message from a family member who is upset. Her first thought is, “I have lost this relationship for good.” She names fear and sadness, then writes down what she knows: the family member is upset and has asked for space. She also notes what she does not know: whether the relationship is over. Maya's balanced thought is, “This is painful, and I do not know what will happen. I can respect the request for space and talk with my counselor about how to respond.” She takes a moment to notice the room before deciding what to do. This is an illustrative example, not a prediction about anyone's relationships.
:::

## Trauma, choice, and support

Trauma is a broad term for experiences that can affect a person's sense of safety and well-being. Experiences of trauma and substance use can occur together, but they do not occur in the same way for everyone. A trauma-informed approach recognizes that trauma can have a wide impact, responds with that knowledge, and works to avoid causing further harm [6]. You do not have to share personal details to deserve respectful support.

You decide whether, when, and with whom to explore trauma. You can ask a provider what a conversation or treatment will involve, set limits, pause, or decline to answer a question. If you want to do deeper trauma work, seek a qualified therapist who can discuss your needs and help you decide what approach is appropriate. Do not pressure yourself to tell your story before you feel ready. A recovery plan can focus on present-day safety and support without exploring past events in detail.

SAMHSA identifies six principles of a trauma-informed approach [6]. You can use them as a guide for the kind of support you want and the choices you can make for yourself.

| Principle | What it can look like in your recovery |
|---|---|
| Safety | Choose settings and people where you feel as safe as possible. Ask for a pause or a different space. |
| Trustworthiness and transparency | Ask what will happen next, what information may be shared, and what choices you have. |
| Peer support | Consider connecting with a peer or mutual-help group if that feels useful and safe. |
| Collaboration and mutuality | Work with helpers as partners. Share what is and is not working for you. |
| Empowerment, voice, and choice | Set the pace, ask questions, and choose what you are ready to discuss. |
| Cultural, historical, and gender issues | Seek support that respects your identity, culture, history, and gender. |

These principles are not a checklist you must satisfy. They can help you notice whether a support relationship respects your voice and whether you want to ask for a change. If a provider or setting does not feel safe, you may ask about other options or talk with someone you trust about next steps.

## When to seek professional help

Consider contacting a qualified mental health professional or health care provider if depression, anxiety, panic, flashbacks, or trouble sleeping persists, feels hard to manage, or interferes with your daily life. A clinician can help assess what support may fit. This guide cannot diagnose a condition or recommend treatment. If you are unsure whether a concern needs professional attention, asking a clinician is a reasonable next step.

Thoughts of suicide need immediate support. Call or text 988 now; the 988 Lifeline is free, confidential, and available 24/7 [3]. If there is immediate danger or a medical emergency, call 911. If you can, tell a trusted person what is happening and ask them to stay with you while you reach help. You do not need to manage a crisis alone.

:::warning If you are having thoughts of suicide
Call or text 988 now, or use the 988 Lifeline chat. If you may act on those thoughts or someone is in immediate danger, call 911. If possible, move near a trusted person and tell them plainly that you need immediate support. You can ask them to help you contact crisis services.
:::

## Finding help

For treatment referrals and information about mental health or substance use disorders, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. It is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day, every day, in English and Spanish. The service can refer individuals and families to local treatment facilities, support groups, and community organizations [4]. You can also use FindTreatment.gov, a confidential and anonymous resource for finding mental health and substance use treatment in the United States and its territories; it is available in Spanish [5].

When you contact a provider, you can ask whether they work with people in recovery, how they approach trauma, what choices you will have, and what to expect at a first visit. You can ask about services that fit your language, culture, schedule, and comfort level. If the first person you contact is not a good fit, asking about another option is appropriate. If you are preparing to leave treatment, jail, or prison, you may want to write down contact information and make a plan for how to reach support before the transition.

# Chapter 9: Relationships, Family and Boundaries

:::objectives
- Explain how relationships can support recovery and how some interactions may raise risk.
- Use practical steps to rebuild trust through steady actions.
- Define boundaries and practice clear ways to set them.
- Consider family support, romantic relationships and amends with attention to safety and choice.
:::

## Relationships can be support and risk

Recovery happens in a life, not apart from it. Relationships can offer connection, practical help, friendship and hope. They can also involve conflict, pressure, or reminders of alcohol or drug use. The same relationship may be supportive in some ways and difficult in others. It is useful to look at what happens in specific situations rather than label a person as simply good or bad for your recovery.

Recovery capital means the personal, social, community and cultural resources a person can draw on to address substance use and strengthen recovery [9]. A trusted relative, a peer who understands recovery, a safe place to stay, or a community group may each be part of that resource base. SAMHSA describes Community as relationships and social networks that offer support, friendship, love and hope [2]. You can build this kind of support over time; it does not have to come from one person.

Some interactions can become high-risk situations. A person might be offered a drink or drug, be invited to a setting where use is common, or feel pulled into an argument that leaves them overwhelmed. Relapse prevention approaches identify high-risk situations and coping responses as factors that can influence whether a lapse occurs [22]. This is not a reason to blame yourself or another person. It is a reason to notice patterns and make a plan that fits your circumstances.

Relationships may change as you do. Some people will understand your choices quickly; others may need time, clear limits, or support of their own. You can care about someone and still choose not to spend time with them in a particular setting. You can also choose to pause a conversation and return to it later. Recovery is person-driven and has many pathways, and choice is part of a trauma-informed approach [1] [6].

## Trust grows through consistent actions

When trust has been hurt, an apology may matter, but it usually cannot do all the work. A person who has been affected may need time to decide what feels safe. They may set limits, ask questions, or choose not to reconnect. Their response is theirs to make. You can control your conduct; you cannot require someone else to trust you on a schedule.

Consistency means doing what you said you would do, being honest when you cannot, and following through on recovery supports that you have chosen. It can also mean accepting a no without arguing, keeping private information private, and not asking a loved one to monitor or manage your recovery. These ordinary actions may seem small, but they make your intentions easier to evaluate over time.

A useful way to think about trust is as a pattern, not a single test. If you miss a commitment, acknowledge it without making excuses, say what you will do next, and then follow through. Avoid asking for immediate praise or reassurance. The other person may notice your effort and still need distance. That does not make the effort pointless.

## Boundaries: clear limits that protect choice

A boundary is a statement about what you will do to protect your safety, time, values or recovery. It is not a demand that another person change. For example, you cannot make someone stop using, but you can say that you will leave if substances are brought into your home. A boundary works best when it is specific, possible for you to carry out, and communicated in a calm and direct way when safe.

You do not have to explain every detail or debate a boundary until the other person agrees. A short statement can be enough. If saying it face to face feels unsafe, you can use a text, ask a trusted person to be present, or choose not to have the conversation. You can skip any exercise in this chapter and return to it later.

### A step-by-step boundary plan

1. **Notice the situation.** Name what is happening without judging yourself. Is there an offer to use, a conversation that becomes threatening, or a pattern that leaves you feeling pressured?
2. **Name what matters.** Identify the need or value involved: safety, recovery time, privacy, respect, or contact with family.
3. **Choose your limit.** Decide what you will and will not take part in. Make it concrete. For example, you may choose to meet in a public place rather than at a home where people are using.
4. **Choose your action.** Plan what you can do if the limit is crossed: end the call, leave with a ride you arranged, or ask a trusted person for help. Do not choose an action that puts you in danger.
5. **Say it briefly.** Use a steady voice and simple words. You do not need to persuade the other person that your boundary is fair.
6. **Follow through and review.** Carry out the action you planned if it is safe. Later, consider whether the boundary worked and whether you want to adjust it with support from a counselor, sponsor or trusted peer.

:::example Illustrative example: A plan for an invitation
Maya is in her first months of recovery. A friend invites her to a gathering where people will be drinking. Maya decides she does not want to attend. She texts, I care about seeing you, but I am not coming to a gathering with alcohol. I would be glad to meet for coffee another day. Her friend is disappointed and sends several messages asking her to reconsider. Maya repeats her limit once, then stops responding for the evening. She plans to talk the situation through with a peer the next day. This illustrative example does not predict how another person will respond; Maya chooses the limit and follow-through that feel safe for her.
:::

Scripts can help when stress makes it hard to find words. Adapt them so they sound like you:

- **To refuse an offer:** I am not using or drinking. Please do not offer again.
- **To leave a risky setting:** I am going to leave now. I will contact you another time.
- **With someone who is still using:** I care about you, but I will not be around substances. We can talk when we are somewhere safer for me.
- **To ask for support:** I am having a hard moment. Could you listen for ten minutes, or help me contact someone from my support plan?
- **To pause conflict:** I want to talk about this, but not while we are upset. I am going to pause and check in later.
- **To protect privacy:** I am not ready to discuss that. I will tell you if I decide I want to talk about it.

You can ask for a specific form of help rather than expecting someone to guess. For example, ask a friend to accompany you to an appointment, check in by phone after a difficult visit, or help you leave a gathering at a planned time. The person may say yes, no, or offer something different. Whenever possible, have more than one support option so that one person is not carrying the whole job.

## Romantic relationships in early recovery

There is no single rule that fits every adult about dating or starting a romantic relationship in early recovery. People have different histories, responsibilities and support systems. Still, it can be helpful to move at a pace that lets you notice how the relationship affects your well-being and recovery routines.

Consider whether you can speak honestly, say no, spend time with other supports, and keep appointments or commitments that matter to you. Notice whether a partner respects your boundaries or pressures you to drink, use, hide things, or withdraw from people who support your recovery. Strong feelings can make it harder to evaluate a situation, so you might choose to talk it through with a counselor, sponsor or trusted peer before making a major decision.

You do not owe anyone a relationship, physical intimacy, or access to private information. You also do not have to end a relationship just because another person tells you that you should. If you feel controlled, threatened, or unsafe, give priority to safety. If it feels safe, make a plan with a trusted person or clinician. In an emergency, call 911. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 [3].

## Family support and different options

Family members and friends may need support whether or not the person they care about is ready for treatment. Al-Anon is a fellowship for relatives and friends of people with drinking problems who share experience to address common concerns; Alateen serves teens [25]. A family member can explore these options for their own support. That choice does not require you to attend or to follow the same path.

Community Reinforcement and Family Training, or CRAFT, is another option for families of people who are not yet in treatment. A review of four trials involving 264 concerned significant others found that CRAFT led to about three times more treatment engagement than Al-Anon or Nar-Anon and about twice as much as the Johnson Institute intervention. About two-thirds of treatment-resistant individuals entered treatment, typically after four to six CRAFT sessions. Family members improved psychosocially across all approaches [26]. These findings describe group results, not a promise for any one family or person. A family can ask a qualified clinician or treatment service whether this approach is available and suitable for them.

If you have a family member who is still using, you can offer information or support without taking responsibility for their choices. You might say, I care about you, and I can help you look for support if you want it. You can also state what you will not do, such as provide money for substances or allow use in your home. If a conversation could become violent or put anyone at risk, do not try to manage it alone. Focus on safety and seek help from a clinician or local emergency services as appropriate.

## Making amends with care

Wanting to repair harm can be an important part of recovery, but contact is not always the safest or kindest choice. An unexpected message or visit may reopen pain, create pressure, or place you at risk. An apology does not create an obligation for the other person to respond, forgive, or resume contact.

Before making amends, discuss the situation with a sponsor or counselor. Consider the other person’s safety and wishes, your own safety, and whether there are domestic violence concerns or legal issues. Do not make contact if doing so could harm someone or put you at risk. In legal situations, get appropriate professional guidance before taking action. You can work on responsibility without contacting someone: for example, by changing your conduct, respecting a request for no contact, or discussing a safer form of repair with your counselor.

Some people may not be available for contact, and some relationships may not be repaired. That can be painful. You can acknowledge harm, learn from it, and keep practicing different actions without demanding a particular outcome. If the other person wants distance, respecting that boundary is itself a meaningful choice.

# Chapter 10: Home, Work and Money

## Stability in daily life

Recovery involves more than substance use. SAMHSA describes four dimensions: Health, Home, Purpose and Community. Home means a stable, safe place to live. Purpose can include work, school, volunteering, family care or creative activity. Community includes relationships that offer support, friendship, love and hope [2].

Here, **recovery housing** means a living arrangement chosen with recovery in mind. It may be a recovery residence, a home shared with trusted people or another place where you can meet your needs and feel as safe as possible. No single arrangement fits everyone; consider safety, affordability, practicality and the support you want. If you are leaving treatment, jail or prison, ask about housing before your departure date. If you lack a safe place, contact a treatment or re-entry professional, peer support person or community organization to explore options.

A residence using the recovery label is not automatically a good fit. Learn what daily life is like, ask questions and review written information. You can return to questions later if you do not feel safe discussing them now.

## A step-by-step stability framework

1. **Name immediate needs.** Note where you can sleep safely, when you need to move, how you will reach work or appointments, and which belongings or documents you need. Separate urgent needs from those that can wait. In crisis, call or text 988. For treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. In an emergency, call 911 [3] [4].

2. **Compare housing options.** Consider a recovery residence, living with family or another trusted person, or independent living if available and workable. Ask how each option handles privacy, visitors, responsibilities, costs and support. Temporary housing can be a bridge, not a permanent plan.

3. **Check details before agreeing.** Ask about rules, fees, drug-free policies, meetings, safety and the written agreement. Request plain-language explanations, time to read the agreement and permission to discuss it with someone you trust. Clarify what happens if you cannot pay on time or need to leave. Do not assume residences share the same rules.

4. **Plan for work and income.** Identify work, training or other meaningful activity you want to pursue, then choose a small next step, such as updating a résumé or contacting a workforce program. Recovery-ready workplaces adopt policies that expand employment for people in or seeking recovery, make it easier to seek help, support treatment and mutual aid, inform employees about possible reasonable accommodations and other protections, and work to reduce substance-misuse risks [27]. Workplace rights depend on the situation. For advice, consult a qualified employment professional or appropriate government office.

5. **Give each dollar a job.** List expected income and essential bills, including food, transportation and phone costs. Add a small amount for unexpected needs if possible. A budget is a plan, not a judgment. If the numbers do not balance, consider a lower-cost option, payment arrangement, added income or community support. Review the plan when circumstances change.

| Fees | What is due and when? | Deposits, late payments |

## Work, opportunity and support

Work can provide income and structure, but purpose can also come from school, caregiving, volunteering or creative work. A job is not proof of recovery, and a period without work does not erase progress.

A recovery-ready workplace takes practical steps to support people in or seeking recovery, such as making it easier to ask for help, access treatment or mutual aid, and learn about possible workplace protections [27]. If you are considering requesting a change at work, identify what is difficult and what might help. You may speak with a supervisor, human resources contact or qualified employment adviser. You choose how much personal information to share; consult a qualified source about disclosure or legal protections.

Some employers may have a financial incentive to consider applicants with particular backgrounds. The Work Opportunity Tax Credit is a federal tax credit for employers who hire people certified as members of one of 10 targeted groups. These include qualified ex-felons, qualified veterans, SNAP recipients, vocational rehabilitation referrals, SSI recipients and people experiencing long-term unemployment. State workforce agencies handle certification [31]. It is an employer tax credit, not a promise of a job. If you have a record, ask an employment counselor or state workforce agency whether it applies to your situation.

# Chapter 11: Purpose and the Long Game

:::objectives
- Explain purpose as one part of recovery, not a demand to find one perfect calling.
- Identify values and turn them into small, practical actions.
- Use small wins and if-then plans to build confidence and follow-through.
- Review recovery resources at 12 months and choose a next step.
:::

## Purpose is a direction, not a finish line

Purpose in recovery need not mean a career, grand mission, or final answer to “What should I do with my life?” It can begin with ordinary commitments: caring for a child, attending class, making something, helping a neighbor, or keeping a promise to yourself. A quiet day can still be meaningful.

SAMHSA describes Purpose as meaningful daily activities, including work, school, volunteering, family caretaking, and creative work, along with the independence, income, and resources that help a person participate in society [2]. Purpose is one of four major dimensions of recovery, alongside Health, Home, and Community [2]. The dimensions affect one another: stable housing may make school attendance easier, while supportive relationships can help you keep commitments. You can work on one small purpose goal even if other areas remain unsettled.

Recovery is a process of change through which people improve health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and strive to reach their full potential [1]. It is person-driven, follows many pathways, and can include setbacks. People can choose supports that fit their needs and values [1]. The first year is not a pass-or-fail test; it is a chance to learn what supports the life you want to build.

Here, values are qualities you want your choices to express, such as care, honesty, learning, creativity, fairness, or dependability. A value differs from a goal: learning is a value; completing one course is a goal that may express it. Goals can be reached or revised, while values can guide your next choice.

If reflection feels unsafe or overwhelming, skip a prompt, return later, or consider working with a trusted person or clinician. This guide is educational, not a substitute for treatment. Discuss medical concerns with a doctor or clinician.

## Build purpose through small actions

A long-range hope can give direction; a small action gives you a place to begin. During transitions from treatment, jail, or prison, or while housing, work, family responsibilities, or health concerns are changing, a useful first goal may be to learn about a program, make one call, or establish a weekly routine. It need not solve everything at once.

Self-efficacy is a belief in your capacity to carry out behaviors needed for a specific outcome. People with higher self-efficacy tend to set bigger goals, persevere, and recover after setbacks; lower self-efficacy can contribute to avoiding difficult tasks and feeling discouraged [23]. It is not a fixed trait or a measure of worth. Choose an action small enough to try, notice what you did, and build from there.

An implementation intention is an if-then plan: “If situation Y occurs, then I will do X.” A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests involving more than 8,000 participants found a medium-to-large positive effect on goal attainment beyond goal intentions alone [24]. It cannot guarantee an outcome, but it makes the next action more specific before a busy or difficult moment arrives.

Giving back can be part of purpose, but it is a choice, not an obligation. SAMHSA includes peer support among recovery principles and notes that helping others can strengthen one’s own recovery [1]. Peer support may mean listening, sharing a resource, or joining a group. Respect your limits and continue to receive support yourself.

A Recovery Café is one example of a community organized around participation. Its membership model includes being drug- and alcohol-free in the space, joining a weekly Recovery Circle, and contributing to the Café. It describes “loving accountability” and “radical hospitality” [17]. This model is not required or right for everyone. Mutual help, therapy, medication, faith, secular support, family, and other pathways may all have a place in recovery [1].

## A five-step purpose framework

Use these steps flexibly. Repeat them, change the order, or ask someone you trust to work through them with you.

1. **Notice what matters.** Name two or three values you want your choices to express. Ask, “When have I felt useful, connected, curious, or proud of how I treated someone?” Skip any memory that does not feel safe to revisit.

2. **Choose one life area.** Consider work or learning, family care, creativity, service, daily structure, or connection. Choose what matters now, not what sounds most impressive to others.

3. **Set a 90-day direction.** Make progress recognizable, while keeping the goal adjustable. For example: “Over the next 90 days, I will explore whether a training course fits my schedule.” Information, a routine, or a clearer decision can be meaningful results.

4. **Make the first action small and plan for it.** Decide what, when, and where. For example: “If it is Tuesday after breakfast, I will spend 15 minutes finding the course contact information.” If the plan depends on someone or something outside your control, identify an alternative action.

5. **Review, recognize, and revise.** Each week, note what you tried, what helped, and what got in the way. Treat effort and learning as information, not failure. Make an ill-fitting plan smaller or choose another route. If you return to use, reconnect with support and review the plan rather than abandoning your sense of purpose. Discuss treatment changes with your clinician.

## A letter to the reader at day 365

You have reached a point on the calendar, not the end of your story. You may feel proud, tired, uncertain, or several things at once; there is no required response. Notice what you have learned about the conditions, people, and choices that help you move toward the life you want.

# Glossary

**Abstinence violation effect:** In a relapse-prevention model, guilt or a sense of failure after a lapse may raise the likelihood of further use for some people. A lapse can instead prompt reconnection with support. [22]

**Community:** Supportive relationships and networks that offer friendship, love, support, and hope. [2]

**Coping response:** An action for managing a difficult situation, feeling, or urge. Effective coping can build confidence in responding. [22]

**Craving:** An urge or desire to use a substance, considered in planning support and coping responses. [22]

**CRAFT:** Community Reinforcement and Family Training, an approach for concerned significant others. A review found greater treatment engagement with CRAFT than comparison approaches in the studies reviewed. [26]

**FindTreatment.gov:** Confidential, anonymous resource for finding mental and substance use disorder treatment in the United States and territories; available in Spanish. [5]

**HALT check:** A personal check-in for noting what you notice or what support you may need. This tracker does not assign meaning to the letters.

**High-risk situation:** A situation that may increase return-to-use risk or make coping harder. Relapse-prevention planning considers situations, coping skills, expected outcomes, urges, and lifestyle balance. [22]

**Implementation intention:** A when-then plan naming a situation and response: “If situation Y occurs, then I will do X.” [24]

**Lapse:** Return to use after a period without use or of recovery progress. It can signal a need to renew or adjust support or treatment; it does not mean treatment has failed. [7]

**Medication for addiction treatment:** FDA-approved medications combined with counseling and behavioral therapies as a whole-patient approach to substance use disorders. Discuss treatment or medication with a doctor or clinician. [18]

**Mutual help:** Support among people sharing experience and recovery goals; one of several recovery pathways. [1]

**Naloxone:** Medicine that can rapidly reverse opioid overdose effects when given in time. It is available as prefilled nasal spray and injectable form usable without medical training. Call 911 in an overdose emergency. [15] [14]

**Peer support:** Support from people with relevant lived experience; one recovery pathway and part of supportive relationships. [1]

**Purpose:** Meaningful activity, such as work, school, volunteering, caregiving, or creative work, and the resources and independence to participate in society. [2]

**Recovery:** A process of change through which people improve health and wellness, live self-directed lives, and work toward their potential. It can follow many pathways and include setbacks. [1]

**Recovery capital:** Personal and social resources that address substance dependence and strengthen recovery capacity and opportunity, including personal, social, community, and cultural resources. [9]

**Recovery residence:** A place to live while pursuing recovery. Oxford Houses are self-run, self-supported homes for people in recovery from substance use disorder. [28]

**Relapse:** Return to substance use after progress or a period without use. It does not prove treatment failed; support or treatment may need renewal or adjustment. [7]

**Self-efficacy:** Belief in one’s ability to carry out actions toward a specific outcome. Confidence can support persistence and recovery from setbacks. [23]

**Sponsor:** A recovery support contact. Roles differ across groups and pathways; ask what support the person or group offers.

**SMART Recovery:** Secular mutual support grounded in cognitive behavioral therapy. Its four points address motivation, urges, thoughts and behavior, and balanced living. [19]

**Tolerance:** Reduced ability to tolerate a previously used opioid amount after a period without use. Returning to that amount after tolerance drops raises overdose and death risk. [12]

**Trauma-informed:** An approach that recognizes trauma’s possible effects, responds with understanding, and seeks to avoid re-traumatization. Principles include safety, trust, peer support, collaboration, choice, and attention to cultural, historical, and gender issues. [6]

**Urge surfing:** Staying with an urge as it rises and peaks, using the breath and allowing time for it to subside without acting. [13]

**Withdrawal:** Symptoms that may follow stopping or reducing substance use. If alcohol withdrawal may be a concern, consult a health care provider; severe withdrawal can be dangerous. [10]

**Health:** Managing disease or symptoms and making informed, healthy choices, a dimension of recovery. [2]

**Home:** A stable, safe place to live, a major dimension of recovery. [2]
