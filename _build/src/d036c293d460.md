# How to Use This Book

Real estate is a referral and relationship business conducted under unusually detailed rules. Agents receive inquiries from portals, websites, signs, open houses, and friends of past clients, and the way those inquiries are handled in the first hour, the first week, and the following months often determines who is hired. At the same time, the tools agents use to follow up, including phones, texts, emails, dialers, and increasingly artificial intelligence, are governed by federal telemarketing law, the Fair Housing Act, commercial email law, state law, and brokerage policy.

This book combines a textbook and a template library. The textbook chapters explain why follow-up matters, what research says about response speed, how consent and contact rules work, and how to design a pipeline that a solo agent or small team can actually run. Each chapter then supplies scripts and tools. The appendices collect a complete 30-day follow-up calendar, scripts for every common situation, and printable trackers. It is written for licensed agents, team leads, inside sales associates, transaction and marketing coordinators, and brokers who supervise them.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this book you should be able to:

- Explain, using published survey data, why responsiveness and relationships drive agent selection.
- Respond to new inquiries quickly and helpfully, with a reply that earns a conversation.
- Distinguish permitted from prohibited contact under federal telemarketing and robocall rules.
- Run a simple eight-stage pipeline with a 15-minute daily routine.
- Use buyer, seller, showing, and open-house scripts that are respectful and specific.
- Write texts and emails that comply with opt-out and commercial email rules.
- Apply Fair Housing principles to every message and advertisement.
- Use automation and AI tools without violating consent rules.
- Nurture long-term leads and past clients without becoming a nuisance.
- Protect client data and warn clients about wire fraud.
- Measure follow-up with a weekly scorecard.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter begins with learning objectives, explains its subject, and provides a step-by-step framework, scripts or tools, a worked example, a real case study with sources, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Worked examples are illustrative and hypothetical; the agents and clients in them are invented. Case studies and statistics come from public sources listed in the References chapter. Where a real company is discussed, its results and circumstances are its own, not TTI's.

# Chapter 1: Why Follow-Up Decides Who Gets Hired

:::objectives
- Describe how buyers and sellers find and choose agents, using national survey data.
- Explain why most clients interview only one agent and what that means for follow-up.
- Identify the three horizons of follow-up: immediate, active, and long-term.
- Set a personal follow-up standard.
:::

## The decision is often made early

Many agents imagine that consumers compare several agents before choosing. National survey data suggest otherwise. The National Association of Realtors' 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers, based on a mailed survey of recent buyers with 6,103 responses, found that 88 percent of buyers purchased through an agent or broker, and 91 percent would use their agent again or recommend them [1]. The same report found that 43 percent of buyers found their agent through a referral from a friend, neighbor, or relative, and that most buyers interviewed only one agent: 67 percent of first-time buyers and 76 percent of repeat buyers [2]. Among sellers, 80 percent contacted only one agent before finding the one they worked with [2].

These figures carry a sobering implication. When a consumer reaches out, they are frequently reaching out to the one agent they intend to evaluate. If that agent responds slowly, vaguely, or not at all, the consumer may simply move to the next name, and the first agent never knows a decision was made.

## Three horizons of follow-up

| Horizon | Time frame | Goal | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|---|
| Immediate | First minutes to first day | Earn a real conversation | Call, personal text, personal email |
| Active | First 30 days | Understand needs; set an appointment | Scheduled touches, showings, market data |
| Long-term | Months to years | Stay helpful until the client is ready; earn referrals | Market updates, check-ins, past-client care |

A follow-up system must serve all three horizons. Agents often do well at one and neglect the others: some respond quickly but stop after two attempts; others send monthly newsletters but take a day to answer a new inquiry.

:::case What national survey data say about agent selection
NAR's 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers reports that referrals from friends, neighbors, or relatives were the most common way buyers found their agent (43 percent), followed by using an agent they had worked with before (15 percent), with smaller shares coming from inquiries about a specific property seen online (7 percent), referrals from another agent (7 percent), websites (6 percent), and open houses (5 percent) [2]. The report also found that 66 percent of recent sellers used an agent who was referred to them or with whom they had worked before [2]. NAR's press release notes that the survey was mailed to 173,250 recent buyers in July 2025 and had an adjusted response rate of 3.5 percent [1]. NAR's results are its own, not TTI's. These are survey results describing the market as a whole; they do not predict any individual agent's business. They do show that both fast response to new inquiries and long-term care of relationships matter, because a large share of business flows through past clients and their networks.
:::

## Setting your follow-up standard

A follow-up standard is a written promise to yourself and your clients about how you will respond. It should be specific enough to measure. For example: "Every permitted new inquiry receives a personal response within 15 minutes during working hours and by 8:30 a.m. the next morning outside them. Every active lead receives a scheduled touch at least twice a week for 30 days. Every past client hears from us at least four times a year with something useful."

## The follow-up foundation framework

**Step 1: List your lead sources** and estimate how many inquiries each produces per month.

**Step 2: Define your working hours** and how after-hours inquiries will be handled.

**Step 3: Write a follow-up standard** for each horizon.

**Step 4: Choose one system of record**, whether a CRM or a simple spreadsheet, so that no lead lives only in someone's phone.

**Step 5: Confirm your broker's policies** on calling, texting, scripts, and advertising.

**Step 6: Review the standard monthly** against actual performance.

:::example Illustrative: an agent's lead sources
A hypothetical solo agent reviews the past six months. She received 14 inquiries from past clients and their referrals, 31 from a listing portal, 9 from her website, 22 sign-ins at open houses, and 4 from sign calls. Her CRM shows that past-client referrals produced 5 closings, portal inquiries 2, open houses 1, and her website none. She also finds that 11 portal inquiries waited more than four hours for a first response. Her new standard prioritizes a 15-minute response to all new inquiries and a quarterly past-client touch plan. Figures are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 2: Speed and the First Reply

:::objectives
- Summarize published research on lead response time.
- Write a first reply that is fast, personal, and useful.
- Design an after-hours response plan that respects consent rules.
- Avoid common first-reply mistakes.
:::

## What the research shows

The most widely cited study of response speed appeared in Harvard Business Review in 2011. Researchers James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington audited 2,241 U.S. companies by submitting a web inquiry to each; 37 percent responded within an hour, 16 percent within one to 24 hours, 24 percent took more than 24 hours, and 23 percent never responded, with an average response time of 42 hours among companies that responded within 30 days [3]. In a separate analysis of 1.25 million leads at 42 companies, firms that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it, defined as having a meaningful conversation with a decision maker, as firms that tried even an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited 24 hours or longer [3].

The study was not specific to real estate, and its findings are correlations across many industries, not guarantees. Still, its direction is consistent with ordinary experience: a consumer who has just asked about a home is thinking about it now.

:::case The Harvard Business Review lead-response audit
The 2011 HBR study is instructive less for its exact multipliers than for what it revealed about ordinary practice: nearly a quarter of companies never answered a web inquiry at all, and the average responder took almost two days [3]. For an agent, this suggests that simply responding promptly and personally can be a meaningful point of difference. The study's authors measured attempts to contact and qualification, not closed sales, and their data are from 2011; readers should treat the findings as directional. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's.
:::

## Anatomy of a strong first reply

A strong first reply does four things. It confirms who you are and how you got the person's information. It answers or acknowledges their specific question. It offers one helpful next step. And it makes declining easy.

| Element | Weak version | Strong version |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | "Hi!" | "Hi Maria, this is Dana Lee with Lakeview Realty." |
| Context | "Saw you were looking." | "You asked about 412 Oak Street on our website a few minutes ago." |
| Answer | "Lots of great homes!" | "It's still available; there's a showing slot Saturday at 11." |
| Next step | "Call me!" | "Would Saturday work, or would you prefer a different time?" |
| Easy exit | (none) | "If you were just browsing, no problem; I'm happy to answer questions anytime." |

## First-reply scripts

**Portal or website inquiry, text (where the consumer provided their number and requested contact):**
"Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Brokerage]. You asked about [address] a few minutes ago. It's [status]. Would you like the disclosures and recent sales nearby, or to set up a showing? Reply STOP to opt out."

**Portal or website inquiry, phone call:**
"Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Brokerage]. You just asked about [address] on [site], so I wanted to answer quickly. Is now an okay moment for two minutes? ... Great. What caught your eye about this one?"

**Voicemail:**
"Hi [Name], it's [Your Name] with [Brokerage] returning your question about [address]. It's [status]. I'll text you the details so you have them. My number is [number]. Thanks."

**Email:**
Subject: Your question about [address]
"Hi [Name], thanks for asking about [address]. Here are the details you requested: [status, price, key facts]. I've also attached [disclosures / recent nearby sales]. If you'd like to see it, I have [two specific times]. If you were just gathering information, that's completely fine; you're welcome to reply with any questions. [Signature with brokerage, license number if required, and physical address]."

## After-hours responses

Consumers inquire at night and on weekends. Options include a personal reply first thing in the morning, a teammate or inside sales associate on call, or an automated acknowledgment. Any automated text or call must meet the consent requirements discussed in Chapter 3, and federal telemarketing rules generally restrict telemarketing calls to between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. local time at the consumer's location [4]. An automated acknowledgment should be honest: it should not pretend to be a person, and it should state when a person will follow up.

## The first-reply framework

**Step 1: Confirm permission**: the consumer requested contact through the channel you plan to use.

**Step 2: Respond within your standard**, by phone first if the consumer provided a number and requested a call.

**Step 3: Answer their specific question** before asking yours.

**Step 4: Offer one concrete next step** with specific times.

**Step 5: Log the contact** in your system of record immediately.

**Step 6: Schedule the next touch** according to the 30-day calendar (Appendix A).

:::example Illustrative: measuring response time
A hypothetical two-agent team measures its response times for one month. Of 48 new inquiries, the median first response was 2 hours 10 minutes, and 9 inquiries received no response for more than a day. The team adopts a rotating "first responder" schedule and saved reply templates. The following month, the median falls to 11 minutes and every inquiry receives a response the same day. Conversations with inquirers rise from 17 to 26. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist First reply
- Permission confirmed for the channel used.
- Identity and brokerage stated.
- Consumer's specific question answered.
- One concrete next step offered.
- Easy way to decline or opt out included.
- Contact logged immediately.
- Next touch scheduled.
:::

:::summary
Research published in Harvard Business Review found that many companies respond slowly or not at all to web inquiries and that faster attempts were associated with much higher odds of a meaningful conversation. A strong first reply states who you are, answers the specific question, offers one next step, and makes declining easy. After-hours plans must be honest and comply with consent and calling-hour rules.
:::

# Chapter 3: Permission, Consent, and Contact Law

:::objectives
- Explain the National Do Not Call Registry and the Telemarketing Sales Rule as they apply to agents.
- Describe consent requirements for autodialed, prerecorded, and artificial-voice calls and texts.
- Apply the FCC's rules on revoking consent.
- Learn from a major real estate telemarketing settlement.
:::

## Why this chapter matters

Real estate agents are frequent targets of telemarketing lawsuits because prospecting traditionally involves calling homeowners, including owners of expired listings and for-sale-by-owner properties. Federal law provides statutory damages per call, so a single campaign can create large liability. This chapter summarizes key federal rules. It is not legal advice; state laws can be stricter, and brokerages often have their own policies.

## The Do Not Call Registry and the Telemarketing Sales Rule

The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule (TSR) generally prohibits telemarketing calls to numbers on the National Do Not Call Registry unless an exemption applies, such as an established business relationship or the consumer's express written permission [4]. Under the TSR, an established business relationship exists for 18 months after a consumer's last purchase or transaction and for three months after a consumer's inquiry or application [4]. Sellers and telemarketers must download the registry and remove registered numbers at least every 31 days, must honor company-specific do-not-call requests, and may call only between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. local time [4]. The FTC states that violations can bring civil penalties of up to $53,088 per call [4]. The FTC's consumer guidance explains that registration does not expire and that the registry restricts sales calls but not, for example, calls from political groups, charities, or surveys [5].

## Autodialers, prerecorded and artificial voices, and texts

Separate rules under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), administered by the FCC, restrict calls made with an automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice. In a February 2024 announcement, the FCC explained that the TCPA requires telemarketers to obtain prior express written consent from consumers before robocalling them, and confirmed that AI-generated voices are "artificial" under the law [7]. Texts are treated as calls for many TCPA purposes. Agents who use dialers, ringless voicemail, prerecorded messages, mass texting platforms, or AI voice tools should obtain written guidance from their broker and an attorney.

## Revoking consent

Consumers may withdraw consent. The FCC's 2024 rules, as summarized by HousingWire, treat replies such as "stop" to a text, use of an automated opt-out mechanism, or a request through a website or phone number the caller provides as reasonable ways to revoke consent; require callers to honor do-not-call and revocation requests within a reasonable time not exceeding 10 business days; and permit a single confirmation text that contains no marketing [8]. The rules took effect April 11, 2025, but the FCC delayed until April 11, 2026, the requirement that a revocation in response to one type of message apply to all future robocalls and robotexts from that caller on unrelated matters [9].

| Situation | General federal rule | Practical safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer submitted a web inquiry asking for contact | Inquiry creates a 3-month established business relationship under the TSR [4] | Log date and source of inquiry |
| Past client who closed with you | 18-month relationship after the transaction [4] | Track closing dates; seek ongoing consent |
| Number on National DNC, no relationship or consent | Telemarketing calls generally prohibited [4] | Scrub lists at least every 31 days |
| Prerecorded or AI-voice message | Prior express consent required; written consent for telemarketing [6][7] | Avoid unless consent is documented |
| Consumer replies "STOP" | Treat as revocation; honor within 10 business days [8] | Honor immediately; send at most one non-marketing confirmation |
| Calling hours | 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. consumer's local time [4] | Configure tools by consumer time zone |

:::case Keller Williams and a $40 million telemarketing settlement
In January 2023, Inman reported that Keller Williams Realty agreed to pay $40 million to settle a class action alleging that agents affiliated with its franchisees made unsolicited prerecorded calls without consent, including calls to numbers on the National Do Not Call Registry [10]. According to Inman, the class included people who received calls or texts between 2014 and 2022, about 2 million people might be eligible for payments of up to $20 each, Keller Williams denied wrongdoing, and the company agreed to create a TCPA compliance task force and to provide additional TCPA and Do Not Call materials to franchisees [10]. The law firm Manatt reported that the court granted final approval and that the complaint alleged the franchisor provided a marketing plan to affiliated agents that included unsolicited telemarketing [11]. Keller Williams' results are its own, not TTI's; the settlement involved no admission of liability. The case shows that telemarketing exposure can reach brokerages and franchisors, not only individual agents.
:::

## Lessons from the case

The allegations centered on familiar prospecting practices: prerecorded messages and calls to registered numbers. The remedy emphasized training and compliance systems. For agents and teams, the practical lessons are to prospect only with permission or a valid exemption, to avoid prerecorded and artificial-voice messages without documented consent, to scrub lists, to honor opt-outs promptly, and to keep records.

## The permission framework

**Step 1: Record the source and date** of every contact's permission or relationship.

**Step 2: Scrub** calling lists against the National Registry and your internal do-not-call list at least every 31 days.

**Step 3: Separate** manual, one-to-one calls and texts from automated or prerecorded tools, and apply stricter consent rules to the latter.

**Step 4: Include** a clear opt-out in marketing texts and emails.

**Step 5: Honor** opt-outs immediately, across channels.

**Step 6: Review** your practices with your broker and an attorney at least annually.

:::example Illustrative: auditing a calling list
A hypothetical team prepares to call 600 homeowners from a purchased list. Its audit finds that 412 numbers are on the National Registry, 37 appear on the team's internal do-not-call list, and the remaining 151 have no relationship or consent on record. The team decides not to call the list at all and instead invests in an open-house and past-client program where permission is documented. The audit is illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Contact compliance
- Permission source and date recorded for every contact.
- Lists scrubbed against the National Registry at least every 31 days.
- Internal do-not-call list maintained and honored.
- No prerecorded, artificial, or AI-voice calls without documented consent.
- Calling hours set by the consumer's time zone.
- Opt-outs honored immediately across channels.
- Broker and attorney review completed this year.
:::

:::summary
The Telemarketing Sales Rule restricts sales calls to numbers on the National Do Not Call Registry, with exemptions for established relationships (18 months after a transaction, three months after an inquiry) and written permission, and sets calling hours and list-scrubbing duties. The TCPA requires consent for autodialed and prerecorded or artificial-voice calls, including AI voices. Consumers can revoke consent by reasonable means. The Keller Williams settlement shows the scale of potential liability.
:::

# Chapter 4: Pipeline Stages and the Daily Routine

:::objectives
- Define eight pipeline stages that apply to buyers and sellers.
- Run a 15-minute daily follow-up routine.
- Document the routine so others can follow it.
- Use a simple tracker or CRM consistently.
:::

## Why stages matter

A pipeline organizes leads by where they are in the decision process. Stages make it obvious what to do next for each person and prevent leads from being forgotten. They also allow measurement: how many people move from one stage to the next, and how long they stay.

## Eight stages

| Stage | Definition | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| 1. New | Inquiry received; no conversation yet | First reply within standard |
| 2. Attempting | Reply sent; no response yet | Follow the Day 0–7 cadence |
| 3. Connected | Two-way conversation occurred | Clarify needs and timeline |
| 4. Nurture | Not ready within 90 days | Long-term touch plan |
| 5. Appointment set | Buyer consultation or listing appointment scheduled | Confirm and prepare |
| 6. Active | Under agreement to represent; showing or listing | Service plan |
| 7. Under contract | Purchase agreement signed | Transaction communication |
| 8. Closed or archived | Closed, or no longer a prospect (with reason) | Past-client plan or archive |

Every lead should be in exactly one stage, with a next action and a date.

## The 15-minute daily routine

1. **New (3 minutes):** respond to any inquiry that has not received a first reply.
2. **Attempting (3 minutes):** complete today's scheduled attempts from the 30-day calendar.
3. **Connected and appointments (4 minutes):** confirm upcoming appointments; send promised information.
4. **Active and under contract (3 minutes):** send updates clients are waiting for.
5. **Nurture and past clients (2 minutes):** send one or two personal, useful messages.

Fifteen minutes will not complete every task, but it ensures that each stage receives attention every day. Larger tasks are scheduled separately.

:::case Documenting routines: Dan Martell's "Camcorder Method"
Software entrepreneur and author Dan Martell has described a practice he calls the Camcorder Method for handing work to others: record yourself performing a task, then have the person taking it over turn the recording into a written playbook they can follow and improve [16]. In the same interview, Martell described a "1-3-1" rule for team communication, in which a team member brings one problem, three possible solutions, and one recommendation [16]. Martell's results are his own, not TTI's. For a real estate team, the method suggests a practical way to document follow-up routines: record the lead agent's daily 15-minute routine on screen, then have an assistant write the checklist that others will use.
:::

## The pipeline framework

**Step 1: Choose your system of record** and create the eight stages.

**Step 2: Move every current lead** into exactly one stage with a next action and date.

**Step 3: Block 15 minutes** at the same time every working day.

**Step 4: Record** yourself completing the routine once, and turn it into a written checklist.

**Step 5: Review** stage counts weekly.

**Step 6: Archive** leads with a reason, rather than deleting them.

:::example Illustrative: cleaning up a pipeline
A hypothetical agent exports 340 contacts from her phone and email into a spreadsheet. She finds 22 people who inquired in the past 30 days but never received a second touch, 61 past clients with no contact in over a year, and 118 contacts with no permission source recorded. She stages each contact, schedules the 22 for follow-up, plans a past-client touch for the 61, and marks the 118 "do not market until permission is confirmed." Figures are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 5: Buyer Follow-Up

:::objectives
- Identify what buyers value from an agent, using survey data.
- Conduct a needs conversation that clarifies timeline, financing, and priorities.
- Use buyer follow-up scripts for the first 30 days.
- Handle common buyer objections respectfully.
:::

## What buyers value

Buyers come to agents for help finding the right home, negotiating, and managing paperwork. NAR's coverage of its 2025 Profile reported that buyers primarily sought help finding the right home, negotiating terms, and navigating paperwork; that more than half valued their agent pointing out property features or flaws they had not noticed; and that 76 percent of first-time buyers credited their agent with helping them understand the process [17]. Follow-up that educates, rather than merely checks in, aligns with these values.

## The buyer needs conversation

A needs conversation is a structured, friendly interview. Its goals are to understand the buyer's motivation, timeline, financing, must-haves, and concerns, and to agree on a next step. Typical questions include:

- "What's prompting the move?"
- "Ideally, when would you like to be in your new home?"
- "Have you spoken with a lender yet? Would an introduction to a few options be helpful?"
- "What are the three things your next home must have?"
- "What would make you walk away from a house you otherwise liked?"
- "Who else will be part of the decision?"
- "What has your experience been with agents so far?"

Questions about neighborhoods must be handled carefully under the Fair Housing Act (Chapter 9). Agents should describe properties and amenities, and direct buyers to objective sources for schools, crime statistics, and demographics, rather than characterizing neighborhoods by who lives there.

## Buyer follow-up scripts

**Day 1, after a first conversation (text):**
"Thanks for the time today, [Name]. As promised, here are three homes that match [criteria]. Any you'd like to see this week?"

**Day 3, education (email):**
Subject: How the buying process works in [area]
"Hi [Name], a few people asked me this week what happens between offer and closing, so I wrote a one-page timeline. It's attached. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau also has a good guide to closing. Happy to walk through either."

**Day 7, new listing (text):**
"A new listing just came up on [street] with [feature you mentioned]. Want the details?"

**Day 14, check-in (call):**
"Hi [Name], I wanted to check in. Has anything changed with your timeline or what you're looking for? ... Would it help to tour two or three homes this weekend, just to calibrate?"

**Day 30, permission check (email or text):**
"Hi [Name], I don't want to fill your inbox if the timing has changed. Would you like me to keep sending homes, switch to a monthly market update, or pause for now? Any answer is fine."

## Common buyer objections

| Objection | Respectful response |
|---|---|
| "We're just looking." | "That makes sense. Would a monthly update on what's selling be useful, or would you prefer I wait to hear from you?" |
| "We'll work with the listing agent." | "That's your choice. If it helps, I can explain how representation works here so you can decide what's best." |
| "We're not ready to talk to a lender." | "No problem. Many buyers find it useful to know their range early; I can share a few names whenever you're ready." |
| "We already have an agent." | "Thanks for letting me know. I'll take you off my list. Best of luck with the search." |

Note that buyer representation agreements, compensation, and disclosure requirements vary by state and brokerage and changed significantly in many markets in 2024. Agents should follow current brokerage forms and policies.

:::case What buyers say they value, from the 2025 NAR Profile
According to NAR, 88 percent of buyers in its 2025 survey purchased through an agent or broker, which NAR described as making agents the most trusted and frequently used information source, ahead of online listings [17]. More than half of buyers said they valued their agent pointing out property features or flaws they had not noticed, and 76 percent of first-time buyers credited their agent with helping them understand the process [17]. These survey results describe buyers in aggregate and are NAR's findings, not TTI's. They suggest that follow-up content which explains the process and evaluates properties honestly is aligned with what buyers report valuing.
:::

## The buyer follow-up framework

**Step 1: Hold a needs conversation** within 48 hours of first contact.

**Step 2: Summarize** the buyer's criteria in writing and confirm it.

**Step 3: Send homes and education** on the 30-day calendar.

**Step 4: Offer showings** with specific times.

**Step 5: Check permission** at Day 30 and adjust frequency.

**Step 6: Move to nurture** if the buyer's timeline exceeds 90 days.

:::example Illustrative: a buyer timeline shift
A hypothetical couple inquires about a condominium, then explains in the needs conversation that their lease ends in eight months. The agent sends a short process guide and two listings, then at Day 30 asks whether they prefer a monthly update. They choose monthly. In month six, they reply to a market update asking to tour three homes, and the agent schedules showings. The example is illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Buyer follow-up
- Needs conversation held within 48 hours.
- Written criteria summary confirmed.
- Lender introduction offered, with several options.
- Education piece sent in week one.

# Chapter 6: Seller Follow-Up

:::objectives

- Prepare for a listing conversation with useful data.

- Follow up after a listing appointment without pressure.

## How sellers choose

NAR's 2025 Profile found that 66 percent of recent sellers used an agent who was referred to them or with whom they had worked before, and 80 percent contacted only one agent [2]. Seller follow-up therefore depends heavily on relationships built long before the listing appointment and on making a strong impression in the first conversation.

## Seller conversations

Seller leads often begin with a valuation request or a question about the market. Sellers want to know what their home might sell for, what preparation would be worth doing, how long a sale might take, and what it will cost. Helpful follow-up answers these questions honestly. Automated valuation estimates can be far from actual sale prices; agents should explain the difference between an online estimate and a comparative market analysis prepared by a licensee.

## Seller follow-up scripts

**Valuation request, first reply (call or text where permitted):**
"Hi [Name], this is [Your Name] with [Brokerage]. You asked for a value estimate on [address]. Online estimates can vary a lot, so I'd like to prepare a comparative market analysis with recent nearby sales. Could I ask a few questions about the home's updates? It takes about five minutes."

**Day 2, CMA delivery (email):**
Subject: Recent sales near [address]
"Hi [Name], attached is a summary of [number] recent sales near you, with notes on how they compare to your home. Based on these, a likely price range is [range], though condition and timing matter. I'd be glad to walk through it in person or by video."

**Day 10, preparation tips (email):**
"Here's a short list of the preparation steps that most often matter in our area, and a few that usually aren't worth the cost."

**After a listing appointment (handwritten note and email same day):**
"Thank you for showing me your home today. I've attached the marketing plan and net-proceeds estimate we discussed. Please take whatever time you need; I'm happy to answer questions."

**Day 7 after appointment, no decision (call):**
"Hi [Name], I wanted to see whether any questions came up as you thought it over. ... Is there anything I could clarify that would help you decide, whether or not you choose us?"

**Owner who decides not to sell now (text or email):**
"Thanks for letting me know. Would a short market update twice a year be useful, so you have current numbers when the time is right?"

## Expired listings and for-sale-by-owner contacts

Calling owners of expired listings and for-sale-by-owner properties is common in the industry and carries the telemarketing risks described in Chapter 3. Agents should scrub numbers against the National Registry and internal lists, avoid prerecorded messages, observe calling hours, and consider permission-based alternatives such as mailed letters with a clear invitation to call.

NAR's 2025 Profile found that 5 percent of buyers met their agent at an open house [2]. That is a modest share, but open houses also generate neighbor conversations, future seller relationships, and feedback for the listing. Their value depends largely on follow-up.

An open-house sign-in sheet or digital form should explain how contact information will be used and allow visitors to choose. A simple approach includes checkboxes: "Send me information about this home," "Send me similar listings," and "I'm working with an agent: ___." Never require contact information as a condition of entry if your brokerage or state rules prohibit it, and always offer a safe, respectful experience.

# Chapter 8: Texting and Email That Respect Opt-Outs

:::objectives
- Distinguish personal, transactional, and marketing messages.
- Apply CAN-SPAM requirements to marketing email.
- Design texts that honor revocation of consent.
- Write concise, useful messages.
:::

## Three kinds of messages

Messages fall into three broad categories. Personal one-to-one messages respond to a specific person's request. Transactional or relationship messages relate to an existing transaction, such as closing reminders. Marketing messages promote services or listings to people generally. Different rules may apply to each, and agents should treat marketing messages with the most care.

## Commercial email under CAN-SPAM

The FTC's CAN-SPAM guidance explains that the law covers any email whose primary purpose is commercial advertisement or promotion, makes no exception for business-to-business email, and imposes penalties of up to $53,088 per violating email [14]. Its requirements include accurate header information, non-deceptive subject lines, identifying the message as an ad, including a valid physical postal address, providing a clear way to opt out, honoring opt-outs within 10 business days, and monitoring others who send email on your behalf [14]. Messages whose primary purpose is facilitating an agreed transaction are largely exempt from most provisions but may not contain false or misleading routing information [14].

## Texting and revocation

Texting is personal and immediate, which is why it works and why it must be used carefully. Under the FCC's 2024 rules, as summarized by HousingWire, a reply of "stop" or a similar standard response to a text is a reasonable means of revoking consent, requests must be honored within no more than 10 business days, and a single confirmation text without marketing content is permitted [8]. Best practice is to honor the request immediately and across all channels for marketing, even before every part of the rule is in force [9].

## Message design principles

1. **Lead with their interest**, not yours.
2. **One message, one purpose.**
3. **Be specific**: addresses, dates, numbers.
4. **Short for text**, fuller for email.
5. **Identify yourself** every time in marketing messages.
6. **Make opting out easy.**

| Message type | Example | Key requirements |
|---|---|---|
| Personal reply | Answer to a showing request | Accurate identity; respond to request |
| Transactional | Inspection reminder | Accurate routing; relates to agreed transaction |
| Marketing email | Monthly market update | CAN-SPAM requirements; opt-out [14] |
| Marketing text | New listing alert | Documented consent; honor STOP [8] |

:::case The FCC's 2024 consent-revocation rules
In February 2024 the FCC adopted rules making it easier for consumers to revoke consent to robocalls and robotexts. HousingWire reported that the FCC listed replies of "stop" or similar responses, automated opt-out mechanisms, and requests through a website or phone number provided by the caller as examples of reasonable revocation, and required callers to honor do-not-call and revocation requests within a reasonable time not exceeding 10 business days, with an effective date of April 11, 2025 [8]. In April 2025, the FCC delayed until April 11, 2026, the requirement to apply a revocation made in response to one type of message to all future robocalls and robotexts on unrelated matters [9]. These are regulatory developments, and the FCC's results are its own, not TTI's. For agents, the practical implication is to build systems that recognize many forms of opt-out and apply them everywhere.
:::

## The message framework

**Step 1: Classify** the message as personal, transactional, or marketing.

**Step 2: Confirm consent** appropriate to the channel and type.

**Step 3: Write** one clear purpose in plain language.

**Step 4: Include** identity, address, and opt-out where required.

**Step 5: Honor** opt-outs immediately across channels.

**Step 6: Review** templates quarterly with your broker.

:::example Illustrative: fixing a market update email
A hypothetical team's monthly email has the subject "URGENT: Your Home Value!" and no physical address. Review shows the subject is misleading and the email lacks required elements. The team changes the subject to "[Neighborhood] market update: October sales," adds the brokerage address and a clear unsubscribe link, and sets opt-outs to process automatically. The example is illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Message compliance
- Message classified by type.
- Consent appropriate to channel documented.
- Subject line accurate.
- Physical address included in marketing email.
- Clear opt-out offered.
- Opt-outs honored across channels promptly.
- Vendors sending on your behalf monitored.
:::

:::summary
Personal, transactional, and marketing messages carry different obligations. CAN-SPAM requires accurate headers and subject lines, ad identification, a physical address, and an opt-out honored within 10 business days. FCC rules let consumers revoke text and robocall consent by reasonable means and require prompt honoring of requests.
:::

# Chapter 9: Fair Housing in Follow-Up and Advertising

:::objectives
- State the protected classes under the federal Fair Housing Act.
- Recognize steering and discriminatory language in follow-up.
- Apply Fair Housing principles to digital advertising and audience targeting.
- Escalate questions to a managing broker.
:::

## The legal framework

The federal Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination in housing transactions on the basis of race, color, national origin, religion, sex (including sexual orientation and gender identity), familial status, and disability [12]. Many states and localities add further protected classes, such as source of income, age, or marital status. Follow-up communications are housing communications: the words agents use in texts, emails, and advertisements, and the decisions about which leads receive which listings, are subject to the law.

## Steering and language

Steering occurs when an agent directs buyers toward or away from areas based on protected characteristics, including through comments about who lives in a neighborhood. Follow-up scripts should describe properties and amenities, not people. Phrases such as "perfect for young professionals," "great family neighborhood," or "safe area" can imply preferences or raise concerns; agents can instead describe features ("three bedrooms, fenced yard, 0.4 miles to the park") and direct clients to objective data sources for schools and crime.

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| "Great neighborhood for families" | "Three bedrooms; fenced yard; near [park name]" |
| "Safe area" | "Here's the city's public crime map so you can review it" |
| "You'd fit in well there" | "Here are the homes that match your criteria" |
| "Not many kids on this street" | (Do not comment on residents) |
| "Walking distance to [house of worship]" | "0.5 miles to [street] transit stop" |

## Equal service in follow-up

Equal service means offering every lead the same standard of response, information, and options. A follow-up system helps by applying the same calendar to everyone. Agents should not, for example, send more listings or faster responses to some inquirers based on names, accents, or assumptions.

:::case United States v. Meta Platforms: housing ads and algorithms
In June 2022, the U.S. Department of Justice announced a settlement resolving allegations that Meta's housing advertising system discriminated in violation of the Fair Housing Act [13]. Under the settlement, Meta agreed to stop using an advertising tool for housing ads (known as "Special Ad Audience") that relied on a discriminatory algorithm, to develop a new system to address disparities in how housing ads are delivered, to refrain from providing targeting options for housing advertisers that relate to FHA-protected characteristics, and to pay a civil penalty of $115,054, the maximum available under the Act [13]. Meta's results are its own, not TTI's. The case shows that Fair Housing law reaches audience targeting and ad delivery, not only the words in an ad, which matters to agents who run digital campaigns for listings or lead generation.
:::

## The Fair Housing framework

**Step 1: Train** everyone who writes or sends follow-up on Fair Housing basics annually.

**Step 2: Review** scripts and templates for language about people rather than properties.

**Step 3: Apply** the same follow-up calendar to all leads.

**Step 4: Use** objective third-party sources for schools, crime, and demographics.

**Step 5: Choose** platform housing-ad categories and avoid targeting by protected characteristics or proxies.

**Step 6: Escalate** any uncertainty to the managing broker before sending.

:::example Illustrative: a template review
A hypothetical team reviews 40 saved templates and finds six problems: two describe neighborhoods as "family-friendly," one calls an area "up-and-coming," one says "ideal for empty nesters," and two include school-quality opinions. The team rewrites them to describe features and link to objective sources, and adds a quarterly review. The example is illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Fair Housing in follow-up
- Annual Fair Housing training completed.
- Templates describe properties, not people.
- Same calendar applied to all leads.
- Objective sources used for schools and crime.
- Housing ad category selected on platforms.
- No targeting by protected characteristics or proxies.
- Escalation path to managing broker known.
:::

:::summary
The Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability, and states add more protections. Follow-up should describe properties, not people, provide equal service, and avoid steering. The DOJ's settlement with Meta shows that Fair Housing law applies to ad targeting and delivery.
:::

# Chapter 10: Automation and AI in Follow-Up

:::objectives
- Identify follow-up tasks suited to automation and AI.
- Apply the FCC's ruling on AI-generated voices.
- Design AI assistance with human oversight and disclosure.
- Evaluate vendors for compliance.
:::

## Where automation helps

Automation can route new inquiries, send acknowledgments, remind agents of scheduled touches, draft messages for human review, summarize call notes, and keep records. AI tools can draft personalized follow-up and answer common questions. These uses can improve consistency. They also introduce risks: unconsented automated contact, inaccurate statements, Fair Housing violations, and privacy problems.

## The FCC's AI voice ruling

On February 8, 2024, the FCC announced a unanimous declaratory ruling that the TCPA's restrictions on "artificial or prerecorded voice" calls cover AI technologies that generate human voices, including voice cloning [6][7]. The FCC's announcement explained that callers must obtain prior express consent before making such calls, that telemarketers need prior express written consent, and that calls must identify the caller and, for telemarketing, provide an opt-out [7]. In a separate 2024 proposal, the FCC noted that its rules do not treat technology used to answer inbound calls in the same way [20]; agents using AI receptionists for inbound calls should still disclose AI use and follow brokerage policy.

| Use | Risk level | Safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| AI drafts text for agent review | Low | Human approves each message |
| Automated acknowledgment to web inquiry | Moderate | Consent; honest identity; opt-out |
| AI answers inbound calls | Moderate | Disclose AI; escalate to human; record policy |
| Outbound AI-voice calls | High | Prior express written consent; identity; opt-out [7] |
| AI-generated listing descriptions | Moderate | Fact-check; Fair Housing review |
| Mass automated texting | High | Documented consent; revocation handling [8] |

:::case The FCC confirms the TCPA covers AI-generated voices
The FCC's February 2024 declaratory ruling, adopted unanimously and effective immediately, confirmed that calls using AI-generated voices are "artificial" voice calls under the TCPA [6][7]. The FCC described the ruling as giving state attorneys general new tools to pursue the people behind robocalls that use voice cloning, and noted that callers must obtain prior express consent, and in the case of telemarketing prior express written consent, before making such calls [7]. The FCC's results are its own, not TTI's. For real estate teams, it means that an AI tool that calls leads with a synthetic voice is subject to the same consent rules as a prerecorded message.
:::

## The automation framework

**Step 1: List** each follow-up task and decide whether to automate, assist, or keep manual.

**Step 2: Check consent** requirements for any automated outbound contact.

**Step 3: Disclose** AI use honestly; never imply a human is responding when one is not.

**Step 4: Keep a human** in the loop for advice, pricing, contract, and Fair Housing matters.

**Step 5: Evaluate vendors** for consent capture, opt-out handling, data security, and records.

**Step 6: Audit** a sample of automated messages monthly.

:::example Illustrative: designing an AI-assisted reply
A hypothetical team uses an AI assistant to draft first replies to web inquiries. The tool drafts; an inside sales associate edits and sends within 10 minutes. Drafts never quote prices beyond listing data, never discuss neighborhoods' residents, and include the brokerage name. After a month, the team audits 50 drafts and finds three that needed factual correction, which reinforces the human review step. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist AI and automation
- Each task classified as automate, assist, or manual.
- Consent confirmed for automated outbound contact.
- AI use disclosed honestly.
- Human review for advice and sensitive topics.
- Vendor consent, opt-out, and security reviewed.
- Monthly audit of automated messages.
:::

:::summary
Automation and AI can make follow-up more consistent but raise consent, accuracy, Fair Housing, and privacy risks. The FCC confirmed in 2024 that AI-generated voices are artificial voices under the TCPA, requiring consent. Keep humans in the loop, disclose AI use, evaluate vendors, and audit regularly.
:::

# Chapter 12: Data Privacy, Wire-Fraud Warnings, and the Weekly Scorecard

:::objectives
- Apply basic data-security principles to lead and client records.
- Warn clients about wire fraud at the right moments.
- Measure follow-up with a weekly scorecard.
- Know when to escalate to a broker.
:::

## Protecting lead and client data

Lead records contain names, phone numbers, emails, financial details, and sometimes sensitive information. The FTC's guide to protecting personal information sets out five principles: take stock of what information you have, scale down to keep only what you need, lock it to protect what you keep, pitch it by properly disposing of what you no longer need, and plan ahead for security incidents [15]. For agents, this means knowing which apps and phones hold client data, limiting access, using strong authentication, and deleting what is no longer needed in line with brokerage retention policies.

## Wire-fraud warnings

Real estate transactions are frequent targets of business email compromise. The FBI's 2024 Internet Crime Report recorded 9,359 real estate fraud complaints with reported losses of more than $173 million [18]. Agents should warn clients early and repeatedly that they will never send wiring instructions by email or text, that clients should verify any wiring instructions by calling a known number for the title or escrow company, and that any change in instructions is a red flag.

**Wire-fraud warning (email at contract signing):**
"Important: Criminals target real estate transactions with fake emails. I will never email or text you wiring instructions. Before sending any money, call the title company at the number on their official website or your contract, not a number in an email, to confirm instructions."

:::case An FBI recovery in a spoofed closing email
The FBI's 2024 Internet Crime Report describes a March 2024 case in which buyers received a spoofed email, appearing to come from their real estate agents, requesting that they wire $956,342 to finalize the closing; two days later the buyers realized the instructions were fraudulent, and after notification the IC3 Recovery Asset Team worked to freeze the receiving account, and $955,060 was stopped and returned [18]. The FBI's results are its own, not TTI's, and many victims are not so fortunate. The case illustrates why agents should warn clients repeatedly and why prompt reporting matters.

# Appendix B: Script Library

**B1. Requested information (email).** "Hi [Name], here's the information you asked for about [address]: [facts]. Attached: [documents]. If you'd like to see it, I have [two times]."

**B2. Second attempt (call and voicemail).** "Hi [Name], it's [Your Name] with [Brokerage] following up on [address]. I sent the details by email. Call or text me at [number] anytime."

**B3. Helpful item (text).** "Hi [Name], here's the floor plan for [address] in case it's useful. Reply STOP to opt out."

**B4. Education (email).** "A quick guide to what to expect when buying in [area]: steps, typical costs, and timelines. Happy to answer questions."

**B5. Check-in (call).** "Hi [Name], just checking whether you had questions about [address] or anything else you're looking at."

**B6. New listing (text).** "New on [street]: [beds/baths], [feature you mentioned]. Want details?"

**B7. Process guide (email).** "Here's a one-page timeline of offer to closing, with the questions people most often ask."

**B8. Timeline check (call).** "Has your timing changed at all? I want to make sure what I send is useful."

**B9. Market update (email).** "[Neighborhood] this month: [number] sales, median price [figure], median days on market [figure]. Source: [MLS]."

**B10. Specific help (text).** "If it would help, I can set up a short tour of two or three homes this weekend. Totally fine if not."

**B11. Process story (email).** "Here's how a typical purchase in our area unfolds, from first showing to keys, so you know what to expect."

**B12. Permission check (email or text).** "I don't want to crowd your inbox. Would you like me to continue, switch to a monthly update, or pause? Any answer is fine."

**B13. Missed call (text, where permitted).** "Hi, this is [Your Name] with [Brokerage]. Sorry I missed your call. How can I help?"

**B14. Showing confirmation (text).** "Confirmed: [address] at [time] on [date]. I'll meet you out front. Reply if anything changes."

**B15. Post-showing (text).** "Thanks for touring [address] today. What did you think? Any you'd like to see again?"

**B16. Offer not accepted (call).** "I'm sorry this one didn't work out. Here's what we learned, and two homes we could look at next."

**B17. Under contract update (email).** "Here's where we are: inspection [date], appraisal [date], financing deadline [date], closing [date]. Next step: [item]."

**B18. Wire-fraud warning (email).** See Chapter 12.

**B19. Opt-out confirmation (text, once).** "You're unsubscribed and won't receive more messages from us."

**B20. Lost to another agent (email).** "Thanks for letting me know, and congratulations. I'll remove you from my list. Best of luck."

# Glossary

**Comparative market analysis (CMA).** A licensee's analysis of recent comparable sales used to estimate a likely price range.

**Consent revocation.** A consumer's withdrawal of permission to receive calls or texts, which under FCC rules may be made by any reasonable means.

**Established business relationship.** Under the Telemarketing Sales Rule, a relationship lasting 18 months after a transaction or three months after an inquiry that permits certain calls to registered numbers.

**Fair Housing Act.** Federal law prohibiting housing discrimination based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability.

**National Do Not Call Registry.** A federal list of telephone numbers whose owners do not wish to receive telemarketing calls.

**Nurture.** Long-term, permission-based contact with people not yet ready to act.

**Pipeline stage.** A defined step in a lead's progress, from New to Closed.

**Prerecorded or artificial voice.** A recorded or synthetic voice in a call, including AI-generated voices, subject to TCPA consent rules.

**Steering.** Directing buyers toward or away from areas based on protected characteristics.

**Telemarketing Sales Rule (TSR).** FTC rule governing telemarketing, including Do Not Call and calling-hour requirements.

**Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA).** Federal law restricting autodialed and prerecorded or artificial-voice calls and texts without consent.

**Wire fraud.** Theft of funds by deceiving a party into wiring money, often using spoofed email.
