# How to Use This Book

Many small businesses lose good jobs not because their work is weak but because a busy day delays a callback, a quote sits unanswered, or nobody checks back politely after a buyer goes quiet. The fix is rarely more aggressive selling. It is faster first replies, clearer quotes, a calm follow-up routine that stops the moment a buyer says no, and honest records.

This workbook combines a library of ready-to-adapt scripts with the reasoning behind them. It draws on research about lead response, evidence on AI assistance at work, consumer-protection rules from the Federal Trade Commission and Federal Communications Commission, and public cases that show what goes right and wrong. It is written for owners of service businesses, contractors, consultants, and solo operators who quote work and follow up with buyers.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this book you should be able to:

- Explain what research says about response speed and lead qualification.
- Write a rules sheet and master AI brief that prevent invented prices and promises.
- Reply to web forms, messages, missed calls, and voicemails within minutes using adaptable scripts.
- Run a four-question discovery conversation and send clear quote cover notes.
- Follow a permission-based seven-touch quote sequence from Day 0 to Day 21.
- Respond honestly to common buyer hesitations.
- Check in after a sale and ask for reviews in ways that follow FTC rules.
- Apply the basics of telemarketing, texting, email, and AI-voice rules.
- Use AI tools safely with customer data.
- Track leads, open quotes, and results on a weekly scoreboard.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives and an explanation, followed by a step-by-step framework, scripts where relevant, an illustrative worked example, a real case study with sources, at least one table, a worksheet or checklist, a summary, and review questions. Worked examples are hypothetical and their figures invented for teaching. Case studies describe real companies, agencies, and research from public sources listed in the References; their results are their own, not TTI's.

Scripts use square brackets for details you must fill in, such as [first name] or [service]. Never send a script with a bracketed placeholder left in it, and never let an AI tool fill a bracket with a guess.

:::warning Scope and limits
This workbook is education only. It is not legal or telecommunications compliance advice. Telemarketing, texting, email, privacy, and AI rules change and vary by state; consult a qualified attorney about your specific practices. No close rate, revenue, or sales result is promised. Scripts are starting points that you must adapt to your business, your prices, and the facts of each conversation.
:::

# Chapter 1: Why Follow-Up Wins: Speed and Persistence

:::objectives
- Summarize research on how response time affects lead qualification.
- Explain why polite persistence matters and when it must stop.
- Identify where leads leak out of a small business.
- Set response-time and follow-up standards.
:::

## The leak nobody sees

Most owners can name their best marketing channel. Few can say how long it takes them to respond to a new inquiry, how many quotes went unanswered last month, or how many buyers were never contacted a second time. These are the quiet leaks in a sales process. They do not show up in advertising reports, but they decide how much of the money spent on marketing turns into work.

:::case The short life of online sales leads
In a 2011 Harvard Business Review article, James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington reported an audit of 2,241 U.S. companies, measuring how long each took to respond to a web-generated test lead. Only 37 percent responded within an hour, 23 percent never responded at all, and the average first response time among those that did respond was 42 hours [1]. Analyzing a larger dataset of leads, they found that firms that tried to contact potential customers within an hour of receiving a query were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as those that tried to contact the customer an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as companies that waited 24 hours or longer [1]. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's, and qualifying a lead is not the same as closing a sale. Even so, the study gives small businesses a clear, low-cost target: respond within the hour, and sooner when possible.
:::

## Persistence, politely

Speed is half the story. Many buyers do not reply to the first message, not because they are uninterested but because they are busy, comparing options, or waiting on a spouse or budget. A short sequence of polite, useful follow-ups gives them several chances to respond. The other half of persistence is restraint: once a buyer says no, asks you to stop, or chooses another provider, the follow-up ends immediately and the request is recorded.

## Where leads leak

| Leak point | Symptom | Standard to set |
|---|---|---|
| New inquiry | Replies take hours or days | First reply within 1 hour in business hours |
| Missed call | No callback until evening | Callback or permitted text within 30 minutes |
| Quote sent | No check-in after sending | Planned follow-up sequence |
| Quiet buyer | Owner gives up after one try | Up to seven polite touches over 21 days |
| Lost job | No record of why | Reason recorded; polite close-out |
| Completed job | No check-in or review request | Day 0, 3, 14, 30 check-ins |

## The speed-and-persistence framework

**Step 1: Measure your current response time** for the last 20 inquiries.

**Step 2: Set a response standard**, such as first reply within one hour during business hours.

**Step 3: Schedule two daily follow-up windows**, such as 12:00 p.m. and 4:30 p.m.

**Step 4: Adopt a written follow-up sequence** (Chapter 5).

**Step 5: Define stop rules** that end follow-up immediately.

**Step 6: Review results weekly** on a scoreboard (Chapter 10).

:::example Illustrative: measuring the leak
A hypothetical handyman business reviews its last 20 web inquiries. The median first reply took 6 hours; 4 inquiries were never answered; only 5 of 14 quotes received any follow-up. The owner adopts a one-hour standard during business hours, two daily follow-up windows, and the seven-touch sequence. Over the next month, the median reply time falls to 40 minutes and every quote receives a planned follow-up. The owner records results without assuming any particular close rate. Figures are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 2: The Rules Sheet and Master AI Brief

:::objectives
- Write a rules sheet that defines what your business can truthfully promise.
- Explain why a business is responsible for what its automated tools say.
- Build a master AI brief for drafting follow-up messages.
- Set review rules for AI-drafted messages.
:::

## Boundaries before scripts

Before writing any script or asking an AI tool to draft messages, write down the facts every message must respect: what you sell, your service area, how you price, how quickly you can actually schedule work, and which jobs you decline. This rules sheet protects both you and the buyer. It keeps you, your staff, and any AI tool from promising prices, timelines, or services that are not true.

The FTC's guidance for small businesses explains that advertising must be truthful and not misleading, and that businesses must have evidence to back up their claims before they make them [20]. A follow-up text that promises "same-day service" when your calendar is full for two weeks is a problem whether a person or a program wrote it.

:::case Air Canada and the chatbot's promise
CBC News reported that British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal found Air Canada liable after a chatbot on its website gave a customer, Jake Moffatt, incorrect information suggesting he could apply for a bereavement fare after travel; the tribunal ordered the airline to pay $812 to cover the difference between the bereavement rate and the full fare he paid [4]. A legal commentary by the law firm McCarthy Tétrault summarized the decision as holding that a company can be liable for negligent misrepresentations made by a chatbot on a publicly available commercial website [5]. Air Canada's case and its outcome are its own, not TTI's, and the decision came from a Canadian small-claims tribunal, not a U.S. court. The lesson for any business using AI to talk with buyers is direct: customers reasonably rely on what your tools tell them, so your tools must be limited to facts you have approved.
:::

## The rules sheet

:::worksheet Sales and follow-up rules sheet
Business name, public phone, and reply email: ___
Core service or product quoted most often: ___
Service area: ___
Standard scheduling window (only what is true now): ___
Pricing rule (fixed menu price or custom quote after inspection): ___
Price ranges approved for messages (if any): ___
Jobs we politely decline: ___
Licenses, insurance, or warranties we may mention: ___
Where opt-outs and do-not-contact requests are recorded: ___
Who reviews AI-drafted messages: ___
:::

## The master AI brief

A master brief is a standing instruction you give an AI tool each time it drafts a message. A sample brief:

> You are drafting short, polite follow-up messages for [business name], a [type of business] serving [service area]. Use only the facts in the rules sheet below. Never state or estimate a price, discount, deadline, arrival time, or guarantee unless it appears in the rules sheet or in my notes for this buyer. If information is missing, write [CHECK] in its place. Keep messages under 80 words for text and under 150 words for email. Use a warm, plain, professional tone. Never pressure, never invent urgency, and never claim results. If the buyer has said no, asked to stop, or chosen another provider, do not draft a follow-up; draft only a brief thank-you if I ask for one. Rules sheet: [paste]. Buyer notes: [paste].

| AI may | AI must not |
|---|---|
| Turn your bullet notes into a clear paragraph | Invent prices, discounts, or deadlines |
| Adjust tone and length | Promise arrival windows you did not check |
| Suggest a subject line | Send messages automatically without review |
| Flag missing information with [CHECK] | Contact anyone who has opted out |
| Summarize a long buyer email | Make legal, warranty, or safety claims |

## The rules-and-brief framework

**Step 1: Complete the rules sheet** and date it.

**Step 2: Write your master AI brief** using the sample.

**Step 3: Test the brief** on five past inquiries and check every draft for invented facts.

**Step 4: Assign a human reviewer** for every AI-drafted message before it is sent.

**Step 5: Update the rules sheet** whenever prices, scheduling, or services change.

**Step 6: Keep a log** of any AI errors and refine the brief.

:::example Illustrative: catching an invented promise
A hypothetical pest-control company tests its master brief on five old inquiries. One draft tells a buyer "we can be there tomorrow morning," a schedule nobody checked. The owner adds a line to the brief requiring [CHECK] for any date or time, and adds "earliest available appointment" to the rules sheet, updated each Monday. In the next test round, all five drafts flag dates with [CHECK]. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::summary
A rules sheet defines the facts every message must respect, and a master AI brief keeps AI drafting within those facts. The Air Canada tribunal decision shows that businesses can be held responsible for what their automated tools tell customers, and the FTC requires that advertising claims be truthful and supported. Every AI-drafted message needs human review.
:::

# Chapter 3: Reply-in-Minutes Scripts

:::objectives
- Explain why the first minutes after an inquiry matter.
- Adapt fast-reply scripts for web forms, messages, email, missed calls, and voicemail.
- Respect consent rules when replying by text.
- Set up a simple after-hours reply.
:::

## The first reply

The first reply has three jobs: confirm that a real person received the inquiry, show that you understood the request, and propose a clear next step. It does not need to quote a price or answer every question. A short, specific reply sent quickly is better than a perfect reply sent tomorrow.

:::case The Lead Response Management study
In a 2007 study presented by InsideSales.com with Dr. James Oldroyd, then at MIT, researchers analyzed three years of lead and call data from six companies [2]. The study summary reports that the odds of contacting a lead if called within 5 minutes versus 30 minutes dropped 100 times, and the odds of qualifying the lead dropped 21 times [2]. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's. The study used a sales-software vendor's data from a small number of companies, and it did not measure closed sales, so its precise multipliers should not be treated as a forecast for any business. Its direction, however, is consistent with the HBR audit in Chapter 1: faster contact is associated with better outcomes, and the first few minutes may matter more than owners assume.
:::

## Fast-reply scripts

**Web form reply (email or text, if the buyer provided a number and asked to be contacted that way):**

> Hi [first name], this is [your name] at [business]. Thanks for reaching out about [service]. I read your note about [one detail they mentioned]. To give you an accurate answer, could I ask two quick questions by phone today? I'm free at [time option 1] or [time option 2]. If text is easier, reply here. Reply STOP to opt out.

**Social media direct message reply:**

> Thanks for messaging [business], [first name]. Happy to help with [service]. So I can point you in the right direction: what's the address or area, and when are you hoping to get this done? You can also reach me directly at [phone].

**Email inquiry reply:**

> Subject: Your [service] request
>
> Hi [first name], thank you for contacting [business]. You mentioned [detail]. The next step is usually a short call or a visit so I can see [what you need to see] and give you an accurate quote. Would [day/time] or [day/time] work? If you'd rather keep this by email, just reply with [the two or three details you need]. Best, [name], [phone], [business address].

**Missed-call text (only where you have the caller's consent or the message is a direct, non-marketing reply to their call; see Chapter 8):**

> Hi, this is [name] at [business]. Sorry I missed your call. I'm with a customer and can call you back by [time]. If it's easier, reply here with what you need. Reply STOP to opt out.

**Voicemail script when calling back:**

> Hi [first name], it's [name] from [business] returning your call about [service]. I'd be glad to help. You can reach me at [phone, said slowly, twice]. I'll also send a short [text/email] so you have my number. Thanks.

**After-hours auto-reply:**

> Thanks for contacting [business]. We've received your message. Our office hours are [hours], and we'll reply by [time] on [next business day]. If this is an emergency involving [safety issue relevant to your trade], please call [emergency number or service].

## Matching channel to situation

| Situation | First reply channel | Target time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Web form with phone and email | Call, then email | Within 1 hour | Text only with consent |
| Social media message | Same platform | Within 1 hour | Move to phone if needed |
| Email inquiry | Email | Within 1 hour | Offer two times |
| Missed call | Callback | Within 30 minutes | Text only where permitted |
| After hours | Auto-reply | Immediate | Real reply next morning |

## The fast-reply framework

**Step 1: Save the six scripts** in your phone or shared inbox as templates.

**Step 2: Customize each** with your business details and one personal detail per buyer.

**Step 3: Check consent** before replying by text.

**Step 4: Reply within your standard**, using the two daily windows to catch anything missed.

**Step 5: Log each inquiry** with source, time received, and time of first reply.

**Step 6: Review response times weekly.**

:::example Illustrative: a two-person shop's fast-reply setup
A hypothetical appliance-repair business with two technicians saves the six scripts as text and email templates. The office manager answers web forms between calls; the owner checks messages at 12:00 and 4:30. They add an after-hours auto-reply. In a month they receive 48 inquiries; 41 receive a first reply within an hour during business hours, and the rest are answered the next morning. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Fast-reply setup
- Six scripts saved as templates.
- Business details filled in.
- Consent rule for texting understood.
- After-hours auto-reply active.
- Inquiry log started.
- Two daily follow-up windows scheduled.
- Weekly response-time review scheduled.
:::

:::summary
The first reply should confirm receipt, show understanding, and propose a next step. The Lead Response Management study, like the HBR audit, associates faster contact with better outcomes, though its precise multipliers come from limited data. Adapt the six scripts, check consent before texting, and log response times.
:::

# Chapter 4: Discovery Questions and Quote Cover Notes

:::objectives
- Run a four-question discovery conversation.
- Explain how perceived value shapes buying decisions.
- Write quote cover notes that make scope, limits, and next steps clear.
- Avoid common quoting mistakes.
:::

## Discovery before price

Buyers often ask "how much?" first. A price given without understanding the job is a guess, and guesses either lose money or lose the buyer. A short discovery conversation lets you quote accurately and show the buyer that you understand the problem.

## The four-question discovery pad

1. **What's going on?** "Tell me what's happening and what made you reach out now."
2. **What does a good result look like?** "When this is done, what would make you say it went well?"
3. **What are the constraints?** "Is there a timeline, a budget range, or anything about access or scheduling I should know?"
4. **Who decides?** "Is anyone else involved in choosing who does the work?"

:::worksheet Discovery pad
Buyer name and contact: ___
Source of inquiry: ___
What's going on: ___
Why now: ___
Good result looks like: ___
Timeline: ___
Budget range (if shared): ___
Access or scheduling notes: ___
Other decision makers: ___
Next step agreed: ___
:::

:::case Hormozi's value equation
Entrepreneur and author Alex Hormozi, in the audio edition of his book $100M Offers, describes what he calls the value equation: "Dream outcome times perceived likelihood of achievement divided by time delay times effort and sacrifice equals value" [6]. Hormozi's results are his own, not TTI's, and the equation is a framework rather than a measured law. It is useful for quoting because it explains why the cheapest quote does not always win. A buyer weighs not only price but how likely the result seems, how long it will take, and how much hassle is involved. Discovery answers tell you which of those matters most to this buyer, and your quote cover note can address them honestly.
:::

## Quote cover notes

A quote cover note is the short message that accompanies a quote. It restates the buyer's goal, summarizes scope and exclusions, explains timing, and proposes a next step. It should never add promises that are not in the quote itself.

**Quote cover note (email):**

> Subject: Your quote for [service] at [address]
>
> Hi [first name], thanks for walking me through [the problem]. You said a good result would be [their words]. The attached quote covers [scope in one or two lines]. It does not include [main exclusions]. Our earliest available start is [date, checked], and the work typically takes [duration]. The quote is valid until [date]. If it looks right, you can approve it by [method]. If anything is unclear, reply or call me at [phone]. [Name]

**Quote cover note (text, with consent):**

> Hi [first name], it's [name] at [business]. I just emailed your quote for [service]. It covers [scope in a few words]; earliest start is [date]. Happy to answer questions. Reply STOP to opt out.

## Common quoting mistakes

| Mistake | Effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Price without scope | Buyer compares unlike quotes | Summarize scope and exclusions |
| Vague timing | Buyer worries about delays | State checked start date and duration |
| No expiry date | Old prices resurface | Add a validity date |
| No next step | Buyer doesn't know how to proceed | State how to approve |
| Promises in the note not in the quote | Disputes later | Keep notes consistent with the quote |

## The discovery-and-quote framework

**Step 1: Ask the four discovery questions** and record answers.

**Step 2: Confirm the next step** before ending the conversation.

**Step 3: Prepare the quote** with scope, exclusions, timing, and validity date.

**Step 4: Write a cover note** that reflects the buyer's goal in their words.

**Step 5: Send within your standard**, ideally the same day.

**Step 6: Schedule the follow-up sequence** (Chapter 5) at the moment you send.

:::example Illustrative: a fence quote
A hypothetical fencing contractor learns in discovery that the buyer's main concern is keeping a new puppy in the yard before a family visit in five weeks. Price matters, but timing matters more. The cover note restates "a secure yard before your family visit," lists scope and exclusions, gives a checked start date of two weeks out and a three-day duration, and sets a 14-day validity. The contractor schedules the follow-up sequence the moment the quote is sent. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::summary
Four discovery questions uncover the problem, the desired result, constraints, and decision makers. Hormozi's value equation explains why buyers weigh likelihood, time, and effort as well as price. Quote cover notes restate the buyer's goal, summarize scope and exclusions, give checked timing, and propose a clear next step without adding promises.
:::

# Chapter 5: The Seven-Touch Quote Follow-Up Sequence

:::objectives
- Follow a permission-based seven-touch sequence from Day 0 to Day 21.
- Make each touch useful rather than repetitive.
- Apply stop rules and record outcomes.
- Recognize the legal risks of unconsented calls and texts.
:::

## Why a sequence

Without a written sequence, follow-up depends on memory and mood. Some buyers get five messages in two days; others get none. A sequence spreads polite touches over three weeks, varies the channel and content, and ends with a respectful close-out. Every touch should give the buyer something: an answer, a useful detail, a scheduling option, or an easy way to say no.

## The sequence

| Day | Channel | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Email + text (with consent) | Send quote and cover note |
| 1 | Text or email | Confirm receipt; offer to answer questions |
| 3 | Phone call | Check fit; address questions |
| 5 | Email | Share one useful detail (process, photos, FAQ) |
| 8 | Text or email | Scheduling check |
| 14 | Email | Gentle check-in; offer to adjust scope |
| 21 | Email | Polite close-out |

## Scripts

**Day 1 — receipt check:**

> Hi [first name], just making sure my quote for [service] came through. Any questions I can answer? [Name], [business]. Reply STOP to opt out.

**Day 3 — call script:**

> Hi [first name], it's [name] from [business]. I'm calling about the quote for [service]. Did you have a chance to look it over? ... Is there anything you'd like me to explain or adjust? ... [If not ready:] No problem at all. Would it be all right if I checked back next week?

**Day 3 — voicemail if no answer:**

> Hi [first name], [name] from [business] about your [service] quote. No need to call back unless you have questions; I'll send a quick email with a couple of details that might help. My number is [phone].

**Day 5 — useful detail:**

> Subject: What to expect with [service]
>
> Hi [first name], a few people have asked how [service] works day to day, so here's a quick overview: [two or three plain facts about the process]. Your quote is valid until [date]. Happy to answer any questions. [Name]

**Day 8 — scheduling check:**

> Hi [first name], our schedule for [month] is filling, and I wanted to let you know [checked availability]. No pressure; just say the word if you'd like me to hold a date. Reply STOP to opt out.

Only describe availability that is true. Never invent scarcity.

**Day 14 — adjust scope:**

> Subject: Any changes to your [service] plans?
>
> Hi [first name], checking in on the [service] quote. If the timing or budget has changed, I'm glad to suggest a smaller first phase or a different schedule. If you've gone another way, that's completely fine; a quick note lets me close your file. [Name]

**Day 21 — polite close-out:**

> Subject: Closing your quote
>
> Hi [first name], I haven't heard back, so I'll assume the timing isn't right and close your quote for now. If anything changes, I'd be glad to help; just reply to this email. Thank you for considering [business]. [Name]

## Stop rules

Stop the sequence immediately and record the outcome when a buyer:

- Replies STOP, unsubscribe, cancel, quit, or similar words, or asks you not to contact them by any means.
- Says "no thanks," "we went another way," or "not interested."
- Accepts the quote (move to onboarding).

:::case The Keller Williams $40 million TCPA settlement
Inman reported in 2023 that Keller Williams Realty agreed to pay $40 million to settle a class action lawsuit alleging that its agents made unsolicited, prerecorded calls to consumers without their consent, including calls to consumers on the National Do Not Call Registry; Keller Williams denied wrongdoing or liability as part of the settlement [14]. According to Inman, eligible claimants included people who, without consent, received two or more calls or texts after appearing on the Do Not Call Registry for at least 31 days, or calls using an artificial or prerecorded voice or an autodialing system, and Keller Williams agreed to create a TCPA task force and provide additional compliance materials to franchisees [14]. The legal firm Manatt also reviewed the settlement [15]. Keller Williams's case and settlement are its own, not TTI's. The lesson for small businesses is that follow-up practices that seem routine, such as calling lists or using dialing software, can carry significant legal risk without consent, Do Not Call checks, and opt-out records.
:::

## The sequence framework

**Step 1: Copy the sequence** into your calendar or tracker the moment you send a quote.

**Step 2: Personalize each touch** with one detail from discovery.

**Step 3: Confirm consent** for each channel before using it.

**Step 4: Send each touch** during your daily follow-up windows.

**Step 5: Apply stop rules** immediately and record the reason.

**Step 6: Review outcomes** weekly: accepted, declined, closed with no reply.

:::example Illustrative: a sequence in practice
A hypothetical painting contractor sends 20 quotes in a month and follows the sequence for each. Six buyers accept by Day 3; four accept between Day 5 and Day 14; three decline, with reasons recorded (price twice, timing once); one replies STOP on Day 8 and is removed from all follow-up the same day; six receive the Day 21 close-out with no reply. The contractor notes that four acceptances came after the third touch and that no buyer complained about frequency. Figures are illustrative.
:::

Quote sent date: ___
Consent for text (yes/no, date, how obtained): ___
Day 1 date: ___
Day 3 date: ___
Day 5 date: ___
Day 8 date: ___
Day 14 date: ___
Day 21 date: ___
Outcome: ___
Reason (if declined): ___
:::

:::summary
A written seven-touch sequence spreads useful, polite follow-ups over 21 days and ends with a respectful close-out. Each touch should add value, describe only true availability, and respect consent. Stop rules end follow-up immediately. The Keller Williams settlement shows that unconsented calls and texts can carry serious legal risk.

# Chapter 6: Honest Replies to Buyer Hesitation

:::objectives
- Recognize common reasons buyers hesitate.
- Respond with information and options rather than pressure.
- Reduce the buyer's perceived risk honestly.
- Know when to let a buyer go.
:::

## Hesitation is information

When a buyer hesitates, they are usually telling you something: the price feels high relative to the value they see, the timing is wrong, they are worried about risk, or someone else needs to agree. The goal of a reply is not to "overcome" the buyer but to understand the concern and offer accurate information or a real option.

:::case Warby Parker's home try-on
Inc. reported that when Warby Parker's founders were planning their online eyewear company, many classmates told them, "You'll never be able to convince people they should be buying this online" [7]. The feedback inspired the idea of letting customers try on five pairs of frames at home for free before buying any, which Inc. described as "a hit with virtually everyone"; co-founder Dave Gilboa said, "That's when we felt really confident that this idea was going to work" [7]. Warby Parker's results are its own, not TTI's. The case shows a respectful response to hesitation: rather than arguing with buyers' doubts, the company changed the offer to reduce the buyer's risk. Small businesses can do the same with clear warranties, small first phases, site visits, references, or written scope.
:::

## Common hesitations and honest replies

| Hesitation | What it may mean | Honest response |
|---|---|---|
| "Your price is higher than another quote." | Unlike scopes, or value unclear | Compare scope line by line |
| "I need to think about it." | Unanswered question or risk | Ask what would help them decide |
| "I need to talk to my spouse/partner." | Shared decision | Offer a summary or joint call |
| "Not right now." | Timing or budget | Offer to check back on a date they choose |
| Silence | Busy or undecided | Follow the sequence, then close out |
| "Can you do it cheaper?" | Budget limit | Offer a smaller scope, not a hidden cut |

## Scripts

**Price comparison:**

> That's a fair question, [first name]. Quotes can differ a lot in what they include. Would it help if we compared them line by line? Ours includes [key items]. If the other quote includes the same scope for less, that may be the better choice for you, and I'd understand.

**"I need to think about it":**

> Of course. Is there anything specific you're weighing, such as timing, cost, or how the work is done? If I can answer it now, it might save you some time.

**Shared decision:**

> That makes sense. Would a one-page summary help, or would a quick call with both of you be easier? I'm happy to do either.

**Timing:**

> Understood. When would be a better time for me to check back? I'll make a note and won't contact you before then.

**Discount request:**

> I'm not able to lower the price for the same scope, but I can suggest a smaller first phase: [option] for [price from rules sheet]. Would that work better?

## When to let go

Some buyers are not a fit: the job is outside your scope, their budget cannot cover the work properly, or they want promises you cannot make. Declining politely protects your reputation and your schedule.

## The hesitation framework

**Step 1: Listen** and restate the concern.

**Step 2: Ask one clarifying question.**

**Step 3: Offer accurate information** or a real option.

**Step 4: Reduce perceived risk** honestly with warranties, references, or a smaller phase.

**Step 5: Respect the decision**, including no.

**Step 6: Record the hesitation** and outcome to improve your offer.

:::example Illustrative: a price comparison
A hypothetical roofing company's quote is $2,100 higher than a competitor's. Comparing line by line with the buyer, the owner points out that her quote includes full tear-off, new underlayment, and permit fees, while the other lists an overlay without permits. The buyer chooses her quote. On another job, the comparison shows the competitor's scope is identical and cheaper; she thanks the buyer and closes the file. Both outcomes are recorded. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Hesitation replies
- Concern restated in the buyer's words.
- One clarifying question asked.
- Information accurate and consistent with the quote.
- Option offered is real and priced from the rules sheet.
- No invented urgency or pressure.
- Decision respected.
- Hesitation and outcome recorded.

:::summary
Hesitation usually signals a specific concern about value, timing, risk, or decision makers. Respond with information and real options, not pressure. Warby Parker's home try-on shows how reducing buyer risk can address doubt directly. Record hesitations to improve your offer, and let go of buyers who are not a fit.

## The sale is the middle, not the end

A completed job is a chance to confirm satisfaction, fix small problems, earn an honest review, and stay in touch for future work and referrals. Most small businesses skip this, even though past buyers already know and trust them.

| 30 | Final check; referral mention | Email |
| Seasonal | Useful reminder relevant to the service | Email (with opt-out) |

**Day 0 — thank you:**

> Subject: A quick favor
>

> Subject: One month later
>

>

:::case The FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials
On August 14, 2024, the Federal Trade Commission announced a final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials, which allows the agency to seek civil penalties against knowing violators [16]. The FTC said the rule prohibits fake or false consumer reviews, including reviews by people who do not exist, such as AI-generated fake reviews, or who did not have actual experience with the business, and it also addresses buying positive or negative reviews and certain review suppression practices [16]. # Chapter 8: The Rules of the Road: Calls, Texts, Email, and AI Voices

:::objectives
- Summarize the main federal rules for telemarketing calls, texts, and marketing email.
- Explain how the FCC treats AI-generated voices.
- Describe current rules for honoring opt-out requests.
- Build a simple compliance routine for a small business.
:::

## Why this chapter matters

Follow-up is only valuable if it is lawful. Federal rules on calls, texts, and email exist because consumers complained about unwanted contact, and penalties can be large. This chapter summarizes key federal rules as of late 2026. State laws can be stricter, and rules change; treat this as orientation, not legal advice.

## Telemarketing calls and the Do Not Call Registry

The FTC's guide to the Telemarketing Sales Rule explains that telemarketers generally may call only between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. local time, must check the National Do Not Call Registry at least every 31 days before calling, and may call numbers on the registry only in limited situations, such as with written permission or an established business relationship; the relationship exception lasts 18 months after a purchase or transaction and three months after an inquiry or application [8]. The FTC states that violations can cost up to $53,088 per call [8]. The FTC's Do Not Call FAQs for consumers explain how the registry works from the buyer's side [9].

## Robocalls, robotexts, and AI-generated voices

:::case The FCC's ruling on AI-generated voices
On February 8, 2024, the Federal Communications Commission released a declaratory ruling confirming that the Telephone Consumer Protection Act's restrictions on the use of "artificial or prerecorded voice" encompass current AI technologies that generate human voices; as a result, calls using such technologies require the prior express consent of the called party, absent an emergency purpose or exemption [10]. The FCC's action is its own, not TTI's. For small businesses tempted to use AI voice tools to call back leads or chase quotes, the ruling is decisive: an AI voice calling a consumer is treated like a prerecorded voice, and the same consent requirements apply.
:::

## Honoring opt-outs

The FCC's consent-revocation rules have changed several times since 2024. Under amendments published in the Federal Register on October 26, 2026, and effective November 25, 2026, callers may designate an exclusive method for consumers to revoke consent, such as a key-press opt-out on a call, a reply to a text with standardized words ("stop," "quit," "end," "revoke," "opt out," "cancel," or "unsubscribe"), or a designated website or phone number, provided the method is clearly and conspicuously disclosed; revocation requests must be honored within a reasonable time not exceeding ten business days [12]. A law-firm summary notes that, under the revised rules, a revocation in response to an informational message may be treated as applying only to that category of informational messages, and that the FCC is considering shortening the ten-business-day deadline [11].

For a small business, the simplest safe practice is stricter than the minimum: treat any clear request to stop, in any channel and in any words, as a request to stop all marketing contact, and process it the same day.

## Marketing email

The FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide explains that commercial email must not use false or misleading header information or deceptive subject lines, must identify the message as an ad where required, must include a valid physical postal address, and must tell recipients how to opt out; opt-out requests must be honored within 10 business days, and businesses remain responsible for vendors who send email on their behalf [13]. The FTC states that each separate email in violation may be subject to penalties of up to $53,088 [13].

## Summary of rules

| Channel | Key requirements | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Telemarketing calls | Calling hours 8 a.m.–9 p.m.; scrub DNC every 31 days; honor company DNC requests | FTC TSR [8] |
| AI or prerecorded voice calls | Prior express consent required | FCC ruling [10] |
| Marketing texts | Consent; clear opt-out; honor within 10 business days | FCC rules [11][12] |
| Marketing email | Accurate headers and subject; postal address; opt-out honored in 10 business days | FTC CAN-SPAM [13] |

## The compliance framework

**Step 1: Record consent** for every phone number you text or call for marketing: date, method, and wording.

**Step 2: Scrub call lists** against the National Do Not Call Registry and your internal list.

**Step 3: Include an opt-out** in every marketing text and email.

**Step 4: Keep one opt-out log** across all channels and process requests the same day.

**Step 5: Avoid AI or prerecorded voice calls** unless you have documented prior express consent and legal advice.

**Step 6: Review practices** with an attorney annually and when rules change.

:::example Illustrative: building a consent record
A hypothetical landscaping company adds a checkbox to its web form: "Yes, [business] may text me about my request. Message frequency varies. Reply STOP to opt out." The form records the date, time, and wording. Phone inquiries are logged with a note when the caller agrees to texts. An opt-out log in the shared tracker lists name, number, date, channel, and the words used; any entry removes the person from all marketing lists the same day. Figures and practices are illustrative and are not legal advice.
:::

:::checklist Compliance routine
- Consent recorded with date, method, and wording.
- Call lists scrubbed against the Do Not Call Registry at least every 31 days.
- Calls only between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. local time.
- Opt-out in every marketing text and email.
- One opt-out log across channels.
- Opt-outs processed the same day.
- No AI or prerecorded voice calls without documented consent.
- Postal address in marketing emails.
- Annual legal review.
:::

:::summary
Federal rules govern calling hours, Do Not Call checks, consent for texts and prerecorded or AI voices, and marketing email content and opt-outs. The FCC's 2024 ruling confirms that AI-generated voices are treated as artificial voices requiring prior express consent. Revised FCC opt-out rules take effect November 25, 2026. Record consent, keep one opt-out log, and process stop requests the same day.
:::

# Chapter 9: Using AI Safely: Drafts, Data, and Fraud

:::objectives
- Summarize evidence on AI assistance in customer-facing work.
- Decide which follow-up tasks AI should and should not perform.
- Protect customer data when using AI tools.
- Recognize payment and email fraud risks.
:::

## What AI is good at in follow-up

AI tools can turn rough notes into clear messages, adjust tone, summarize long emails, suggest subject lines, and draft responses to common questions. They are much less reliable at facts they have not been given, such as your schedule, your prices, or the details of a buyer's job.

:::case Generative AI at work
Economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond studied the staggered introduction of a generative AI conversational assistant among 5,172 customer-support agents and reported in the Quarterly Journal of Economics that access to AI assistance increased productivity, measured by issues resolved per hour, by 15 percent on average [3]. Less experienced and lower-skilled workers improved both speed and quality, while the most experienced and highest-skilled workers saw small gains in speed and small declines in quality; the researchers also reported that customers were more polite and less likely to ask to speak to a manager [3]. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's, and they studied one large firm's support operation. For small businesses, the findings suggest that AI drafting can help newer staff respond faster and more consistently, while experienced people should keep reviewing messages where quality and accuracy matter.
:::

## Task allocation

| Task | AI role | Human role |
|---|---|---|
| First-reply drafts | Draft from template and notes | Review and send |
| Quote cover notes | Polish wording | Confirm scope, price, dates |
| Follow-up touches | Personalize from discovery notes | Check facts; apply stop rules |
| Hesitation replies | Suggest phrasing | Decide options; ensure honesty |
| Review requests | Draft | Ensure FTC compliance |
| Pricing, discounts, legal claims | None | Human only |

## Protecting customer data

The FTC's guide "Protecting Personal Information" sets out five principles: take stock of the personal information you have, scale down to keep only what you need, lock it to protect what you keep, pitch it by disposing properly of what you no longer need, and plan ahead to respond to security incidents [18]. Applied to AI tools:

- Know what data each tool receives and how the vendor uses and stores it.
- Paste only the details needed to draft a message; leave out payment details, government ID numbers, and sensitive personal information.
- Use business accounts with access controls rather than personal accounts.
- Delete or archive old conversations according to a set schedule.

## Fraud risks

Follow-up involves invoices, payments, and email, which makes small businesses targets for fraud. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reported in its 2024 annual report that business email compromise, in which criminals impersonate trusted contacts to redirect payments, accounted for about $2.77 billion in reported losses [19]. Practical defenses include verifying any change in payment instructions by calling a known phone number, enabling multi-factor authentication on email accounts, and training staff to pause before acting on urgent payment requests.

## The safe-AI framework

**Step 1: List follow-up tasks** and assign AI and human roles using the table.

**Step 2: Use the master brief** from Chapter 2 for all drafting.

**Step 3: Limit data** pasted into AI tools to what each draft requires.

**Step 4: Review every AI draft** before sending.

**Step 5: Secure accounts** with multi-factor authentication and business-level access.

**Step 6: Verify payment changes** by phone using a known number.

:::example Illustrative: catching a fake payment change
A hypothetical cabinet maker receives an email, apparently from a regular supplier, saying that bank details have changed and an overdue invoice of $8,400 should be paid to a new account. Following her rule, the office manager calls the supplier at the number on file. The supplier has not changed banks; the email was fraudulent. She reports it and alerts her email provider. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Safe AI and data
- AI and human roles assigned for each task.
- Master brief used for all drafts.
- Only necessary data shared with AI tools.
- Every AI draft reviewed before sending.
- Business accounts with multi-factor authentication.
- Data retention schedule set.
- Payment changes verified by phone.
- Staff trained on email fraud.
:::

:::summary
Research by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond found that AI assistance raised support productivity by 15 percent on average, with the largest gains for less experienced workers. Use AI to draft and polish, but keep humans responsible for facts, prices, and compliance. Follow the FTC's data-protection principles, and defend against business email compromise, which accounted for about $2.77 billion in losses reported to the FBI in 2024.
:::

# Chapter 10: Trackers, Metrics, and the Weekly Scoreboard

:::objectives
- Keep a lead log and open-quote tracker.
- Measure response time, follow-up completion, and outcomes.
- Audit your own responsiveness.
- Run a 15-minute weekly scoreboard review.
:::

## What gets tracked

Without records, follow-up depends on memory, and memory fails on busy days. Three simple records cover most small businesses: a lead log (every inquiry, its source, and response time), an open-quote tracker (every quote and its sequence dates), and a weekly scoreboard (a handful of numbers reviewed every week).

:::case Auditing response time the HBR way
The researchers behind the 2011 Harvard Business Review study did not rely on companies' descriptions of their own responsiveness; they submitted web inquiries to 2,241 companies and measured how long each took to respond [1]. That method revealed that only 37 percent responded within an hour and 23 percent never responded [1]. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's. A small business can borrow the method: ask a friend to submit an inquiry through your website, call after hours, or send a social media message, and measure what happens. A self-audit often reveals gaps, such as a form that sends notifications to an unchecked inbox, that no report would show.
:::

## Metrics that matter

| Metric | Definition | Example target |
|---|---|---|
| Median first-reply time | Middle value of response times | Under 1 hour in business hours |
| Inquiries never answered | Count per week | Zero |
| Quotes sent within 1 business day | Percentage | 90 percent |
| Sequence completion | Quotes with all planned touches or a recorded outcome | 100 percent |
| Quote acceptance rate | Accepted ÷ quotes sent | Track the trend; no fixed target |
| Opt-outs processed same day | Percentage | 100 percent |
| Reviews requested | Completed jobs with a review request | 100 percent |

## The 15-minute weekly scoreboard review

Each week, at the same time:

1. Count new inquiries and calculate median first-reply time (3 minutes).
2. Check the open-quote tracker for overdue touches (3 minutes).
3. Record accepted, declined, and closed quotes with reasons (3 minutes).
4. Check the opt-out log and confirm all requests were processed (2 minutes).
5. Choose one improvement for next week (4 minutes).

**Step 1: Set up the lead log** (Appendix B).

**Step 2: Set up the open-quote tracker** (Appendix C).

**Step 3: Run a self-audit** of your response time through each channel.

**Step 4: Hold the weekly scoreboard review** for 15 minutes.

**Step 5: Choose one improvement** each week.

**Step 6: Compare four-week trends** rather than single weeks.

# Glossary

**Business email compromise.** Fraud in which criminals impersonate a trusted contact to redirect payments or obtain information.

**CAN-SPAM Act.** The federal law setting rules for commercial email.

**Consent record.** A record of when and how a person agreed to be contacted, including the wording used.

**Discovery.** A short conversation to understand a buyer's problem, goals, constraints, and decision makers before quoting.

**Do Not Call Registry.** The national list of phone numbers whose owners have asked not to receive telemarketing calls.

**Established business relationship.** A relationship that allows certain telemarketing calls to registered numbers for a limited time after a purchase or inquiry.

**Master AI brief.** Standing instructions that limit an AI tool to approved facts and tone.

**Opt-out.** A request to stop receiving calls, texts, or emails.

**Quote cover note.** The short message sent with a quote summarizing goal, scope, exclusions, timing, and next step.

**Rules sheet.** A dated list of the facts every sales message must respect.

**Stop rules.** Conditions that end a follow-up sequence immediately.

**TCPA.** The Telephone Consumer Protection Act, the federal law governing many calls and texts, including those using artificial or prerecorded voices.

**Telemarketing Sales Rule.** The FTC rule governing telemarketing calls, including calling hours and Do Not Call requirements.
