# How to Use This Book

This workbook is for owners and managers of small businesses (from solo operators to teams of about fifty) who know cybersecurity matters but do not have a security department. It translates guidance from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), and the FBI into a sequence of decisions and checklists you can complete in a few hours a month.

The book is organized around a simple idea: most losses at small businesses come from a short list of causes (stolen passwords, fraudulent payment requests, unpatched devices, missing backups, and weak vendor access), and a short list of habits prevents most of them. Each chapter explains a risk, presents a framework, works through an illustrative example with numbers, examines a real case drawn from public records, and ends with a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. The appendices provide templates for your inventory, account register, vendor review, incident plan, and annual review.

The cases describe what organizations and regulators have reported publicly. Those outcomes are their own, not TTI's, and they illustrate risks rather than predict them.

## What you will be able to do

- Explain the most common and costly cyber risks for small businesses.
- Organize your security work using the six functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0.
- Build an inventory of your devices, accounts, data, and vendors.
- Protect accounts with password managers and multi-factor authentication.
- Stop payment fraud with verification procedures.
- Maintain updates, backups, and vendor controls.
- Respond calmly and correctly when something goes wrong.

:::warning Scope of this workbook
This book offers general educational guidance. It does not replace advice from a qualified IT or security professional, legal counsel on breach notification and privacy laws, or your insurer. If you handle regulated data (health records, payment cards, financial or tax information), additional legal requirements apply.
:::

# Chapter 1: Why Small Businesses Are Targets

:::objectives
- Describe the scale of cybercrime reported to the FBI.
- Identify the crime types that cause the largest losses.
- Explain why small businesses are attractive targets.
- Estimate your own exposure in dollars.
:::

Many owners assume criminals pursue only large companies. The evidence says otherwise. Criminals use automated tools that probe millions of accounts and send millions of messages, and they succeed wherever basic defenses are missing, regardless of company size.

## What the FBI data shows

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) received 1,008,597 complaints in 2025, with reported losses of about $20.9 billion, a 26 percent increase over 2024 [1]. Business email compromise (BEC), in which criminals impersonate a trusted person to redirect a payment, was the second-largest category by losses, at about $3.05 billion, behind investment fraud [1]. Wire transfers accounted for most BEC losses, and BEC losses rose from about $2.77 billion in 2024 [1][2]. These figures include only incidents reported to the FBI, so actual losses are higher.

## Why small businesses are attractive

| Factor | Why it helps attackers |
|---|---|
| Few dedicated security staff | Warnings and updates are missed |
| Owners handle payments personally | One deceived person can send a large wire |
| Shared or reused passwords | One leaked password opens many accounts |
| Reliance on vendors and cloud services | Weak vendor security becomes your weakness |
| Valuable data (customer, payment, payroll) | Data can be sold or held for ransom |
| Access to larger partners | Small firms can be a path into bigger targets |

## Key terms

- **Threat**: anything that could cause harm, such as a criminal group or a careless mistake.
- **Vulnerability**: a weakness that a threat could exploit, such as an unpatched laptop.
- **Risk**: the likelihood and impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability.
- **Control**: a safeguard that reduces risk, such as multi-factor authentication.

## Five common myths

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| "We are too small to be a target." | Automated attacks do not check company size; they look for weak accounts and unpatched systems. |
| "Our antivirus protects us." | Antivirus helps with malware but does nothing against a convincing payment request or a stolen password. |
| "Our cloud provider handles security." | Providers secure their platform; you remain responsible for accounts, access, settings, and many backups. |
| "A complex password is enough." | Any password can leak; MFA protects you when it does (Chapter 4). |
| "Security is too expensive." | The most effective controls (MFA, verification rules, updates) cost little or nothing. |

## How to read the rest of this workbook

Each later chapter addresses one part of the exposure you estimate below. If time is short, start with Chapters 4 and 5 (accounts and payment fraud), which address the largest dollar losses for most small businesses, then work through the 90-day plan in Chapter 12.

:::example Illustrative: estimating exposure
A landscaping company with 12 employees pays suppliers about $40,000 a month by wire and ACH, holds $60,000 in its operating account, stores 2,000 customer records with addresses and payment details, and relies on scheduling software for daily operations.

Rough exposure estimates:
- A single fraudulent wire request could cost one month of supplier payments: about $40,000.
- Ransomware that stops scheduling for 5 days could cost revenue of about $3,000 a day: $15,000, plus recovery costs.
- A customer data breach could require notification, professional help, and lost trust; even at a modest $10 per record for response, 2,000 records cost about $20,000.

The total plausible exposure from these three scenarios is about $75,000, more than the company's operating cash. The point of the exercise is not precision; it is to show that a few hundred dollars a year in controls protects tens of thousands of dollars.
:::

# Chapter 2: Owning the Risk with a Simple Framework

:::objectives
- Describe the six functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0.
- Explain the leader's role in cybersecurity.
- Assign responsibilities in a small organization.
- Set priorities for the first year.
:::

Cybersecurity is a business risk, not only a technical one. CISA's Cyber Essentials, written for small-business leaders, encourages owners to approach cyber as a business risk, lead investment in basic cybersecurity, and develop security policies [4].

## The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

NIST's Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 organizes cybersecurity outcomes into six functions, and NIST's Small Business Quick-Start Guide adapts them for organizations with modest or no cybersecurity plans [3].

| Function | Question it answers | Small-business examples |
|---|---|---|
| Govern | Who decides, and what are our rules? | Name a responsible person; write simple policies |
| Identify | What do we have and what matters most? | Inventory devices, accounts, data, vendors |
| Protect | How do we prevent harm? | MFA, updates, backups, training |
| Detect | How would we notice a problem? | Account alerts, bank alerts, staff reporting |
| Respond | What do we do when something happens? | Incident plan, contacts, reporting |
| Recover | How do we get back to normal? | Restore from backups, review lessons |

This workbook follows the same order: governance in this chapter, identification in Chapter 3, protection in Chapters 4 through 10, and detection, response, and recovery in Chapter 11.

## Assigning responsibility

Even a two-person business needs a named **security lead**: the person who keeps the inventory, tracks the checklist, and coordinates help. This person need not be technical. In larger small businesses, responsibilities might be split:

| Role | Typical responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Owner or manager | Approves policies and budget; final decisions in incidents |
| Security lead | Maintains inventory and checklists; schedules reviews |
| Bookkeeper or finance lead | Applies payment verification procedures |
| IT provider (if any) | Configures systems, updates, backups |
| All staff | Follow policies; report suspicious activity |

:::example Illustrative: prioritizing with limited time
A bakery owner has about 4 hours a month for security. She lists candidate actions with estimated risk reduction (high, medium, low) and effort in hours:

| Action | Risk reduction | Effort (hours) |
|---|---|---|
| Turn on MFA for email, bank, and payment processor | High | 2 |
| Adopt a password manager | High | 3 |
| Payment verification rule | High | 1 |
| Automatic updates on all devices | Medium | 1 |
| Cloud backup of recipes and accounts | Medium | 2 |
| Staff phishing briefing | Medium | 1 |
| Full written policy set | Low (initially) | 8 |

She completes the high-impact, low-effort items first: MFA, payment verification, and automatic updates (4 hours) in month one; password manager and staff briefing (4 hours) in month two; backups in month three. The full policy set can wait until the basics are in place.
:::

:::checklist Governance basics
- A security lead is named and knows the role.
- The owner has reviewed this workbook's priorities and set a monthly time budget.
- Responsibilities for payments, IT, and reporting are written down.
- A date is set for a quarterly security review.
- Staff know whom to tell about suspicious messages or activity.
:::

:::summary
Cybersecurity is a leadership responsibility. The NIST CSF 2.0 organizes the work into six functions (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover), and NIST's Small Business Quick-Start Guide adapts them for small organizations. Name a security lead, assign responsibilities, and prioritize high-impact, low-effort controls first.
:::

# Chapter 3: Know What You Have

:::objectives
- Build an inventory of devices, accounts, data, and vendors.
- Classify data by sensitivity.
- Identify your most critical assets.
- Keep the inventory current.
:::

You cannot protect what you do not know you have. CISA recommends that organizations learn what is on their network by maintaining inventories of hardware and software assets [4], and NIST's quick-start guide recommends creating and maintaining an inventory of hardware, software, systems, and services [3].

## Four inventories

1. **Devices**: computers, phones, tablets, point-of-sale terminals, routers, printers, and other connected equipment.
2. **Accounts**: email, banking, payment processors, accounting, payroll, domain registrar, website hosting, social media, and cloud storage.
3. **Data**: what information you hold, where it lives, and who can access it.
4. **Vendors**: outside companies with access to your systems or data (Chapter 8).

## Classifying data

| Class | Examples | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| Restricted | Social Security numbers, bank account numbers, payment card data, health information, passwords | Minimize; encrypt; strict access; secure disposal |
| Confidential | Customer contact lists, pricing, payroll, contracts | Limit access; protect in storage and transit |
| Internal | Schedules, procedures, internal memos | Share within the business only |
| Public | Website content, published prices | No special handling |

## Critical assets

A **critical asset** is one whose loss would stop or seriously harm the business. For most small businesses, critical assets include the main email account (which can reset other passwords), bank and payment accounts, the domain name, and the system that runs daily operations (scheduling, point of sale, or practice management).

:::example Illustrative: inventory reveals hidden risk
A three-person accounting practice builds its account inventory and finds 38 business accounts. Of these, 9 were created by a former employee using her personal email address, 6 use the same password, and 4 (including the domain registrar) have no MFA. The registrar account matters most: whoever controls the domain controls the firm's email and website.

The practice transfers ownership of the 9 accounts to a shared business address, changes the 6 reused passwords, and enables MFA on the 4 unprotected accounts, starting with the registrar. Total time: about 5 hours. Before the inventory, none of these risks were visible.
:::

# Chapter 4: Accounts, Passwords, and Multi-Factor Authentication

:::objectives
- Explain why stolen credentials are the most common entry point for attackers.
- Set a password standard and adopt a password manager.
- Deploy multi-factor authentication, choosing stronger methods first.
- Apply least privilege and remove access promptly.
:::

If a criminal has your password, every other safeguard is beside the point: they simply log in. Passwords are stolen through phishing, guessed through automated attacks, or bought in batches leaked from other websites. Because people reuse passwords, a breach at an unrelated shopping site can open a business email account.

## Key terms

- **Credential**: the information that proves identity, usually a username and password.
- **Password manager**: software that generates, stores, and fills unique passwords, protected by one strong master password.
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**: a login that requires something beyond a password, such as a code from an authenticator app, a push approval, or a physical security key.
- **Least privilege**: giving each person only the access needed for their job.

## Passwords that hold up

CISA advises that every password be long, random, and unique, and recommends using a password manager to create and store them [10]. Length matters more than complexity: a passphrase of four or more random words is both stronger and easier to type than a short string of symbols. Uniqueness matters most of all, because a reused password turns one breach into many.

## The evidence for MFA

Microsoft reported in 2019 that MFA can block over 99.9 percent of account compromise attacks [6]. A later Microsoft Research study of real-world accounts found that MFA reduced the risk of compromise by 99.22 percent overall and by 98.56 percent even when the attacker already had a leaked password; it also found that authenticator apps performed better than SMS text codes [7]. MFA is not perfect, since attackers can trick people into approving prompts or reading out codes, but it is the single most effective control most small businesses can add.

## Choosing MFA methods

| Method | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hardware security key (FIDO2/passkey) | Strongest | Resists phishing; costs about $25 to $60 per key |
| Passkey on phone or computer | Very strong | Resists phishing; supported by major platforms |
| Authenticator app (codes or push with number matching) | Strong | Free; watch for repeated unexpected prompts |
| SMS text code | Fallback | Better than nothing; vulnerable to SIM swap |

## Where to start

Turn on MFA in this order: (1) main email and administrator accounts, (2) banking and payment processors, (3) accounting and payroll, (4) domain registrar and website host, (5) password manager, (6) social media and advertising accounts, (7) everything else that supports it.

:::case Colonial Pipeline: one password, no second factor
In May 2021, a ransomware attack led Colonial Pipeline to shut down the largest fuel pipeline in the United States. Testifying to the Senate, CEO Joseph Blount said attackers entered through a legacy virtual private network (VPN) account that used only a password, without multi-factor authentication; he described the password as complicated, "not a Colonial123-type password" [8]. Investigators from the firm Mandiant found that the account was no longer in active use and that its password had appeared in a batch of leaked passwords [8]. At the hearing, the company acknowledged paying a ransom of about $4.4 million [9].

The lesson for small businesses is direct. A strong password did not help once it leaked, and an unused account was never removed. A second factor, or simply disabling the old account, would have closed that door. Colonial's experience is its own; no claim is made here about what any other company will face.
:::

:::example Illustrative: cost of a password manager and keys
A 10-person business compares costs. A business password manager at about $4 per user per month costs 10 × $4 × 12 = $480 a year. Two hardware keys for each of the 3 administrators cost 6 × $40 = $240 once. MFA via authenticator app for everyone else is free.

First-year total: $720. Against the $40,000 single-wire exposure from Chapter 1, this is under 2 percent of one plausible loss. The time cost is about 1 hour per employee for setup, or 10 hours total.
:::

## Account recovery: the side door

Attackers who cannot get past MFA often try the account recovery process instead: they call a phone carrier to transfer your number to their SIM card (a "SIM swap"), or they target the personal email listed as a recovery address. Close this side door by:

- Setting recovery email addresses to business-controlled accounts that also have MFA.
- Adding a PIN or port-out lock with your mobile carrier.
- Storing printed backup codes for critical accounts in a locked location.
- Registering at least two MFA methods (for example, two hardware keys) for administrators, so a lost device does not lock you out.

## Handling MFA fatigue

Some attackers who already have a password send repeated push prompts, hoping a tired employee will tap "approve" to make them stop. Teach staff a simple rule: an approval request you did not trigger is an attack in progress. Deny it, then report it and change the password. Where available, turn on number matching, which requires typing a number shown on the login screen and defeats blind approvals.

## Least privilege and offboarding

CISA recommends ensuring that only those who belong on your systems have access, including using MFA, unique passwords, and least privilege, and promptly removing access for people who leave [5]. In practice:

- Use separate administrator accounts for configuration and standard accounts for daily work.
- Avoid shared logins; where unavoidable, store them in the password manager's shared vault.
- Remove access on the employee's last day, including email, cloud storage, and payment systems.
- Review all accounts quarterly and disable any not used in 90 days.

:::checklist Account protection
- Every staff member uses a password manager.
- All passwords are unique; reused passwords have been changed.
- MFA is on for email, banking, payments, accounting, payroll, domain, hosting, and the password manager.
- Administrators use phishing-resistant MFA (keys or passkeys) where available.
- Administrator accounts are separate from daily-use accounts.
- An offboarding checklist removes access on an employee's last day.
- Unused accounts are disabled during each quarterly review.
:::

:::summary
Stolen credentials are the most common way in. Use long, random, unique passwords stored in a password manager, and turn on MFA everywhere, starting with email, banking, and administrator accounts. Microsoft's research found MFA reduces compromise risk by over 99 percent. Colonial Pipeline's breach began with a leaked password on an unused account without MFA. Apply least privilege and remove access promptly.
:::

# Chapter 5: Email, Phishing, and Payment Fraud

:::objectives
- Recognize phishing and business email compromise.
- Build a payment verification procedure.
- Protect email accounts from takeover and hidden rules.
- Respond quickly when a fraudulent payment is sent.
:::

Email is where most attacks begin. A **phishing** message tries to trick the reader into clicking a link, opening an attachment, entering a password, or sending money. **Business email compromise (BEC)** is a targeted form of fraud in which a criminal impersonates an executive, vendor, or employee, often from a lookalike or hijacked account, to redirect a payment or payroll deposit. The IC3 reported about $3.05 billion in BEC losses in 2025 [1].

## Common patterns

| Pattern | What it looks like | Red flags |
|---|---|---|
| Vendor bank change | "We changed banks; please send future payments here" | New account details, urgency, email-only request |
| CEO request | "I need a wire sent today; I'm in a meeting" | Secrecy, urgency, unusual channel |
| Payroll diversion | "Please update my direct deposit" | Request by email rather than through HR system |
| Gift card request | "Buy gift cards for clients and send me the codes" | Gift cards as payment, secrecy |
| Fake invoice | Invoice for services never ordered | Unknown vendor, slight name variations |
| Credential phishing | "Your mailbox is full; log in here" | Lookalike login page, mismatched web address |

## The payment verification rule

The most effective defense against BEC is procedural, not technical. Adopt this rule in writing:

:::tip The verification rule
Any new payee, any change to payment details, and any payment above a set threshold must be confirmed by calling a known phone number already on file (never a number provided in the request itself) before money moves. A second person approves payments above the threshold.
:::

The FTC similarly advises businesses to verify payment requests and to train staff not to send money in response to unexpected requests without checking through a separate channel [22].

:::example Illustrative: setting a dual-approval threshold
A distributor makes about 120 payments a month. Most are under $2,500; 15 are between $2,500 and $25,000; and 2 exceed $25,000. Setting a dual-approval threshold at $2,500 means 17 payments a month need a second approver. At 3 minutes each, that adds 51 minutes a month.

If a single fraudulent $18,000 payment is prevented once every few years, the time cost (about 10 hours a year, roughly $400 at $40 per hour) is easily justified. A threshold set too low creates fatigue and rubber-stamping; set it where the second approval remains meaningful.
:::

## Protecting the mailbox itself

When attackers gain access to an email account, they often create hidden **forwarding rules** that copy or delete messages, letting them watch payment conversations and insert themselves at the right moment. Protect your mailbox by:

- Requiring MFA on all email accounts.
- Reviewing forwarding and inbox rules monthly and deleting any you did not create.
- Enabling alerts for logins from new locations.
- Turning on the email provider's built-in phishing and malware filtering.
- Marking messages from outside the organization with an "external" banner.
- Asking your IT provider or domain host to configure SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records, which help prevent criminals from sending email that appears to come from your domain.

## New tools, old tricks

Generative AI makes phishing messages more fluent and personalized, and voice-cloning tools can imitate a familiar voice on a phone call. Spelling mistakes and awkward grammar are no longer reliable warning signs. The defense does not change: verify through a channel you already trust. If "the owner" calls asking for an urgent transfer, hang up and call the owner back on the number you have stored. Some businesses agree on a private code word for urgent payment requests, which a cloned voice cannot know.

## Reading a suspicious message

Before acting on any message that asks for money, credentials, or sensitive data, pause and check:

1. Is the sender's full address correct, or only the display name?
2. Does the link go where it claims? (Hover or long-press to see the real address.)
3. Is the request unusual for this person or vendor?
4. Is there pressure to act quickly or keep it confidential?
5. Can I confirm through a separate, known channel?

Any "no" or "unsure" means stop and verify.

## If money was sent

Speed matters. Call your bank immediately and ask it to attempt a recall of the transfer; then file a complaint with the IC3 at ic3.gov [19]. Preserve the emails, record the timeline, and change passwords on any compromised account from a clean device. Banks may be able to stop or recover funds only within a short window.

:::summary
Phishing and business email compromise exploit trust and urgency. Adopt a written verification rule: confirm new payees and payment changes by calling a known number, and require dual approval above a threshold. Protect mailboxes with MFA, rule reviews, and domain authentication. If money is sent, call the bank immediately and report to the IC3.

# Chapter 6: Devices, Updates, and Networks

:::objectives
- Explain why software updates are a core security control.
- Configure devices with basic protections.
- Secure office Wi-Fi and remote work.
- Plan for replacing unsupported equipment.
:::

Every computer, phone, router, and point-of-sale terminal runs software, and software has flaws. Vendors release updates (also called patches) to fix them, and attackers study those updates to target anyone who has not installed them. CISA's Cyber Essentials lists protecting systems and applications, including keeping software patched and up to date, as a core element [13].

## Device baseline

| Control | What it means | How to apply |
|---|---|---|
| Automatic updates | Operating system and apps update themselves | Turn on in settings; restart weekly |
| Supported software | Vendor still releases security updates | Replace devices past end of support |
| Screen lock | Device locks after a few minutes idle | Set to 5 minutes or less with PIN or biometric |
| Disk encryption | Data unreadable if device is lost | Turn on BitLocker (Windows) or FileVault (Mac); phones encrypt by default when a passcode is set |
| Endpoint protection | Antivirus/anti-malware with central reporting | Use built-in tools or a business-grade product |
| Standard user accounts | Daily work without administrator rights | Use admin accounts only for installs |
| Remote locate and wipe | Erase a lost device remotely | Enable via device management or platform tools |

## Key terms

- **Patch**: a software update that fixes a flaw.
- **End of support**: the date after which a vendor stops providing security updates.
- **Encryption**: converting data so it can be read only with the right key.
- **Mobile device management (MDM)**: tools that let a business configure, monitor, and wipe devices centrally.

## Networks and Wi-Fi

Office networks deserve the same care. Change the router's default administrator password, keep its firmware updated, use WPA2 or WPA3 encryption, and run a separate guest network for customers and personal devices. Point-of-sale and other sensitive systems should be kept separate from guest traffic. Replace routers that no longer receive firmware updates.

For remote work, require device encryption, screen locks, and updates on any device that accesses business data. Public Wi-Fi is less dangerous than it once was because most websites encrypt traffic, but staff should avoid entering sensitive credentials on unfamiliar networks and prefer a phone hotspot when handling payments.

:::example Illustrative: budgeting for device replacement
A firm has 14 laptops. Five are seven years old and will lose operating system support within a year. Replacing them at $900 each costs $4,500. Spreading replacement over a four-year cycle means budgeting about 14 ÷ 4 = 3.5 laptops a year, or roughly $3,150 annually.

Delaying replacement may save money in the short term, but unsupported devices receive no security fixes. The firm schedules the five oldest for replacement this year and the rest on the four-year cycle.
:::

:::case Target and the cost of a flat network
The 2013 Target breach, discussed in detail in Chapter 8, also illustrates a network lesson. The Senate Commerce Committee's analysis found that after entering through a vendor's credentials, attackers moved from a vendor-facing system to Target's point-of-sale systems, and it identified the company's failure to adequately isolate sensitive network assets as a key weakness [11]. Separating guest, business, and payment networks is a small-scale version of the same principle: a compromise in one area should not reach everything. Target's outcome is its own; the case is offered only to illustrate the design principle.
:::

:::checklist Devices and network
- Automatic updates are on for every device and application.
- No device is running an operating system past end of support.
- Every laptop and phone has a screen lock and encryption.
- Endpoint protection is active and reporting.
- Staff use standard accounts for daily work.
- The router admin password has been changed and firmware is current.
- Wi-Fi uses WPA2 or WPA3, with a separate guest network.
- Payment systems are separated from guest traffic.
- Lost or stolen devices can be located and wiped.
:::

:::summary
Updates fix known flaws, so automatic updates and supported software are basic requirements. Apply a device baseline of screen locks, encryption, endpoint protection, and standard user accounts. Secure the router, separate guest and payment traffic, and budget a replacement cycle so no device runs unsupported software.
:::

# Chapter 7: Backups and Ransomware Readiness

:::objectives
- Explain how ransomware works and why backups are the main defense.
- Apply the 3-2-1 backup rule.
- Test restores and measure recovery time.
- Decide in advance how to handle a ransom demand.
:::

**Ransomware** is malicious software that encrypts files or systems and demands payment to unlock them; many groups also steal data and threaten to publish it. CISA's StopRansomware guide recommends maintaining offline, encrypted backups of critical data and regularly testing their availability and integrity [15]. CISA's Cyber Essentials likewise calls for backups that are kept separate from the main network, so ransomware cannot reach them [14].

## The 3-2-1 rule

Keep **3** copies of important data, on **2** different types of storage, with **1** copy offsite or offline. A cloud backup service with version history plus a periodically disconnected external drive meets this rule for most small businesses.

## Key terms

- **Backup**: a copy of data that can be used to restore the original.
- **Versioning**: keeping older versions, so you can restore files from before an infection.
- **Recovery time objective (RTO)**: how quickly a system must be restored.
- **Recovery point objective (RPO)**: how much recent data you can afford to lose, measured in time.
- **Immutable backup**: a backup that cannot be changed or deleted for a set period.

## Backups for cloud services

Do not assume your cloud provider backs up everything. Software-as-a-service providers protect their infrastructure, but accidental or malicious deletion by a user may be your responsibility. Review the retention and restore options for email, file storage, and accounting systems, and consider a third-party backup for critical cloud data.

| System | RPO target | RTO target | Backup method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accounting files | 1 day | 1 day | Provider export plus cloud backup |
| Customer database | 1 day | 4 hours | Nightly cloud backup with versioning |
| Email | 1 day | 1 day | Provider retention plus third-party backup |
| Shared files | 1 hour | 1 day | Cloud sync with version history |
| Point of sale | Real time | 4 hours | Vendor-hosted; confirm offline mode |

:::example Illustrative: the value of a restore test
A dental office backs up its practice management data nightly. During a quarterly restore test, staff discover that the backup job had been failing silently for 47 days because a password changed. Restoring from the last good backup would have meant re-entering 47 days of appointments and billing, about 1,400 patient visits.

After fixing the job, the office adds a weekly email alert confirming success and a monthly test restore of one file. The test took 2 hours; discovering the failure during an actual ransomware attack would have cost far more.
:::

## Deciding about ransom in advance

Paying a ransom does not guarantee recovery, may fund further crime, and can raise legal issues. CISA and the FBI do not encourage paying a ransom, and they ask victims to report incidents [15]. Make the decision framework part of your incident plan now: who decides, whom you consult (insurer, legal counsel, law enforcement), and what your backups allow you to restore without paying.

:::summary
Ransomware encrypts or steals data for extortion, and tested backups are the main defense. Follow the 3-2-1 rule with at least one offline or immutable copy, set recovery time and recovery point objectives, confirm cloud services are backed up, and test restores regularly. # Chapter 8: Vendors and Third Parties

:::objectives
- Identify vendors with access to your systems or data.
- Assess vendor risk with a simple tiering method.
- Apply access controls to vendor accounts.
- Include security in vendor contracts and offboarding.
:::

Small businesses rely on outside providers for bookkeeping, IT support, payroll, software, and maintenance. Each provider with access to your systems or data extends your security boundary. A vendor's weakness can become your breach.

:::case Target: entry through an HVAC vendor
In late 2013, attackers stole payment card and personal data from Target. The Senate Commerce Committee's "Kill Chain" analysis found that attackers first stole credentials from Fazio Mechanical Services, a refrigeration and HVAC contractor that had access to a Target vendor system for billing, contract submission, and project management, likely through malware delivered in email at least two months before the breach [11]. CBS News reported that the breach affected as many as 110 million customers, that Fazio had used a free consumer-grade anti-malware product, and that the report concluded requiring two-factor authentication for vendor access might have stopped the attack [12].

The lessons for a small business work in both directions. As a customer, limit what vendors can reach and require MFA on their accounts. As a vendor to larger companies, your own security protects your clients and your contracts. Target's and Fazio's experiences are their own, cited only for what the public record shows.
:::

## Vendor tiers

| Tier | Description | Examples | Review |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1: Critical | Access to money, restricted data, or administrator rights | IT provider, payroll, bookkeeper, payment processor | Annual questionnaire; MFA required; contract terms |
| 2: Important | Access to confidential data or business systems | CRM, scheduling, marketing agency | Check security page; MFA required |
| 3: Limited | No system or sensitive data access | Office supplies, cleaning | Basic due diligence |

## Questions for critical vendors

1. Do you require MFA for all your staff and for accounts that access our systems?
2. How do you protect and back up our data, and where is it stored?
3. Will you notify us promptly if you have a security incident affecting our data?
4. Who on your team can access our systems, and how is access removed when they leave?
5. Do you carry cyber liability insurance?
6. Can you provide a security summary or independent assessment (for example, a SOC 2 report)?

:::example Illustrative: right-sizing vendor access
A property management firm gives its IT provider a permanent administrator account, its bookkeeper full access to online banking, and a marketing agency the main social media password. After tiering, the firm makes three changes: the IT provider gets a named account with MFA and activity logging; the bookkeeper's banking access is changed to "prepare but not release" payments, so releases require the owner; and the marketing agency is added as a partner in the social media platform's business manager rather than using the shared password.

Time required: about 3 hours. Each change narrows what a compromised vendor account could do.
:::

:::checklist Vendor controls
- Every vendor with system or data access is listed in the vendor inventory.
- Vendors are tiered as critical, important, or limited.
- Critical vendors have answered the six questions.
- Vendor accounts are named individually and protected with MFA.
- Vendors have only the access their work requires.
- Contracts include breach notification and data return or deletion.
- Access is removed promptly when a vendor relationship ends.
:::

:::summary
Vendors extend your security boundary. The Target breach began with credentials stolen from an HVAC contractor. Tier vendors by access, ask critical vendors about MFA, data protection, and incident notification, give each vendor named and limited access with MFA, and remove access promptly when relationships end.
:::

# Chapter 9: Protecting Customer and Employee Data

:::objectives
- Apply the FTC's five principles for protecting personal information.
- Minimize the data you collect and keep.
- Store, share, and dispose of sensitive data safely.
- Recognize when special legal requirements apply.
:::

Customers and employees trust you with their personal information. The FTC's guide "Protecting Personal Information" organizes a sound data security plan around five principles: take stock, scale down, lock it, pitch it, and plan ahead [17].

## The five principles

| Principle | Meaning | Practical step |
|---|---|---|
| Take stock | Know what personal information you have and where | Use the data inventory from Chapter 3 |
| Scale down | Keep only what you need | Stop collecting unnecessary data; delete old records |
| Lock it | Protect what you keep | Encryption, access controls, locked cabinets |
| Pitch it | Dispose of what you no longer need | Shred paper; securely wipe devices |
| Plan ahead | Prepare to respond to incidents | Incident plan (Chapter 11) |

The FTC's "Start with Security" guidance adds lessons drawn from its enforcement cases, such as not collecting personal information you do not need, restricting access to sensitive data, requiring secure passwords and authentication, and making sure service providers implement reasonable security [21].

## Minimization in practice

The safest data is data you never collected. Ask of each field on every form: do we need this, and for how long? Common reductions include not storing full card numbers (use your payment processor's tokenization instead), truncating Social Security numbers where only identity confirmation is needed, and deleting applicant files after the legally required retention period.

## Key terms

- **Personally identifiable information (PII)**: information that can identify a specific person, alone or combined with other data.
- **Tokenization**: replacing sensitive data, such as a card number, with a non-sensitive substitute held by a payment processor.
- **Retention schedule**: a written list of how long each type of record is kept.
- **Secure disposal**: destroying data so it cannot be recovered.

:::example Illustrative: shrinking the data footprint
A fitness studio stores 6,500 member records, including 4,200 former members who left over three years ago. Records include birth dates, emergency contacts, and in some cases full card numbers saved in a spreadsheet from an old billing process.

The studio deletes the spreadsheet after confirming its processor holds tokenized payment details, sets a retention schedule of 3 years after membership ends, and removes 4,200 stale records. Its record count drops from 6,500 to 2,300, a 65 percent reduction in what could be exposed in a breach.
:::

## Special requirements

Some data carries legal obligations beyond general good practice: payment card data (industry standards from the PCI Security Standards Council), health information (HIPAA for covered entities and their business associates), customer financial information for certain financial businesses (the FTC Safeguards Rule), and children's information. Every U.S. state also has breach notification laws. If any apply to you, consult a qualified professional.

:::worksheet Data retention schedule

Employee and payroll records kept for: ___
Applicant files kept for: ___
Tax and financial records kept for: ___
Where full card numbers or Social Security numbers appear today: ___
Method of disposing of paper and old devices: ___
:::

:::summary
Follow the FTC's five principles: take stock, scale down, lock it, pitch it, and plan ahead. Minimize collection, use tokenization rather than storing card numbers, set a retention schedule, and dispose of data securely. Recognize when special legal requirements apply and get qualified advice.
:::

# Chapter 10: People, Training, and Culture

:::objectives
- Explain why people are both the main target and the main defense.
- Design short, recurring security training.
- Build a reporting culture without blame.
- Write simple, usable security policies.
:::

Attackers target people because people can be persuaded. CISA's Cyber Essentials identifies staff as a central element of security and recommends developing a security-aware culture, including training and making staff aware of how to report suspicious activity [4][20].

## Effective training

Long annual presentations are quickly forgotten. Short, frequent, practical sessions work better.

| Element | Format | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Onboarding briefing | 30 minutes: passwords, MFA, phishing, reporting | Each new hire |
| Monthly tip | 5-minute discussion at a team meeting | Monthly |
| Phishing practice | Realistic sample messages discussed together | Quarterly |
| Payment fraud drill | Walk through a fake vendor bank change | Twice a year |
| Policy acknowledgment | Review and sign key policies | Annually |

## A blameless reporting culture

The most valuable security habit is reporting quickly. An employee who clicked a bad link and reports it within five minutes allows a password reset before damage occurs; an employee who hides it out of embarrassment may allow weeks of access. Thank people for reports, including false alarms, and never punish an honest mistake that was reported promptly.

## Policies that people actually read

Policies should fit on a page or two and use plain language. A small business can start with five: acceptable use, passwords and MFA, payment verification, remote work and devices, and incident reporting. Appendix F provides a template for a one-page policy.

:::example Illustrative: measuring reporting
A 20-person firm discusses sample phishing messages quarterly. In the first quarter, staff correctly identify 11 of 20 suspicious examples on average (55 percent) and reported 2 real suspicious emails that month. After three quarters of short practice sessions, the average reaches 17 of 20 (85 percent), and real reports rise to 9 a month.

Rising reports are a good sign: they indicate staff are noticing and speaking up. The firm tracks reports rather than penalizing clicks.
:::

:::checklist Training and culture
- Every new hire receives a security briefing in the first week.
- Security is discussed briefly at least monthly.
- Staff know exactly how and to whom to report suspicious activity.
- Reports are thanked, not punished.
- A payment fraud drill is run at least twice a year.
- Core policies are written in plain language and acknowledged annually.
:::

:::summary
People are the main target and the main defense. Replace long annual training with short, frequent sessions, practice recognizing phishing and payment fraud, and build a culture where fast reporting is thanked. Keep policies short and readable.
:::

# Chapter 11: Detecting, Responding, and Recovering

:::objectives
- Set up simple detection through alerts and reporting.
- Write a one-page incident response plan.
- Follow the first steps for common incidents.
- Report incidents and recover with lessons learned.
:::

No set of controls is perfect. The NIST CSF 2.0 includes Detect, Respond, and Recover functions because preparation determines how much an incident costs [3]. CISA's Cyber Essentials similarly asks leaders to limit damage and restore normal operations quickly, which requires an incident plan that is written and practiced in advance [4].

## Detection for small businesses

Large companies run security operations centers; small businesses can rely on alerts that are already available:

- Login alerts from email, banking, and payment providers.
- Bank alerts for payments above a threshold or to new payees.
- Endpoint protection alerts.
- Backup success and failure notifications.
- Staff reports of unusual messages, pop-ups, or slow devices.
- Customer reports of strange emails appearing to come from you.

Route alerts to a monitored address, not to a former employee's inbox.

## The incident response plan

A one-page plan answers five questions: who leads, whom to call, what to do first, what to record, and when to notify others. Appendix E provides a contact sheet.

| Incident | First steps |
|---|---|
| Fraudulent payment sent | Call bank fraud line immediately; request recall; report at ic3.gov; preserve emails |
| Email account compromised | Reset password from a clean device; sign out all sessions; check forwarding rules; enable MFA; warn contacts |
| Ransomware or suspicious encryption | Disconnect affected device from network (do not power off or wipe); call IT provider and insurer; do not use affected backups until checked |
| Lost or stolen device | Remote lock and wipe; change passwords stored on it; record what data it held |
| Customer data exposed | Contain; preserve evidence; consult legal counsel on notification; follow FTC breach response guidance |

The FTC's "Data Breach Response" guide advises businesses to secure operations, fix vulnerabilities, and notify appropriate parties, including law enforcement, affected businesses, and individuals as required by law [18].

## Reporting

Report cyber-enabled crime to the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov [19]. Reporting helps law enforcement and, in payment fraud cases, may help with recovery efforts. Notify your cyber insurer promptly, as most policies require early notice. Breach notification obligations depend on state law and the data involved.

:::example Illustrative: the cost of an hour
A bookkeeper notices at 9:10 a.m. that a vendor bank change email she acted on yesterday looks wrong; a $22,000 payment was sent at 4:30 p.m. the previous day. Following the plan, she calls the bank's fraud line at 9:15 and files an IC3 complaint by 10:00.

Compare two paths. Calling within the hour gives the bank the best opportunity to attempt a recall before funds are moved onward. Waiting until the owner returns from vacation two days later would likely make recovery much harder. A written plan turns a moment of panic into a five-minute phone call. No recovery is guaranteed in either case; speed improves the odds.
:::

## Running a tabletop exercise

A tabletop exercise is a 30- to 60-minute discussion in which the team walks through a realistic scenario using the incident plan. It reveals gaps, such as a missing phone number or an unclear decision-maker, while the stakes are low. Choose a scenario, read it aloud in stages, and ask at each stage: what do we do now, who does it, and what do we need? Record gaps and assign fixes. Appendix H offers three ready-made scenarios.

| Stage | Prompt | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery | "A staff member reports a ransom note on the front-desk computer." | Does everyone know whom to call? |
| Containment | "Two more computers show the same note." | Who disconnects devices? Is the plan accessible offline? |
| Decision | "The attackers claim to have customer data." | Who contacts insurer and counsel? |
| Recovery | "Backups exist, but the last test was a year ago." | How long would restore take? |
| Communication | "A customer calls asking if their data is safe." | Who speaks for the business, and what do they say? |

## Recovery and lessons learned

After an incident, restore from known-good backups, reset credentials, and confirm that the root cause is fixed. Within two weeks, hold a short, blameless review: what happened, what worked, what did not, and what will change. Update the inventory, checklists, and training accordingly.

:::worksheet Incident first hour
Who leads the response: ___
Backup leader if unavailable: ___
IT provider contact: ___
Bank fraud line: ___
Cyber insurer claim line and policy number: ___
Legal counsel: ___
Where the incident log is kept: ___
:::

:::summary
Use existing alerts and staff reports for detection, write a one-page incident plan, and follow first steps for common incidents: call the bank for payment fraud, reset and check rules for email compromise, and disconnect devices for ransomware. Report to the IC3 and your insurer, restore from clean backups, and hold a blameless review.

# Glossary

**Business email compromise (BEC)**: fraud in which a criminal impersonates a trusted person by email to redirect a payment or obtain sensitive information.

**Control**: a safeguard that reduces risk.

**Credential**: information that proves identity, typically a username and password.

**DMARC, DKIM, and SPF**: email authentication standards that help prevent others from sending email that appears to come from your domain.

**Encryption**: converting data into a form readable only with the correct key.

**End of support**: the date after which a vendor stops providing security updates for a product.

**Endpoint protection**: software that detects and blocks malware on devices.

**Immutable backup**: a backup that cannot be altered or deleted for a defined period.

**Least privilege**: granting each person only the access needed for their role.

**Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**: a login method requiring two or more types of evidence of identity.

**Passkey**: a phishing-resistant login credential stored on a device and unlocked with a PIN or biometric.

**Password manager**: software that creates, stores, and fills unique passwords.

**Patch**: a software update that fixes a flaw.

**Phishing**: a deceptive message designed to trick a recipient into revealing information, clicking a link, or sending money.

**Personally identifiable information (PII)**: information that can identify a specific individual.

**Ransomware**: malicious software that encrypts or steals data and demands payment.

**Recovery point objective (RPO)**: the maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured in time.

**Recovery time objective (RTO)**: the maximum acceptable time to restore a system.

**Risk**: the likelihood and impact of a threat exploiting a vulnerability.

**Threat**: a potential cause of harm.

**Tokenization**: replacing sensitive data with a non-sensitive substitute.

**Vulnerability**: a weakness that a threat could exploit.
