# How to Use This Book

Owners of small businesses rarely lack effort. What they lack is attention that is directed on purpose. An owner can spend a full day answering questions, approving small purchases, and putting out fires, and finish the day with the business no easier to run than it was that morning. The problem is not laziness; it is a system that sends every decision and interruption to one person.

This book offers a different system. It draws on management classics, psychological research on goals and interruptions, labor economics on working hours, and the public practices of entrepreneurs and companies who have experimented with their own time. It is written for owners of very small businesses, solo professionals, and managers of small teams who want to get the right work done without working longer.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this book you should be able to:

- Record and analyze where an ordinary week actually goes.
- Choose a small number of results that matter each week.
- Build a calendar with protected time, buffers, and recovery.
- Capture commitments in one trusted list and plan next actions.
- Reduce avoidable interruptions while routing real problems quickly.
- Delegate outcomes using documented processes.
- Run fewer, better meetings.
- Set a working pace you can sustain.
- Use tools, automation, and AI assistants with sensible guardrails.
- Hold a weekly review that keeps the system working.
- Make decisions quickly without becoming the bottleneck.
- Plan your work around energy, sleep, and recovery.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives and an explanation, followed by a step-by-step framework, an illustrative worked example, a real case study with sources, at least one table, a worksheet or checklist, a summary, and review questions. Worked examples are hypothetical and their figures invented for teaching. Case studies describe real people, companies, and research from public sources listed in the References; their results are their own, not TTI's.

A word on tone: productivity advice often promises to double your output or add hours to your day. This book makes no such promises. The research it cites shows real but bounded effects, and it shows that the right answer for many owners is to do less, not more.

:::warning Scope and limits
This book is education only. It is not medical, psychological, legal, tax, or employment advice. No increase in income, sales, or free time is promised. If you have persistent problems with sleep, stress, mood, or health, consult a qualified professional. Employment law governs hours, breaks, and pay for employees; check with your state labor agency or an employment attorney before changing schedules or classifying workers.
:::

# Chapter 1: See Where Your Time Actually Goes

:::objectives
- Explain why a time record should come before a time plan.
- Conduct a two-week time and energy audit.
- Classify activities by value, energy, and the cost of handing them off.
- Identify the patterns that consume your week.
:::

## Start with the record, not the plan

Peter Drucker, one of the founders of modern management thought, argued in The Effective Executive that effective executives do not start with their tasks; they start by finding out where their time actually goes, through a three-step process of recording time, managing time, and consolidating time [1]. Most owners skip the first step. They plan a perfect week based on what they think they do, and the plan fails because it ignores the hidden load: supplier calls, searches for missing information, rework, and requests that only reach the owner because no one else knows the answer.

Memory is a poor guide to time. People tend to remember the dramatic events of a week and forget the small, repeated ones. A five-minute question asked ten times a week is nearly an hour, and it may break up three longer tasks. Only a record reveals this.

## What to record

A useful audit does not require minute-by-minute timing. Three or four times a day, take a minute to note what you mainly did in the previous block, using broad categories:

| Category | Examples | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Customer work | Delivering the service, producing the product | Could someone else do parts of this? |
| Sales and marketing | Quotes, calls, follow-up, content | Which activities produced customers? |
| Owner decisions | Pricing, hiring, strategy | Does this truly require the owner? |
| Administration | Invoices, bookkeeping, scheduling | Could this be automated or delegated? |
| Interruptions | Questions, calls, problems | Which were preventable with a process? |
| Waiting | Blocked by a supplier, customer, or approval | Could a follow-up date replace worry? |

Mark two more attributes for each block: whether it gave you energy or drained it, and roughly how expensive it would be to pay someone else to do it.

:::case Dan Martell's time and energy audit
Dan Martell, a software entrepreneur, coach, and author of Buy Back Your Time, described his audit on the Startups for the Rest of Us podcast. He said that roughly every four months he audits his calendar for two weeks, highlighting in red the activities that drain his energy and in green those that energize him, and annotating each with one to four dollar signs to indicate how inexpensive or expensive it would be to pay someone else to do it [2]. He then gathers the red, one-dollar-sign items and looks for someone else to do them first [2]. Martell's results are his own, not TTI's, and his business operates at a scale far beyond most readers'. The method is useful at any scale because it combines three questions owners rarely ask together: How much time does this take? How does it affect my energy? What would it cost to hand off?
:::

## Reading the audit

At the end of the record, look for patterns rather than totals. Ask three questions adapted from the original edition of this guide. What work did only I need to do? What work happened because a process was missing? What work did I add even though no one needed it? The last two categories are usually the best places to reduce load. Note unusual events so that an abnormal week is not mistaken for a normal one.

## The audit framework

**Step 1: Choose two ordinary weeks** and note any unusual events.

**Step 2: Record your main activity** three or four times a day in broad categories.

**Step 3: Mark energy** (green for energizing, red for draining) and hand-off cost (one to four dollar signs).

**Step 4: Total time by category** and identify the three largest consumers.

**Step 5: Highlight red, low-cost items** as first candidates to delegate, automate, or eliminate.

**Step 6: Identify missing processes** that cause repeated interruptions.

:::example Illustrative: the lost mornings
A hypothetical owner of a counter-supply store plans to prepare quotes every morning. A two-week audit shows that four mornings went to supplier calls, two to urgent customer issues, and four to searching old email for current item prices. Of roughly 40 hours a week, she spends about 7 on searches and calls that a single, dated price sheet and an afternoon call window could reduce. She cannot remove urgent customer issues, but she can route them. Her first change is the price sheet; she will compare the next two weeks before changing anything else. Figures are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choose What Matters This Week

:::objectives
- Distinguish urgent from important work.
- Set one to three weekly results.
- Use a simple priority matrix without treating it as dogma.
- Connect weekly results to longer-term goals.
:::

## Urgent versus important

The most common productivity failure is not slowness; it is spending the week on urgent but unimportant work. Urgent work announces itself through phone calls, messages, and customers at the counter. Important work, such as improving a process, training a team member, or building a marketing channel, rarely announces itself, and so it waits.

:::case Eisenhower and the urgent versus the important
The popular "Eisenhower matrix" is named after President Dwight D. Eisenhower. Quote Investigator traced the idea to a 1954 address Eisenhower gave at Northwestern University to the Second Assembly of the World Council of Churches, in which he quoted an unnamed former college president: "I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent" [4]. Quote Investigator notes that Eisenhower did not claim credit for the saying and that the familiar "seldom" wording appears in a listener's later recollection rather than the speech transcript [4]. Eisenhower's career and results are his own, not TTI's. The case shows two things: the distinction between urgent and important is old and widely recognized, and popular productivity attributions are often simplified. A careful reader checks sources, even for productivity slogans.
:::

## The priority matrix

| | Urgent | Not urgent |
|---|---|---|
| Important | Do now, then ask how to prevent recurrence | Schedule protected time |
| Not important | Route, delegate, or batch | Eliminate or defer |

The matrix is a thinking aid, not a law. Many urgent tasks really are important: a safety problem, a payroll error, or a major customer complaint. The point is to ask the question before reacting, and to notice when important, non-urgent work has been missing from the calendar for weeks.

## Weekly results, not long lists

A weekly result is a specific outcome you intend to finish this week, stated so that you will know on Friday whether it happened. "Work on marketing" is an activity. "Send the revised quote template to five past customers" is a result. One to three results is enough; more than that becomes a wish list.

| Weak (activity) | Strong (result) |
|---|---|
| Work on hiring | Post the job description and screen first 10 applicants |
| Improve bookkeeping | Reconcile October bank statement |
| Marketing | Publish two customer-question posts and reply to all comments |
| Fix scheduling | Write and test the new booking confirmation message |

## The weekly-result framework

**Step 1: Review longer-term goals** for the quarter.

**Step 2: List candidate results** that would move those goals forward.

**Step 3: Sort candidates** with the priority matrix.

**Step 4: Choose one to three results** stated as finished outcomes.

**Step 5: Estimate time required** and check it against your realistic available time.

**Step 6: Schedule the first work session** for each result before the week begins.

:::example Illustrative: choosing among competing priorities
A hypothetical bakery owner lists twelve possible tasks for the week. Using the matrix, she sees that a broken walk-in cooler is urgent and important and that renewing her food-handler certificate is important but not yet urgent. Updating the website menu is neither. She chooses three results: cooler repaired and a maintenance contract signed; wholesale price sheet sent to four cafés; certificate renewal scheduled. Her audit shows about 8 hours of truly flexible time, so she drops a fourth result rather than pretending to fit it. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::summary
Urgent work crowds out important work unless the owner chooses results deliberately. The urgent-important distinction, associated with Eisenhower's 1954 remarks, is a useful thinking aid. # Chapter 3: Build a Calendar With Real Space in It

:::objectives
- Explain time blocking and its benefits.
- Build a weekly template with focus, administration, and buffer time.
- Plan for uncertainty and replan without guilt.
- Protect time for recovery and life outside work.
:::

## From list to calendar

A task list says what to do; a calendar says when. Many owners keep a list and react to whatever arrives between appointments. Cal Newport, a computer science professor at Georgetown and author of Deep Work, calls this the list/reactive method and recommends instead that people give every minute of the workday a job, a technique he calls time blocking [5]. Newport has written that running a day from a to-do list or an inbox leads to sub-optimal returns and that in his experience serious time blocking can roughly double productive output compared with the list/reactive approach [6].

:::case Cal Newport's time blocking
Newport has described time blocking on his blog since at least 2013 and popularized it in his 2016 book Deep Work [5]. In his method, the workday is divided into blocks, each assigned specific work; when the day changes, as it often does, he redraws the remaining blocks rather than abandoning the plan, because, in his words, the key is maintaining "intention" about your time, not "perfection" in your planning [5][6]. Newport's results are his own, not TTI's, and his claim about doubling output is a personal estimate, not a controlled study. The approach is valuable for owners because it forces a reckoning with capacity: once every hour has a job, it becomes obvious that a 30-item list cannot fit into a day with five hours of open time.
:::

## Designing a weekly template

A weekly template is a default arrangement of time that you adjust each week. It should include:

| Block type | Purpose | Typical length | Placement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focus | Weekly results; work requiring concentration | 60–120 minutes | Your highest-energy hours |
| Customer and team | Appointments, service, check-ins | Varies | Predictable windows |
| Administration | Email, invoices, approvals | 30–60 minutes, once or twice daily | Lower-energy times |
| Buffer | Overruns, urgent issues | 15–30 minutes after major blocks | Throughout the day |
| Recovery | Breaks, meals, exercise | 15–60 minutes | Fixed and protected |
| Weekly review | Planning the next week | 45–60 minutes | Same time weekly |

The original edition of this guide made a point worth repeating: the goal is not to squeeze a task into every free minute. A schedule that has no room for overruns, uncertain work, and life outside the business will fail and leave the owner feeling defeated.

## Replanning without guilt

Plans change. A customer emergency or a sick employee can consume a morning. When that happens, the time-blocking approach is simply to redraw the rest of the day's blocks, moving or dropping lower-priority work. Replanning is not failure; it is the system working.

## The calendar framework

**Step 1: Mark fixed commitments** such as appointments, school pickup, and meals.

**Step 2: Place focus blocks** for weekly results in your best hours.

**Step 3: Add administration blocks** to batch email and small tasks.

**Step 4: Insert buffers** after major blocks and leave at least 20 percent of the day unassigned.

**Step 5: Protect recovery time** and an end-of-day stop time.

**Step 6: Replan** at midday or whenever a major disruption occurs.

:::example Illustrative: a contractor's weekly template
A hypothetical electrical contractor blocks 6:30 to 8:00 a.m. for estimates (focus), 8:00 to 3:30 for job sites, 3:30 to 4:15 for calls and email (administration), and 4:15 to 4:45 as a buffer. Friday afternoon holds a 60-minute weekly review. He leaves Wednesday morning unassigned for overruns. In his first month, his audit shows that estimates go out in an average of two days instead of five. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::worksheet Weekly calendar template
Fixed commitments: ___
Best focus hours: ___
Focus blocks (days and times): ___
Administration blocks: ___
Buffer blocks: ___
Recovery and meals: ___
Daily stop time: ___
Weekly review time: ___

:::

:::summary
Time blocking, popularized by Cal Newport, assigns work to specific blocks on the calendar and replans when the day changes. A weekly template places focus work in high-energy hours, batches administration, includes buffers and recovery, and leaves room for uncertainty. A calendar that cannot absorb disruption is not a useful plan.
:::

# Chapter 4: Use One Task List You Can Trust

:::objectives
- Explain why unfinished commitments occupy attention.
- Build a single capture system and trusted list.
- Write tasks as concrete next actions.
- Separate active tasks, waiting items, and someday ideas.
:::

## Why open loops drain attention

Psychologists have long observed that unfinished goals tend to stay on the mind, a pattern often associated with the Zeigarnik effect. For an owner, this means that every uncaptured promise ("I'll send you that price tomorrow") occupies attention until it is either done or reliably recorded. Owners who keep commitments in their heads, in three notebooks, and in scattered text threads experience the predictable result: constant low-level worry and occasional dropped balls.

:::case Plan making and unfulfilled goals
Psychologists E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister reported in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2011 that unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts during an unrelated reading task, high mental accessibility of goal-related words, and poorer performance on an unrelated anagram task [7]. When participants were allowed to formulate specific plans for their unfulfilled goals, these interference effects were eliminated; the authors concluded that committing to a specific plan may not only help attain a goal but also free cognitive resources for other pursuits [7]. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's, and were obtained in laboratory studies rather than businesses. The practical lesson for owners is modest but useful: you do not have to finish everything to quiet your mind, but you do need a specific plan for each open commitment.
:::

## One capture point, one trusted list

A trusted system has two parts. A capture point is where new commitments land the moment they arise: a notebook in your pocket, a phone app, or a voice note. A trusted list is where captured items are processed into clear tasks with dates or contexts. "Trusted" means you review it often enough that you no longer feel the need to remember everything.

| List | What belongs there | Review frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Inbox (capture) | Anything new, unprocessed | Daily |
| Next actions | Concrete tasks you can do | Daily |
| Calendar | Time-specific commitments | Daily |
| Waiting for | Items blocked by others, with a check date | Weekly |
| Projects | Outcomes requiring several steps | Weekly |
| Someday/maybe | Ideas not committed to | Monthly |

## Writing next actions

A next action is the very next physical step, specific enough that you could start it without further thought. "Insurance" is a topic. "Call Maria at the agency to ask for a renewal quote on the van policy" is a next action. Vague tasks are procrastinated because the first step of thinking about them has not been done.

## The trusted-list framework

**Step 1: Choose one capture point** you always carry.

**Step 2: Gather every open commitment** from notebooks, email, texts, and memory into the inbox.

**Step 3: Process each item**: do it if it takes under two minutes, delegate it, schedule it, put it on the next-actions list, or discard it.

**Step 4: Rewrite tasks as next actions** with verbs.

**Step 5: Move blocked items** to "waiting for" with a check date.

**Step 6: Review daily and weekly** so the list stays trusted.

:::example Illustrative: clearing the backlog
A hypothetical cleaning-company owner spends one afternoon gathering commitments from two notebooks, 140 unread emails, and her memory. She records 63 items. Eleven take under two minutes and are done immediately; 9 are delegated to her office manager; 14 go on the calendar; 17 become next actions; 6 move to "waiting for" with check dates; and 6 are discarded. She reports that the following week she stops waking at night to remember tasks, though she knows that is her own experience and not a guaranteed effect. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Trusted list
- One capture point chosen and always available.
- All open commitments gathered.
- Each item processed with the five options.
- Tasks written as verb-first next actions.
- Waiting items have check dates.
- Daily review scheduled.
- Weekly review scheduled.
:::

:::summary
Unfinished commitments occupy attention, but research by Masicampo and Baumeister suggests that making specific plans for them can reduce that interference. A single capture point and a trusted, regularly reviewed list of next actions, calendar items, waiting items, and projects free attention for the work at hand.
:::

# Chapter 5: Manage Interruptions Without Blocking Real Problems

:::objectives
- Describe the costs of interrupted work.
- Classify interruptions as preventable, routable, or essential.
- Design communication windows and escalation rules.
- Reduce self-interruption.
:::

## The hidden cost of interruptions

Interruptions are unavoidable in a small business; customers, employees, and suppliers need answers. But not every interruption needs the owner, and not every one needs an answer right now. Interruptions also carry costs that are easy to underestimate.

:::case Interrupted work: more speed and more stress
In a 2008 experiment, Gloria Mark of the University of California, Irvine, with Daniela Gudith and Ulrich Klocke of Humboldt University, studied 48 participants completing tasks with and without interruptions by phone or instant message [8]. Surprisingly, participants completed interrupted tasks in less time with no difference in quality; the authors suggest that people compensated by working faster, but at a price: more stress, higher frustration, time pressure, and effort [8]. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's. For owners, the finding reframes the problem. Interruptions may not always reduce output in the short run, but a workday of constant interruption is likely to be more stressful and effortful, which matters for a pace you hope to sustain for years.
:::

## Three kinds of interruptions

| Type | Example | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Preventable | "Where is the price list?" | Create the missing resource or process |
| Routable | Routine customer scheduling question | Assign to a team member or system |
| Essential | Safety incident; major customer complaint | Clear escalation path to the owner |

Your audit from Chapter 1 shows which interruptions are most frequent. The most effective response to a preventable interruption is to fix its cause once: write the price list, the FAQ, or the checklist.

## Communication windows and escalation rules

Communication windows are set times for handling non-urgent messages, for example 11:30 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. Escalation rules define what is urgent enough to interrupt focus time. A simple rule might be: interrupt me immediately for safety, a customer threatening to cancel, or a problem costing more than $500; otherwise, add it to the shared list for the next window. Without a rule, team members either interrupt for everything or hesitate when they should not.

## Self-interruption

Many interruptions are self-inflicted: checking email, phone notifications, or social media during focus blocks. Practical defenses include closing email during focus time, silencing non-essential notifications, and keeping a notepad to capture stray thoughts for later.

## The interruption framework

**Step 1: Log interruptions** for one week, noting source and topic.

**Step 2: Classify** each as preventable, routable, or essential.

**Step 3: Fix the top three preventable causes** with a resource or process.

**Step 4: Route routine questions** to a person, document, or system.

**Step 5: Publish communication windows and an escalation rule** to your team and, where appropriate, customers.

**Step 6: Reduce self-interruption** during focus blocks.

:::example Illustrative: an interruption log
A hypothetical auto-repair shop owner logs 86 interruptions in a week. Thirty-one concern parts availability (preventable with a shared parts sheet), 24 are customer status calls (routable to the service writer with a text-update template), and 9 are true escalations. After the changes, a second log records 41 interruptions. He still handles the 9 escalations immediately. Figures are illustrative.
:::

Fix for it: ___

Who or what handles it: ___

:::summary
Research by Mark and colleagues found that people may compensate for interruptions by working faster, but with more stress and effort. Classify interruptions as preventable, routable, or essential; fix causes, route routine questions, publish communication windows and escalation rules, and reduce self-interruption.

# Chapter 6: Delegate Results, Not Scraps

:::objectives
- Calculate the value of your time.
- Use a loop of audit, transfer, and fill.
- Document tasks efficiently for hand-off.
- Delegate decisions with clear limits.
:::

## Why owners hold on

Owners often keep tasks because "no one else does it right," because training takes time, or because they believe they cannot afford help. Each belief has a kernel of truth, but together they create a bottleneck: the business cannot grow beyond the owner's hours.

Delegation fails most often when owners delegate scraps, isolated tasks without context, rather than results. "Type up these notes" is a scrap. "Own the weekly customer follow-up so every quote gets a response within two days" is a result.

:::case Dan Martell's Buyback Loop and camcorder method
In a conversation on the Startups for the Rest of Us podcast, Dan Martell described the Buyback Loop from his book Buy Back Your Time: audit, transfer, and fill [2]. In the transfer stage, he uses what he calls the camcorder method: "I literally record myself doing the work while I'm doing the work so it takes me no extra time," so that when he hires someone he has three to five videos that become the first week of onboarding [2]. A Genius Network summary of a separate conversation describes the fill stage as refilling the schedule with high-value activities and lists his 1-3-1 rule, in which a team member bringing a problem defines it, presents three possible solutions, and offers one recommendation [3]. Martell's companion workbook suggests estimating a "Buyback Rate" by dividing annual income by 2,000 hours and then by four, as a guide to which tasks to hand off [15]. Martell's results are his own, not TTI's, and his figures reflect a business far larger than most readers'. The methods scale down well: recording a task as you do it costs little, and the 1-3-1 rule teaches team members to think rather than simply escalate.
:::

## Calculating a hand-off threshold

Using Martell's formula as a rough guide, an owner earning $100,000 a year has an hourly value of about $50 and a Buyback Rate of about $12.50 [15]. Tasks that could be done well by someone paid less than that rate are candidates to hand off, provided the business can afford it and the freed time is used for higher-value work. Small businesses that cannot yet hire can start with automation, an hourly contractor, or a part-time employee for a few hours a week.

| Task type | Hand-off option | Documentation needed |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduling and reminders | Booking software; assistant | Rules and templates |
| Bookkeeping entry | Bookkeeper | Chart of accounts; process video |
| Routine customer questions | Team member; FAQ; front-desk tool | Approved answers; escalation rule |
| Social media posting | Contractor | Brand guide; examples |
| Estimates for standard jobs | Trained employee | Pricing sheet; video walkthrough |

## Delegating decisions

Delegation includes decision rights. Define what the person can decide alone, what they must decide and report, and what requires your approval, often with dollar limits. A clear limit prevents both bottlenecks and unpleasant surprises.

## The delegation framework

**Step 1: Audit** for draining, low-cost tasks (Chapter 1).

**Step 2: Define the result** each hand-off should achieve and how it will be measured.

**Step 3: Record the task** as you do it, then turn the recording into a checklist.

**Step 4: Transfer** to a person, contractor, or tool with decision limits.

**Step 5: Review** results weekly at first, then less often.

**Step 6: Fill** the freed time with your weekly results.

:::example Illustrative: handing off invoicing
A hypothetical landscaping owner spends about 5 hours a week on invoicing. He records himself invoicing three times, and a part-time bookkeeper builds a checklist from the videos. The bookkeeper, paid $25 an hour, takes 3 hours a week after training. The owner's estimated hourly value is $45. He uses the freed 5 hours for two sales calls a week. He reviews invoices weekly for a month and finds two errors, both fixed by updating the checklist. Figures are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 7: Run Fewer, Better Meetings

:::objectives
- Evaluate whether a meeting is needed.
- Design meetings with a purpose, agenda, and decision record.
- Use asynchronous updates where possible.
- Protect meeting-free time.
:::

## The meeting tax

In a small business, meetings may be few, but each consumes the time of everyone present. A one-hour weekly meeting with five people costs about 260 person-hours a year. Many meetings exist because they always have, not because they produce decisions.

:::case Shopify's calendar purge
In January 2023, the e-commerce company Shopify removed all recurring meetings with more than two people from employee calendars and re-affirmed a rule against meetings on Wednesdays, Bloomberg reported via the Los Angeles Times [10]. NPR reported a month later that Shopify's chief operating officer, Kaz Nejatian, said the company had deleted 322,000 hours of meetings in a company of about 10,000 employees, which he equated to adding 150 employees; employees were told to wait two weeks before adding anything back and to be "really, really critical" about what returned [9]. Shopify's results are its own, not TTI's, and a large remote technology firm differs from a small local business. The lesson that transfers is the method: periodically remove recurring meetings and require each to earn its way back.
:::

## Does this need a meeting?

| Purpose | Better format |
|---|---|
| Sharing information | Written update, short video, or shared document |
| Status reports | Shared task board or weekly written summary |
| Making a complex decision | Meeting with a clear agenda |
| Resolving conflict | Meeting or one-on-one conversation |
| Building relationships | Meeting, ideally short and regular |

## Designing a good meeting

A good meeting has a stated purpose, a short agenda sent in advance, only the people needed, a time limit, and a record of decisions and next actions with owners and dates. For small teams, a 15-minute daily or weekly huddle can replace many interruptions.

## The meeting framework

**Step 1: List every recurring meeting** and its cost in person-hours.

**Step 2: Pause all recurring meetings** for two weeks.

**Step 3: Reinstate only those** with a clear purpose that requires live discussion.

**Step 4: Replace information sharing** with written or recorded updates.

**Step 5: Use an agenda and decision record** for each meeting.

**Step 6: Protect one meeting-free block** each week.

:::example Illustrative: a restaurant manager's meeting reset
A hypothetical restaurant has three recurring meetings: a 45-minute Monday staff meeting with 12 people, a weekly 60-minute owner-manager meeting, and a 30-minute vendor call. The manager replaces most of the Monday meeting with a written weekly note and a 10-minute pre-shift huddle, keeps the owner-manager meeting with an agenda, and moves the vendor call to email unless a problem arises. The change saves about 7 person-hours a week. Figures are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 8: Protect a Pace You Can Repeat

:::objectives
- Summarize evidence on long working hours and output.
- Recognize the signs of an unsustainable pace.
- Set working-hour limits and recovery routines.
- Experiment with schedule changes carefully.
:::

## More hours, diminishing returns

Many owners respond to a busy season by working longer, and occasionally that is necessary. As a permanent strategy, it has limits. Productivity research suggests that after a point, additional hours add less and less output, while fatigue, errors, and accidents rise.

Stanford economist John Pencavel examined historical records of British munition workers, most of them women, from the World War I era. He found that the relationship between output and hours was nonlinear: below a threshold, output was proportional to hours, but above the threshold, output rose at a decreasing rate as hours increased [11]. He linked these findings to research on the effects of long working hours on accidents and injuries [11]. The workers were factory employees a century ago, not modern owners, so the precise numbers do not transfer, but the shape of the relationship is a useful warning.

:::case The UK four-day week pilot
From June to December 2022, 61 companies with about 2,900 workers in the United Kingdom took part in what the think tank Autonomy described as the world's largest four-day working week trial to date, with analysis by academics at the University of Cambridge and Boston College [12]. Autonomy reported that 56 of the 61 companies (92 percent) were continuing with the four-day week after the trial and that 18 had confirmed the change as permanent [12]. The participating companies' results are their own, not TTI's, and companies that volunteer for such trials may differ from those that do not. The case does not show that every business should adopt a four-day week. It shows that many organizations, when they redesigned work deliberately, found ways to maintain operations with fewer hours, a reason for owners to question the assumption that more hours are the only route to more output.
:::

## Signs of an unsustainable pace

| Sign | What it may indicate | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Rising error rate | Fatigue | Reduce hours; add checks |
| Irritability with customers or staff | Stress | Build recovery into the week |
| Sleep problems | Overload or health issue | Consult a professional; reduce load |
| Important work always postponed | Reactive schedule | Protect focus blocks |
| No time off in months | Owner bottleneck | Delegate; plan coverage |

## Setting limits

Set a default weekly hour limit and a daily stop time, and treat exceptions as exceptions with an end date. Schedule at least one full day off a week and plan vacations in advance with coverage. If you employ others, remember that federal and state laws govern overtime and, in some states, breaks; ask your state labor agency before changing schedules.

## Experimenting carefully

If you want to test a changed schedule, such as a shorter week in a slow season or a later start, run it as an experiment: define the change, the period, the measures (revenue, customer response times, errors, how you feel), and the criteria for keeping or reversing it.

## The sustainable-pace framework

**Step 1: Record actual hours** for two weeks.

**Step 2: Note warning signs** from the table.

**Step 3: Set a default weekly limit** and daily stop time.

**Step 4: Schedule recovery**: breaks, a day off, vacations with coverage.

**Step 5: Design one experiment** to reduce hours without hurting key measures.

**Step 6: Review results** after the experiment period.

:::example Illustrative: a seasonal schedule experiment
A hypothetical pool-service owner works 62 hours a week in summer and 45 in winter. He tests a winter schedule of four 9-hour days for eight weeks, with Friday reserved for emergencies only. He measures response time to service requests, revenue, and his own energy on a 1–10 scale. Response times stay within 24 hours, revenue matches the prior winter, and his energy rating rises from 5 to 7. He keeps the schedule for winter and plans a similar test for spring. Figures are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 9: Tools, Automation, and AI Assistants

:::objectives
- Choose tools based on process, not features.
- Identify tasks suitable for automation.
- Summarize evidence on generative AI in customer support.
- Set guardrails for AI use.
:::

## Process first, tool second

A new app rarely fixes a broken process. Before buying software, write down the process step by step, then ask which steps a tool would make faster or more reliable. A simple, shared system used consistently beats a sophisticated one that nobody updates.

| Process | Common tool type | What to check before buying |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduling | Online booking | Integrates with your calendar; reminder options |
| Tasks and projects | Task manager | Team can use it on phones; simple enough |
| Invoicing | Accounting software | Bank connection; accountant compatibility |
| Customer messages | Shared inbox or front-desk tool | Escalation; record keeping |
| Documentation | Shared drive or wiki | Easy search; permissions |

## Automation candidates

Good automation candidates are frequent, rule-based, and low-risk if something goes wrong: appointment reminders, invoice reminders, standard confirmations, and data transfer between systems. Poor candidates require judgment, involve sensitive situations, or carry legal risk if handled incorrectly.

:::case Generative AI in customer support
Economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond studied the staggered introduction of a generative AI-based conversational assistant among 5,172 customer-support agents, publishing their findings in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2025 [14]. Access to AI assistance increased productivity, measured by issues resolved per hour, by 15 percent on average, with large differences among workers: less experienced and lower-skilled agents improved both speed and quality, while the most experienced and highest-skilled agents saw small gains in speed and small declines in quality [14]. The researchers' results are their own, not TTI's, and they studied one large company's support operation. For small businesses, the study suggests that AI assistants can help most with routine, well-documented work and less-experienced staff, and that experienced people should review where quality matters.
:::

## Guardrails for AI

AI tools can draft emails, summarize documents, answer routine questions, and organize notes. They can also make mistakes, invent facts, and mishandle sensitive information. Sensible guardrails include:

- Review AI-drafted customer communications before sending, at least at first.
- Do not enter sensitive customer, employee, or financial information into tools whose data practices you have not reviewed.
- Keep humans responsible for decisions about money, safety, legal matters, and employment.
- Disclose when customers are interacting with an automated system where required or expected.
- Check marketing claims for accuracy; the business remains responsible for what it says.

## The tools framework

**Step 1: Write the process** step by step.

**Step 2: Identify bottleneck steps** where a tool would help.

**Step 3: Choose the simplest tool** that solves the bottleneck.

**Step 4: Automate frequent, rule-based, low-risk steps.**

**Step 5: Set AI guardrails** for review, data, and decisions.

**Step 6: Review after 30 days**: Is the tool used? Did the bottleneck improve?

:::example Illustrative: automating appointment reminders
A hypothetical dental-hygiene practice with two hygienists averages 9 no-shows a month. Staff spend about 6 hours a month calling patients with reminders. The practice adds automated text and email reminders 48 hours and 2 hours before appointments, with a reply option to reschedule. After three months, no-shows average 5 a month and reminder calls drop to about 1 hour a month for patients who prefer calls. A staff member reviews the reminder wording for accuracy and patient privacy. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Tool and AI readiness
- Process written step by step.
- Bottleneck identified.
- Simplest suitable tool chosen.
- Automation limited to rule-based, low-risk steps.
- Human review for customer-facing AI output.
- Sensitive data rules defined.
- Human responsibility for key decisions.
- 30-day review scheduled.
:::

:::summary
Choose tools after mapping the process, and automate frequent, rule-based, low-risk steps. Research by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond found that an AI assistant raised customer-support productivity by 15 percent on average, with the largest gains for less experienced workers. Guardrails for review, data, and decisions keep AI use responsible.
:::

# Chapter 10: The Weekly Review and Daily Routine

:::objectives
- Explain why a regular review keeps the system working.
- Conduct a weekly review in under an hour.
- Design a simple daily start-up and shutdown routine.
- Improve one habit at a time.
:::

## Why systems decay

Every productivity system decays without maintenance. Lists accumulate stale items, calendars fill with obligations, and old habits return. The weekly review is the maintenance routine that keeps the system trustworthy.

:::case Benjamin Franklin's daily scheme and self-examination
In his autobiography, Benjamin Franklin, printer, entrepreneur, and statesman, described a small book he kept to track his practice of thirteen virtues, with a page for each and a daily examination [13]. Because his precept of Order required that "every part of my business should have its allotted time," one page contained a "scheme of employment for the twenty-four hours of a natural day," opening with the morning question "What good shall I do this day?" and closing in the evening with "What good have I done to-day?" [13]. Franklin wrote, "I was surpris'd to find myself so much fuller of faults than I had imagined; but I had the satisfaction of seeing them diminish" [13]. Franklin's results are his own, not TTI's. The case is a reminder that the core elements of a modern productivity system, a daily plan, protected time for work, and regular honest review, are centuries old.
:::

## The weekly review

| Step | Time | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Clear inboxes and capture points | 10 min | Is everything captured? |
| Review last week's calendar | 5 min | What happened? What needs follow-up? |
| Review next two weeks' calendar | 5 min | What needs preparation? |
| Review results | 5 min | Did I finish my weekly results? Why or why not? |
| Update lists | 10 min | Are next actions current? What is waiting? |
| Choose next week's results | 10 min | What matters most? |
| Block time | 10 min | When will I do it? |
| One improvement | 5 min | What one habit or process will I improve? |

## Daily start-up and shutdown

A short daily start-up, perhaps ten minutes, reviews the calendar, chooses the day's most important task, and checks for urgent messages. A shutdown routine, also about ten minutes, captures loose ends, updates the list, and sets up tomorrow. A shutdown is also the moment to redraw tomorrow's blocks; as Cal Newport puts it, the key is maintaining intention about your time, not perfection in your planning [5]. Franklin's evening question, "What good have I done to-day?", is a gentle version of the same idea [13].

## One habit at a time

Trying to change everything at once usually fails. Choose one habit or process each week or month, such as a daily shutdown, an interruption rule, or a delegated task, and practice it until it is routine before adding another.

## The review framework

**Step 1: Schedule a weekly review** at the same time each week.

**Step 2: Follow the review steps** in the table.

**Step 3: Design a daily start-up** of about ten minutes.

**Step 4: Design a daily shutdown** of about ten minutes.

**Step 5: Choose one improvement** each week or month.

**Step 6: Review the system itself** quarterly: What is working? What is not?

:::example Illustrative: a first month of weekly reviews
A hypothetical photographer schedules weekly reviews on Friday at 3 p.m. In week one, the review takes 85 minutes because her lists are out of date; by week four, it takes 40. She finishes 4 of 9 weekly results in the first two weeks and 5 of 6 in the next two, after reducing the number she chooses. Her one improvement each week is, in order: a shutdown routine, a communication window, a delegated editing task, and a booking template. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::summary
Systems decay without maintenance. A weekly review clears inboxes, updates lists, reviews results, chooses new results, and blocks time. Daily start-up and shutdown routines bracket the workday. Franklin's centuries-old scheme, with its morning and evening questions, shows the lasting value of planning and honest review.

# Chapter 11: Make Decisions Without Becoming the Bottleneck

:::objectives
- Distinguish reversible from irreversible decisions.
- Match the decision process to the stakes.
- Decide with sufficient, not perfect, information.
- Push routine decisions to the people closest to the work.
:::

## Decision fatigue in small businesses

In many small businesses, the owner is the default decision maker for everything: refunds, schedule swaps, supply orders, social posts, and pricing exceptions. Each decision may take only a minute, but together they fragment the day and leave the team waiting. Worse, the owner gives every decision the same treatment, deliberating as long over a $40 refund as over a five-year lease.

:::case Amazon's one-way and two-way doors
In his letter to shareholders published in April 2016, Amazon founder Jeff Bezos distinguished "Type 1" decisions, which are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible, from "Type 2" decisions, which are changeable and reversible, like doors you can walk back through; Business Insider reported his warning that as organizations grow they tend to use the heavyweight Type 1 process on many Type 2 decisions, resulting in "slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently, and consequently diminished invention" [17]. In his 2016 letter, Bezos added that "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had," and that "if you wait for 90%, in most cases, you're probably being slow" [16]. Amazon's results are its own, not TTI's, and the 70 percent figure is a rule of thumb, not a measurement. For a small business, the transferable idea is to sort decisions by reversibility and move quickly on the many that can be undone.
:::

## Sorting decisions

| Decision | Reversible? | Who decides | Process |
|---|---|---|---|
| Refund under $100 | Yes | Front-line staff | Policy; report weekly |
| Trying a new supplier for one order | Yes | Manager | Quick comparison |
| Weekly social media topics | Yes | Team member | Content calendar |
| Hiring a full-time employee | Partly | Owner | Structured interviews; references |
| Signing a multi-year lease | No | Owner with advisers | Careful analysis; legal review |
| Taking on significant debt | No | Owner with advisers | Projections; professional advice |

## Sufficient information

For reversible decisions, ask: What would the missing information change? If the answer is "probably not much," decide, set a review date, and move on. For irreversible decisions, slow down, seek advice, and gather the information that would change your choice.

## Pushing decisions down

Decision rules, such as dollar limits for refunds, standard answers for common requests, and clear criteria for scheduling, allow team members to decide without asking. Combine these with Martell's 1-3-1 rule from Chapter 6 so that, when people do escalate, they bring a recommendation [3].

## The decision framework

**Step 1: List recurring decisions** that reach you each week.

**Step 2: Classify each** as reversible or irreversible.

**Step 3: Write rules** for routine, reversible decisions and assign them to others.

**Step 4: Set dollar or risk limits** above which you must be consulted.

**Step 5: Decide reversible matters quickly** with a review date.

**Step 6: Reserve careful process** for irreversible decisions.

:::example Illustrative: a retail owner's decision rules
A hypothetical gift-shop owner records 47 decisions that reach her in a week. She classifies 39 as routine and reversible. She writes rules allowing staff to approve returns up to $75, reorder any item on the standard list when stock falls below a set level, and swap shifts with notice in the shared schedule. The next week, 14 decisions reach her. She keeps a short list of decisions she alone makes: hiring, leases, and any spending over $1,000. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::worksheet Decision rules
Decisions reaching me per week: ___
Routine and reversible decisions: ___
Rule for decision 1: ___
Rule for decision 2: ___
Rule for decision 3: ___
Dollar limit for staff decisions: ___
Decisions reserved for the owner: ___
Review date for new rules: ___
:::

:::summary
Owners become bottlenecks when every decision gets the same slow treatment. Bezos's distinction between irreversible and reversible decisions, and his rule of thumb about deciding with about 70 percent of desired information, suggest a better approach: move quickly on reversible decisions, push routine ones to the team with clear rules and limits, and reserve careful process for decisions that cannot be undone.
:::

# Chapter 12: Energy, Sleep, and Recovery

:::objectives
- Explain why energy, not only time, limits an owner's output.
- Summarize public health guidance and data on adult sleep.
- Match tasks to your energy across the day.
- Build recovery into the workweek and the year.
:::

## Time is not the only constraint

Two owners with identical calendars can produce very different results, because attention and energy vary across the day and across the week. A difficult estimate written at 9:00 p.m. after a twelve-hour day is more likely to contain errors than the same estimate written at 8:00 a.m. Productivity systems that count only hours miss this. The audit in Chapter 1 already asked you to mark energy; this chapter treats energy as a resource to be planned.

:::case Public health data on adult sleep
The CDC's National Center for Health Statistics reported that in 2024, 30.5 percent of U.S. adults slept less than 7 hours on average in a 24-hour period, and that 54.8 percent woke up well-rested; the report notes that the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends at least 7 hours of sleep for adults [18]. In an earlier release, the CDC stated that sleeping less than seven hours a day is associated with increased risk of chronic conditions, including obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, and frequent mental distress, and suggested lifestyle changes such as consistent bed and wake times and removing screens from the bedroom [19]. These are public health statistics; the CDC's findings are its own, not TTI's, and this book does not offer medical advice. For owners, the data are a reminder that sleep is not a luxury to trade away for work, and that persistent sleep problems deserve a conversation with a qualified health professional.
:::

## Matching tasks to energy

Most people have periods of higher and lower alertness. Use your audit to identify yours, then match tasks accordingly.

| Energy level | Best-suited work | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| High | Concentration, creativity, hard conversations | Pricing, estimates, writing, planning, difficult calls |
| Medium | Collaborative and customer work | Team check-ins, sales calls, service delivery |
| Low | Routine, rule-based tasks | Filing, data entry, simple email, tidying the workspace |

## Recovery during the day

Short breaks between focus blocks, a real meal away from the desk, and brief movement are simple ways to recover attention. The buffers in your weekly template (Chapter 3) are also recovery time when they are not needed for overruns. Owners who work physically demanding jobs need to consider safety as well: fatigue raises the risk of mistakes with tools, vehicles, and equipment.

## Recovery across the year

Plan time off at the start of each year, as you would plan a busy season. A vacation that is scheduled, covered, and communicated in advance is more likely to happen and less likely to be interrupted. Delegation playbooks (Appendix C) and decision rules (Chapter 11) make coverage possible.

## The energy framework

**Step 1: Mark your energy** in the time audit for two weeks.

**Step 2: Identify high-, medium-, and low-energy periods.**

**Step 3: Move concentration work** into high-energy periods.

**Step 4: Protect sleep** with a consistent schedule and a daily stop time.

**Step 5: Build in daily recovery**: breaks, meals, movement.

**Step 6: Schedule time off** for the year with coverage plans.

:::example Illustrative: rearranging a day by energy
A hypothetical bookkeeper who runs her own practice notices in her audit that she rates her energy 8 out of 10 from 7:00 to 10:00 a.m. and 4 out of 10 after 3:00 p.m. She had been reconciling client accounts, her most error-prone work, in the late afternoon. She moves reconciliations to the morning, calls and email to midday, and filing to late afternoon. Over the next month, her correction rate on reconciliations falls from about 6 per week to 2. She also sets a 10:30 p.m. phone-off time. Figures are illustrative.

# Appendix F: Common Productivity Myths

**Myth: The answer is to work longer.** Pencavel's study of munition workers found that output rises more slowly once hours pass a threshold [11], and the UK four-day week pilot suggests that many organizations can redesign work to use fewer hours [12]. Before adding hours, look for work to stop, simplify, move, or share.

**Myth: A new app will fix it.** Tools amplify processes; they do not create them. Write the process first (Chapter 9).

**Myth: Multitasking saves time.** Interrupted work may be finished faster, but Mark and colleagues found it came with more stress, frustration, and effort [8]. Batch similar tasks and protect focus blocks.

**Myth: Good owners answer everything immediately.** Customers and staff need reliable responses, not instant ones for every question. Communication windows plus a clear escalation rule give fast help for real emergencies and predictable answers for the rest (Chapter 5).

**Myth: Delegating costs more than doing it myself.** Sometimes true in the first weeks, while training happens. Over months, a documented, delegated task usually costs less of the owner's time; Martell's camcorder method reduces the up-front cost by recording the task while you do it [2].

**Myth: Planning wastes time because plans always change.** Plans change, but the act of planning still helps. Masicampo and Baumeister found that making specific plans reduced the mental interference of unfinished goals [7], and Newport stresses intention over perfection when the day changes [5].

**Myth: Big decisions and small decisions deserve the same care.** Bezos's distinction between one-way and two-way doors suggests otherwise [17]. Move quickly on reversible decisions and slow down on irreversible ones (Chapter 11).

**Myth: Sleep is the first thing to cut in a busy season.** CDC data link short sleep with increased health risks [19]. A busy season is better managed with temporary help, fewer commitments, and protected rest.

**Myth: Productivity means doing more.** In this book, productivity means getting the right work done at a pace you can repeat. Sometimes the most productive change is removing a task, a meeting, or a commitment entirely.

# Glossary

**Buyback Loop.** Dan Martell's three-stage cycle of audit, transfer, and fill for reclaiming an owner's time.

**Buyback Rate.** Martell's rough threshold for delegation: annual income divided by 2,000 hours, then by four.

**Camcorder method.** Recording yourself doing a task as you do it, to create training material.

**Capture point.** The place where new commitments are recorded the moment they arise.

**Communication window.** A scheduled time for handling non-urgent messages.

**Escalation rule.** A rule defining which problems justify interrupting the owner.

**Next action.** The very next physical step needed to move a task forward.

**One-way door (Type 1 decision).** A consequential decision that is hard or impossible to reverse.

**Time blocking.** Assigning specific work to specific blocks of time on the calendar.

**Trusted list.** A regularly reviewed list of commitments complete enough that you need not remember them.

**Two-way door (Type 2 decision).** A reversible decision that can be made quickly.

**Weekly result.** A specific outcome to finish within the week.

**Weekly review.** A recurring session to clear inboxes, update lists, and plan the next week.
