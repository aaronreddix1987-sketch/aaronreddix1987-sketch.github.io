# How to Use This Book

Email remains one of the few marketing channels a small business can own outright. Social platforms change their algorithms, advertising costs rise and fall, and search rankings shift, but a list of people who have asked to hear from you belongs to your business. That ownership comes with obligations: to the law, to the mailbox providers that decide whether your messages reach the inbox, and above all to the subscribers who trusted you with their address.

This book is a structured course in permission-based email marketing for owners and small teams. It begins with the rules, because a program built on non-compliant practices eventually fails, and proceeds through deliverability, list building, writing, sequencing, segmentation, selling, measurement, and list protection.

## Who This Book Is For

- Owners of service businesses, local shops, and online businesses starting an email program.
- Marketers in small organizations who need a rigorous, compliant framework.
- Creators who want to sell products to an audience without resorting to hype.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Apply the CAN-SPAM Act and understand when other laws, such as Canada's CASL, apply.
- Meet mailbox provider sender requirements, including authentication and one-click unsubscribe.
- Build a list through clear, honest opt-in offers.
- Write a welcome sequence and regular emails that people read.
- Plan a sustainable calendar and segment your list.
- Run a launch sequence ethically and measure results with metrics that are still reliable.
- Maintain list hygiene and protect subscriber data.

## How Each Chapter Is Built

Every chapter opens with learning objectives and closes with a summary and review questions. In between, you will find defined terms, a step-by-step framework, a worked example labelled "illustrative" (a hypothetical scenario to show the method, not a real client), and, where useful, a case study drawn from public records with a citation. Checklists and worksheets turn each chapter into action.

:::warning Disclaimer
This book is educational and is not legal advice. Email laws vary by jurisdiction and change over time; consult a qualified attorney about your obligations. Case studies describe the documented experiences of named people and companies; their results are their own, not TTI's, and are not typical or guaranteed. Nothing in this book promises any level of subscribers, sales, or income.
:::

# Chapter 1: The Case for Permission-Based Email

:::objectives
- Define permission-based email marketing and distinguish it from spam.
- Explain why an email list is an owned asset.
- Describe the relationship between trust, relevance, and results.
- Identify the core components of an email program.
:::

**Permission-based email marketing** is the practice of sending commercial and relationship email only to people who have affirmatively asked to receive it, with a clear understanding of what they will receive and an easy way to stop. It stands in contrast to **spam**, which is unwanted email sent without meaningful consent.

## Owned Versus Rented Audiences

An **owned audience** is a group you can contact directly without an intermediary deciding who sees your message. An email list is owned in this sense; a social media following is **rented**, because the platform controls distribution. Ownership is not absolute (mailbox providers still filter mail, as Chapter 3 explains), but it is far more stable than reach governed by an algorithm you do not control.

## Trust as the Operating Principle

Every successful email program rests on a simple exchange: the subscriber gives attention and an address; the sender gives value and respect. When senders break this exchange through irrelevant volume, deceptive subject lines, or ignored unsubscribes, subscribers stop opening, start marking messages as spam, and mailbox providers respond by filtering the sender's mail. Trust is thus not only an ethical principle but an operational one.

:::case Nathan Barry: A Small, Engaged List
Author and later ConvertKit (now Kit) founder Nathan Barry has published detailed accounts of his early product launches. When he launched his first ebook, The App Design Handbook, in 2012, his blog received fewer than 100 visitors a day and his email list had 795 subscribers, yet the launch produced about $12,000 in its first 24 hours ([ProBlogger](https://problogger.com/make-money-from-a-low-traffic-blog-case-study/)). He attributed the result to months of teaching his audience for free before asking them to buy. His results are his own, not TTI's, and are far from typical; the lesson is that list engagement and relevance matter more than raw list size.
:::

# Chapter 2: The Rules Come First

:::objectives
- Summarize the CAN-SPAM Act's main requirements.
- Distinguish commercial from transactional or relationship messages.
- Recognize when Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation may apply.
- Build a compliance checklist for every send.
:::

## The CAN-SPAM Act

The **CAN-SPAM Act** is the U.S. federal law setting rules for commercial email. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) explains that, despite its name, it "doesn't apply just to bulk email"; it covers all commercial messages, including business-to-business email, and "each separate email in violation of the CAN-SPAM Act is subject to penalties of up to $53,088" ([FTC, CAN-SPAM Compliance Guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)).

The FTC summarizes the main requirements ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)):

1. Don't use false or misleading header information.
2. Don't use deceptive subject lines.
3. Identify the message as an ad (with leeway in how).
4. Tell recipients where you're located, with a valid physical postal address (a street address, registered P.O. box, or registered private mailbox).
5. Tell recipients how to opt out of future marketing email, clearly and conspicuously.
6. Remember that subscribers and members can opt out of marketing email too.
7. Honor opt-out requests promptly: within 10 business days, without charging a fee or requiring more than an email address or a visit to a single web page; keep the opt-out mechanism working for at least 30 days after sending; and never sell or transfer the addresses of people who opted out.
8. Monitor what others do on your behalf; you cannot contract away responsibility.

## Commercial Versus Transactional Messages

The FTC explains that what matters is a message's **primary purpose**. A **transactional or relationship** message, such as an order confirmation, a warranty or safety notice, a change to account terms, or delivery of purchased goods, is exempt from most CAN-SPAM provisions but may not contain false routing information ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)). When a message mixes content, it is commercial if a recipient reading the subject line would likely conclude it is promotional, or if the transactional content does not appear mainly at the beginning.

| Message | Likely classification | Unsubscribe required? |
|---|---|---|
| Order receipt with shipping details | Transactional | No (but still accurate headers) |
| Monthly newsletter featuring products | Commercial | Yes |
| Receipt with a large promotion at the top | Likely commercial | Yes |
| Password reset | Transactional | No |
| "Your membership terms are changing" | Relationship | No, if purely informational |

## Beyond the United States

If you email people in other countries, their laws may apply. Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL), for example, requires senders of commercial electronic messages to "(1) obtain consent, (2) provide identification information, and (3) provide an unsubscribe mechanism" ([CRTC, CASL FAQ](https://crtc.gc.ca/eng/com500/faq500.htm)). Note that CASL's starting point, consent before sending, is stricter than CAN-SPAM's, which permits commercial email until the recipient opts out. Building your program on affirmative consent from the outset satisfies the stricter standard and improves deliverability.

:::case Verkada: The Largest CAN-SPAM Penalty to Date
In August 2024, the FTC announced that security-camera company Verkada would pay $2.95 million to settle allegations that it violated CAN-SPAM, which the FTC described as "the largest penalty obtained by the FTC for a CAN-SPAM violation." According to the complaint, Verkada sent more than 30 million commercial emails over three years, and its messages failed to include an opt-out option, failed to honor opt-out requests, and lacked a physical postal address ([FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/ftc-takes-action-against-security-camera-firm-verkada-over-charges-it-failed-secure-videos-other)). The court-entered order, issued in September 2024, also permanently prohibits future CAN-SPAM violations ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/295m-penalty-and-permanent-injunction-resolves-lawsuit-against-verkada-inc-alleged-unlawful)). Verkada neither admitted nor denied the allegations. The case shows that the basic requirements, an unsubscribe link, a postal address, and prompt opt-out processing, are exactly what regulators check.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Auditing One Email
A yoga studio's promotional email reads: Subject "Re: your account." Body: a 20 percent off class pack offer. Footer: no address; unsubscribe link leads to a login page.
Problems:

- Deceptive subject line ("Re:" implies a reply; "your account" implies a transactional message).
- No physical postal address.
- Opt-out requires login, more than an email address or a single page visit.
Corrections: Subject "20% off class packs through Sunday"; add the studio's street address; link unsubscribe to a one-page form requiring only the email address.
:::

:::checklist Pre-Send Compliance
- "From" name and address accurately identify my business.
- Subject line accurately reflects the content.
- Commercial messages are identifiable as advertising.
- Valid physical postal address in the footer.
- Clear, conspicuous unsubscribe link that works without login.
- Opt-outs processed within 10 business days (sooner is better; see Chapter 3).
- Opted-out addresses never sold or transferred.
- Vendors sending on my behalf follow these rules.
- I have considered laws of other countries where subscribers live.
:::

:::summary
CAN-SPAM covers all commercial email, with penalties up to $53,088 per violating email. It requires accurate headers and subject lines, ad identification, a postal address, a clear opt-out honored within 10 business days, and oversight of vendors. Transactional messages are mostly exempt. Other countries, such as Canada under CASL, require consent before sending. The Verkada settlement shows regulators enforce the basics.
:::

# Chapter 3: Deliverability and Your Sending Infrastructure

:::objectives
- Define deliverability and the factors that influence it.
- Explain SPF, DKIM, and DMARC in plain language.
- Summarize Gmail's sender requirements, including spam-rate thresholds.
- Choose an email service provider using defined criteria.
:::

**Deliverability** is the likelihood that a message reaches the recipient's inbox rather than the spam folder or being rejected. It depends on three broad factors: **authentication** (proving you are who you claim to be), **reputation** (the history of how recipients respond to your mail), and **content and list quality**.

## Authentication in Plain Language

- **SPF (Sender Policy Framework)** is a DNS record listing the servers allowed to send mail for your domain.
- **DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail)** adds a cryptographic signature to each message so receivers can verify it was not altered and came from your domain.
- **DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance)** tells receivers what to do when a message fails SPF or DKIM alignment and sends you reports.

Your email service provider will give you the DNS records to add; your domain registrar or web host is where you add them.

## Gmail's Sender Requirements

Starting February 2024, Google began requiring all senders to Gmail accounts to meet baseline requirements, with additional requirements for senders of 5,000 or more messages a day ([Google, Email Sender Guidelines](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/81126)). For bulk senders, these include SPF and DKIM authentication, a DMARC record (a policy of "none" is acceptable), alignment of the From domain with SPF or DKIM, valid DNS records, TLS, one-click unsubscribe for marketing and subscribed messages, and a clearly visible unsubscribe link in the body ([Google](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/81126)).

Google's FAQ adds that unsubscribe requests must be honored within 48 hours, and that senders should keep their user-reported spam rate below 0.1 percent and never let it reach 0.3 percent; bulk senders above 0.3 percent become ineligible for delivery mitigation ([Google, Sender Guidelines FAQ](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/14229414)). Note that Google's two-day unsubscribe expectation is far shorter than CAN-SPAM's ten business days. Practically, your standard should be the stricter one: process unsubscribes immediately through your provider.

| Requirement | Applies to | Practical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| SPF or DKIM | All senders | Add DNS records from your provider |
| SPF and DKIM, plus DMARC | 5,000+/day | Add all three; start DMARC at p=none |
| Spam rate below 0.3% | All senders | Aim below 0.1% (1 complaint per 1,000) |
| One-click unsubscribe | 5,000+/day, marketing mail | Provider adds List-Unsubscribe headers |
| Honor unsubscribes within 48 hours | 5,000+/day | Automate immediate removal |

## Choosing an Email Service Provider

An **email service provider (ESP)** is the software that hosts your list, sends your email, and handles compliance features. Evaluate providers on:

1. Authentication support and clear setup guidance for SPF, DKIM, and DMARC.
2. Automatic unsubscribe handling and one-click unsubscribe headers.
3. Signup forms and landing pages.
4. Tagging, segmentation, and automation capabilities.
5. Reporting that includes clicks and conversions, not only opens.
6. Data export (so your list remains portable).
7. Pricing that scales reasonably with list size.
8. Account security features such as multi-factor authentication.

## Use Your Own Domain

Send from an address at your own domain (for example, news@yourbusiness.com) rather than a free mailbox address. Authentication requires a domain you control, and a consistent sending domain builds reputation over time.

:::example Illustrative: Diagnosing Poor Inbox Placement
A consulting firm notices its newsletter's click rate fell by half. Diagnosis:

- DNS check: SPF present; DKIM not configured; no DMARC.
- Postmaster data: spam rate 0.4 percent after an import of old contacts who never opted in.
Actions: configure DKIM and DMARC through the ESP's instructions; remove the imported contacts who never subscribed; send a re-permission email only to former clients; monitor until spam rate falls below 0.1 percent.
:::

:::checklist Deliverability Setup
- Sending from my own domain.
- SPF record added.
- DKIM configured through my ESP.
- DMARC record added (p=none to start).
- One-click unsubscribe enabled.
- Unsubscribes processed immediately.
- Google Postmaster Tools set up for my domain.
- Spam rate target below 0.1 percent.
- ESP account protected with multi-factor authentication.
:::

:::summary
Deliverability rests on authentication, reputation, and list and content quality. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC authenticate your mail. Gmail requires authentication, low spam rates (below 0.3 percent, ideally below 0.1 percent), and, for bulk senders, one-click unsubscribe honored within 48 hours. Choose an ESP that supports these requirements and lets you export your data.
:::

# Chapter 4: Building Your List With Consent

:::objectives
- Design a lead magnet that attracts the right subscribers.
- Write opt-in forms that set clear expectations.
- Compare single and double opt-in.
- Identify ethical list-growth channels.
:::

List building is the process of inviting the right people to subscribe. The goal is not the largest possible list but the most relevant one, because irrelevant subscribers depress engagement, raise complaint rates, and increase costs.

## The Subscription Promise

Every opt-in should communicate a **subscription promise**: what the subscriber will receive, how often, and why it is valuable. "Join our newsletter" is vague. "One practical bookkeeping tip for contractors every Tuesday" is a promise you can keep and the subscriber can evaluate.

## Lead Magnets

A **lead magnet** is a free resource offered in exchange for an email address. Effective lead magnets share four traits:

1. **Specific:** solves one narrow problem.
2. **Quick:** consumed in minutes, not hours.
3. **Relevant:** connected to what you ultimately sell, so subscribers self-select.
4. **Honest:** delivers what the opt-in page describes.

| Lead magnet type | Good for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Checklist | Service businesses | "Pre-move checklist" from a moving company |
| Template | B2B services | "Client onboarding email templates" |
| Short guide | Educators, consultants | "Five questions before hiring a contractor" |
| Calculator or worksheet | Financial services | "Monthly cash-flow worksheet" |
| Mini course by email | Creators | "Five-day introduction to sourdough" |
| Early access or waitlist | Product launches | "First look at our spring collection" |

## Opt-In Form Design

A good form asks for as little as necessary (often email and first name), restates the subscription promise near the button, links to a privacy policy, and avoids pre-checked boxes for additional marketing.

## Single Versus Double Opt-In

**Single opt-in** adds subscribers immediately after they submit a form. **Double opt-in** (also called confirmed opt-in) sends a confirmation email and adds subscribers only after they click a link. Double opt-in produces a smaller list with fewer typos, fake addresses, and spam traps, and it creates documented evidence of consent, which is useful where laws require consent, such as under CASL ([CRTC](https://crtc.gc.ca/eng/com500/faq500.htm)). Single opt-in grows faster but requires more diligent hygiene.

## Ethical Growth Channels

- Your website: header, footer, end-of-article forms, and a dedicated landing page.
- Point of sale: in-store sign-up with a clear statement of what customers will receive.
- Events and webinars: registration with a separate, unchecked box for marketing email.
- Partnerships: co-created resources where each partner's subscribers opt in explicitly.
- Social media: links to your landing page.

Never add people from business cards, purchased lists, or scraped directories without their explicit agreement. Gmail's guidance stresses ensuring that recipients "explicitly opt into getting messages from the address you use to send email" ([Google, Top 10 Gmail Sender Issues](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/15256272?hl=en)).

:::case Nathan Barry: Teaching Before Selling
Nathan Barry has described building his early audience by publishing free, detailed articles and offering sample chapters by email before launching his books, so that subscribers already knew what the product would teach ([ProBlogger](https://problogger.com/make-money-from-a-low-traffic-blog-case-study/)). His lead magnets were directly related to his paid products, which meant those who subscribed were likely buyers. His results are his own, not TTI's.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Designing an Opt-In
A residential landscaper wants to grow a list.

- Lead magnet: "The Seasonal Lawn Calendar for Zone 7" (one page, printable).
- Promise: "Get the calendar now, plus one short seasonal reminder each month. Unsubscribe anytime."
- Form: first name, email; button reads "Send my calendar."
- Confirmation: double opt-in email with the calendar link in the confirmation step.
- Placement: homepage, a blog post about spring lawn care, and a QR code on yard signs.
After the first season, the landscaper reviews which placement produced subscribers who later booked estimates.
:::

# Chapter 5: The Welcome Sequence

:::objectives
- Explain the purpose of a welcome sequence.
- Outline a five-email welcome sequence.
- Write each email with a single purpose and call to action.
- Set timing and exit conditions.
:::

A **welcome sequence** is a series of automated emails sent to new subscribers in the days after they join. It is typically the most-read set of messages you will send, because subscribers are paying attention right after they sign up. A good sequence delivers the promised resource, introduces you, sets expectations, delivers quick value, and invites a next step.

## The Five-Email Framework

| Email | Timing | Purpose | Call to action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Delivery | Immediately | Deliver the lead magnet; confirm the promise | Download the resource |
| 2. Introduction | Day 1–2 | Who you are and why you do this work | Reply with your biggest challenge |
| 3. Quick win | Day 3–4 | Teach one useful technique | Try it today |
| 4. Proof and perspective | Day 5–6 | Share a principle, documented case, or common mistake | Read a related article |
| 5. Next step | Day 7–9 | Introduce your core offer, softly and honestly | Book, buy, or reply |

## Why Ask for Replies

Asking subscribers to reply serves three purposes: it gives you direct research on their problems, it builds a personal relationship, and replies are a positive engagement signal. Read and answer replies; they are the most valuable data you will collect.

## Writing Each Email

Each welcome email should have one purpose and one primary call to action. Keep paragraphs short. Use your name in the From field so the relationship feels personal. Repeat the subscription promise so subscribers know what comes next.

## Exit Conditions

Define what happens when the sequence ends. Typically, subscribers move into your regular newsletter. If someone purchases during the sequence, exit them from sales-oriented emails and move them into a customer onboarding sequence.

:::case Nathan Barry's Pre-Launch Sequence
Nathan Barry published the email sequence he used before a book launch, including the order of teaching emails, sample chapters, and launch announcements, along with open rates for each ([Nathan Barry, Behind the Scenes](https://nathanbarry.com/behind-the-scenes/)). The structure moved from free education to a clear offer only after subscribers had received value. His results are his own, not TTI's, and open-rate figures from that era are not comparable with today's, for reasons explained in Chapter 10.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Welcome Sequence for a Tax Preparer
- Email 1 (immediately): "Your small-business tax deadline calendar." Link to the calendar; promise of one short tip every other week.
- Email 2 (day 2): "Why I started preparing taxes for contractors." Two paragraphs of story; question: "What's the most confusing part of taxes for you? Hit reply."
- Email 3 (day 4): "The 10-minute receipt habit." A simple weekly routine.
- Email 4 (day 6): "The most common mistake I see with estimated taxes." Explains the IRS estimated-tax concept and links to the IRS page.
- Email 5 (day 8): "If you'd like help this year." Describes services and pricing honestly; link to schedule a consultation.
- Exit: moves to biweekly newsletter; clients exit to onboarding sequence.
:::

A welcome sequence greets new subscribers when attention is highest. The five-email framework delivers the resource, introduces you, gives a quick win, offers perspective or documented proof, and invites a next step. # Chapter 6: Writing Emails People Read

:::objectives
- Write accurate, compelling subject lines.
- Structure an email for skimming and action.
- Choose between plain-text and designed formats.
- Edit for clarity, honesty, and accessibility.
:::

## The Anatomy of an Email

| Element | Function | Guideline |
|---|---|---|
| From name | Identifies the sender | Use a recognizable person or brand name |
| Subject line | Earns the open | Accurate and specific; CAN-SPAM forbids deception |
| Preview text | Extends the subject line | Add a second reason to open |
| Opening line | Earns the next line | Get to the point quickly |
| Body | Delivers value | Short paragraphs; one idea |
| Call to action | Directs the next step | One primary action |
| Footer | Compliance and trust | Address, unsubscribe, reason for receiving |

## Subject Lines

The FTC requires that "the subject line must accurately reflect the content of the message" ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)). Within that rule, effective subject lines tend to be specific ("Three ways to cut your HVAC bill this winter"), useful ("Your March deadline checklist"), or genuinely curious without being misleading ("The question every new client asks me"). Avoid fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" prefixes, false urgency, and all-capital letters.

## The Body: Clarity Over Cleverness

1. **Lead with the point.** State in the first two sentences what the email is about and why it matters.
2. **One idea per email.** Multiple ideas split attention and weaken the call to action.
3. **Short paragraphs.** Many readers use phones.
4. **Concrete language.** Replace "optimize your workflow" with "save an hour on Friday invoicing."
5. **One primary call to action.** Repeat it if needed, but make it the same action.

## Plain Text Versus Designed

Plain-text-style emails feel personal and render reliably. Designed emails with images and layouts suit product showcases and retail. Many small businesses do best with a simple, lightly formatted style. Whatever the format, include descriptive alt text for images and ensure the message makes sense if images do not load.

## Accessibility

Use sufficient color contrast, readable font sizes, descriptive link text ("Download the checklist" rather than "click here"), and logical heading order. Accessible emails are easier for everyone to read.

## Honesty in Persuasion

Persuasive writing is legitimate when it is truthful. Do not invent scarcity ("only 3 left" when there are hundreds), fabricate testimonials, or imply income or results you cannot substantiate. The FTC requires that disclosures needed to prevent deception be clear and conspicuous, and its guidance on endorsements applies to email as it does to other media ([FTC, Disclosures 101](https://www.ftc.gov/node/60343)).

:::example Illustrative: Rewriting a Weak Email
Before. Subject: "Newsletter #14." Body: four topics, three links, and a closing paragraph about a sale.
After. Subject: "The 5-minute gutter check before the first freeze." Preview: "Plus a checklist you can print." Body: two short paragraphs on why gutters fail in freezes, three steps, a link to the printable checklist, and a single closing line offering inspection booking.
The rewrite has one purpose, a specific accurate subject, and one primary action.
:::

:::checklist Editing Checklist
- Subject line accurately describes the content.
- Preview text adds value.
- Point stated in the first two sentences.
- One idea and one primary call to action.
- Paragraphs of three sentences or fewer.
- Jargon replaced with concrete language.
- Images have alt text; message works without images.
- Link text is descriptive.
- No invented scarcity, testimonials, or results.
- Footer includes address and unsubscribe.
:::

:::summary
Effective emails have accurate, specific subject lines, a clear opening, one idea, short paragraphs, and one call to action. Choose a format that renders reliably, design for accessibility, and persuade only with truthful claims, consistent with FTC rules on subject lines and disclosures.
:::

# Chapter 7: The Editorial Calendar and Sending Cadence

:::objectives
- Choose a sustainable sending frequency.
- Build a content mix that balances value and offers.
- Create a 90-day editorial calendar.
- Batch-produce emails efficiently.
:::

## Choosing a Cadence

**Cadence** is how often you send. The right cadence is the one you can sustain with consistent quality and that matches your subscription promise. Weekly is common for businesses with frequent news or educational content; biweekly or monthly suits slower-moving businesses. Consistency matters more than frequency: subscribers learn to expect your messages, and sudden bursts after long silences often produce complaints.

## The Content Mix

A practical guideline is to make most emails primarily valuable on their own (education, insight, useful news) and fewer primarily promotional. Value-first emails earn the attention that makes occasional offers effective.

| Content type | Example | Approximate share |
|---|---|---|
| Teach | How-to, checklist, explanation | 40% |
| Show | Behind the scenes, process, documented case | 20% |
| Curate | Useful resources from others | 15% |
| Connect | Stories, questions, subscriber replies | 10% |
| Offer | Product, service, event | 15% |

These proportions are a starting point, not a rule; adjust based on measured response (Chapter 10).

## Building a 90-Day Calendar

1. List the dates of known events: seasons, holidays relevant to your customers, product launches, and industry deadlines.
2. Assign one theme per month.
3. Draft weekly topics within each theme using the content mix.
4. Mark offer emails and space them out.
5. Leave one open slot per month for timely topics.

## Batch Production

Writing emails in batches reduces context switching. A common rhythm: one session per month to outline the next month's emails, one session per week to write and schedule.

:::example Illustrative: A Quarter for a Pet Groomer
Theme for October: "Fall coat care." Emails: (1) Teach: brushing routine for shedding season; (2) Show: a day in the grooming van; (3) Curate: three trusted articles on pet nutrition; (4) Offer: holiday appointment booking opens.
November: "Holiday safety." December: "Gifts and new-year routines."
Sending: weekly on Tuesday mornings. Offers: three across the quarter, each tied to a real calendar event.
:::

Month 3 theme: ___ Topics (weeks 1–4): ___

Batch writing sessions scheduled: ___

:::summary
Choose a cadence you can sustain and that matches your subscription promise. Lead with value-first content and space out offers. Build a 90-day calendar around real events and monthly themes, and batch production to reduce effort.

# Chapter 8: Segmentation and Automation

:::objectives
- Define segmentation and identify useful segmentation variables.
- Design tags and custom fields for a small business.
- Build three foundational automations.
- Avoid common automation errors.
:::

**Segmentation** is dividing your list into groups that receive different messages based on characteristics or behavior. **Automation** is sending messages automatically when a subscriber meets a condition. Together they let a small team send relevant email without manual effort.

## Segmentation Variables

| Variable | Example segments | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Interest | Residential vs. commercial | Signup form choice |
| Lifecycle stage | Subscriber, lead, customer, past customer | Purchase records |
| Behavior | Clicked a topic link | Link click tracking |
| Product owned | Bought service A vs. B | Purchase integration |
| Location | Service area A vs. B | Form field or ZIP code |
| Engagement | Engaged in last 90 days vs. not | Click and purchase activity |

Prefer behavior and purchase data over open data for segmentation, because opens are now unreliable (Chapter 10).

## Tags and Fields

A **tag** is a label applied to a subscriber (for example, "interest-commercial" or "customer-2026"). A **custom field** stores a value (for example, service-renewal date). Keep a written tag dictionary so names stay consistent.

## Three Foundational Automations

1. **Welcome sequence** (Chapter 5), triggered by subscribing.
2. **Customer onboarding**, triggered by purchase: thank you, what happens next, how to get help, request for feedback.
3. **Re-engagement**, triggered by inactivity (for example, no clicks in 120 days): a short series asking whether the subscriber still wants your email, followed by removal if they do not respond (Chapter 11).

## Common Automation Errors

- Sending sales emails to people who already bought.
- Overlapping automations that send several messages in one day.
- Broken personalization ("Hi {first_name}").
- Forgetting to update automations when offers or prices change.

Review every automation at least quarterly.

:::example Illustrative: Interest-Based Segmentation
An HVAC company adds one question to its signup form: "Are you a homeowner or a property manager?" Homeowners receive seasonal maintenance tips and residential service offers; property managers receive content on multi-unit maintenance scheduling and commercial service agreements. Both groups receive the same company news. The question takes one second to answer and makes every subsequent email more relevant.
:::

:::checklist Automation Audit
- Tag dictionary written and current.
- Welcome sequence active and tested.
- Customer onboarding automation built.
- Re-engagement automation built.
- Buyers excluded from sales sequences for products they own.
- No overlapping sends on the same day.
- Personalization fallbacks set.
- Automations reviewed this quarter.
:::

:::summary
Segmentation sends different messages to different groups; automation sends messages when conditions are met. Segment primarily on interests, lifecycle, behavior, and purchases. Build welcome, onboarding, and re-engagement automations first, maintain a tag dictionary, and audit automations quarterly to avoid errors.
:::

# Chapter 9: Offers, Launches, and Selling With Integrity

:::objectives
- Structure a launch sequence for a product or service.
- Use pricing tiers and deadlines honestly.
- Interpret launch results, including post-launch decay.
- Apply FTC disclosure principles to promotional email.
:::

Selling through email works best when the offer is a natural continuation of the value subscribers have already received. A **launch** is a concentrated period of promotion for a new or relaunched offer, typically with a defined open and close.

## The Launch Sequence

| Phase | Emails | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-launch | 2–4 over 1–2 weeks | Teach the problem; preview the solution; set the launch date |
| Open | 1 | Announce availability, price, and deadline |
| Middle | 1–3 | Answer questions; share details, samples, FAQs |
| Close | 1–2 | Honest reminder of the real deadline |
| Post-launch | 1 | Thank buyers; tell non-buyers what's next |

Let subscribers opt out of launch emails without unsubscribing entirely, for example with a "not interested in this offer" link that tags them and excludes them from the remaining launch messages.

## Pricing Tiers

Offering two or three packages lets buyers choose the level that fits. Nathan Barry reported that for one launch, sales were split roughly 48, 26, and 26 percent across three packages ([Nathan Barry, Self-Doubt Launch Stats](https://nathanbarry.com/self-doubt-launch-stats/)). Tiering should reflect genuinely different value, not artificial decoys.

## Deadlines and Scarcity

A real deadline, such as a launch discount ending or an enrollment closing because a cohort starts, helps people decide. A fake deadline that resets for every subscriber is deceptive. If you say a price ends Friday, it must end Friday.

:::case Launch, Decay, and Relaunch
Nathan Barry documented that after his first book's launch day of about $12,000, daily revenue fell to roughly $6,000 the next day and eventually to around $150 a day, a pattern he described as typical of launches ([Nathan Barry, Part 2](https://nathanbarry.com/39k-ebook-sales-part-2/)). By late 2013, sales of that book had declined to about $1,000 a month. He then released an updated iOS 7 edition with a co-author, which produced $36,297 in its first 24 hours ([Nathan Barry, App Design Stats](https://nathanbarry.com/app-design-stats/)). These are his documented results, not TTI's, and are not typical. The academic lesson is structural: launch revenue concentrates in a short window and decays, and refreshing an offer with genuinely new value can create a new launch event.
:::

## Disclosures and Claims

If your email includes affiliate links, sponsored content, or endorsements, disclose the relationship clearly and close to the claim. The FTC's guidance states that disclosures should be clear, conspicuous, and placed where consumers will notice them ([FTC, Disclosures 101](https://www.ftc.gov/node/60343)). Avoid earnings or results claims you cannot substantiate.

:::example Illustrative: A Service Launch
A personal trainer opens a six-week small-group program limited to 12 participants because of studio space.

- Pre-launch (two emails): "Why most January fitness plans fail by February" and "What the six-week program includes."
- Open: price ($240), start date, 12-spot limit (real), enrollment closes when full or on January 5.
- Middle: FAQ email (schedule, skill level, refunds).
- Close: "Enrollment closes Sunday at 8 p.m."
- Post-launch: participants receive onboarding; others are told about the spring session.
Result tracking: number of enrollments, revenue, unsubscribes, and spam complaints per email.
:::

:::checklist Ethical Launch
- Offer directly continues value already provided.
- Launch dates, prices, and deadlines are real.
- Scarcity claims are true and explainable.
- Subscribers can opt out of the launch without unsubscribing.
- Affiliate and sponsored relationships disclosed.
- No unsubstantiated results or income claims.
- Buyers removed from remaining sales emails.
- Post-launch message sent to buyers and non-buyers.
:::

:::summary
A launch moves from pre-launch education to open, middle, close, and post-launch messages. Tiered pricing should reflect real value, and deadlines and scarcity must be true. Launch revenue typically spikes and decays, as Nathan Barry's records show; refreshing an offer can create a new launch. Disclose material relationships clearly.
:::

# Chapter 10: Measurement in the Privacy Era

:::objectives
- Define key email metrics and calculate them.
- Explain why open rates are no longer reliable.
- Choose primary metrics that reflect business outcomes.
- Run a simple, valid A/B test.
:::

## Core Metrics

| Metric | Formula | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery rate | Delivered / Sent | Infrastructure and list health |
| Bounce rate | Bounced / Sent | List quality |
| Open rate | Opens / Delivered | Unreliable; directional at best |
| Click rate | Unique clickers / Delivered | Engagement with content |
| Click-to-open rate | Unique clickers / Unique opens | Distorted by inflated opens |
| Conversion rate | Conversions / Delivered (or clickers) | Business outcome |
| Unsubscribe rate | Unsubscribes / Delivered | Relevance and frequency fit |
| Spam complaint rate | Complaints / Delivered | Trust; deliverability risk |
| Revenue per email | Revenue attributed / Delivered | Monetary value of sends |
| List growth rate | (New − Lost) / Starting list | Program health |

## Why Opens Are Unreliable

Apple's Mail Privacy Protection, when enabled, "downloads remote content in the background by default — regardless of whether you engage with the email," and routes it through relays that hide the user's IP address ([Apple, Mail Privacy Protection](http://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/mail-privacy-protection/)). Because open tracking depends on loading a tiny remote image, this background loading can register an "open" whether or not a person read the message. Twilio SendGrid reported a 24.8 percent increase in unique opens across its customers in the weeks after the feature launched in September 2021 ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/resource-center/apple-mail-privacy-protection-email-senders-adapt)). Open rates therefore overstate attention, and subject-line tests based on opens are unreliable.

## Choosing Primary Metrics

For most small businesses, the primary metrics should be **clicks**, **conversions** (purchases, bookings, replies), **revenue per email**, **unsubscribes**, and **spam complaints**. Track opens only as a rough trend within a single audience over time.

## A/B Testing Basics

An **A/B test** sends two versions of one element to random portions of the list and compares outcomes.

1. Test one variable at a time (for example, the call-to-action wording).
2. Choose a metric that reflects the goal (clicks or conversions, not opens).
3. Ensure each group is large enough; small lists produce noisy results, so treat small-list tests as hints rather than proof.
4. Decide the evaluation rule before sending.
5. Record results in a test log.

:::example Illustrative: Calculating Metrics
A newsletter is sent to 2,000 subscribers.

- Delivered: 1,960 (bounces: 40; bounce rate 2.0%).
- Unique clickers: 98 (click rate 98 / 1,960 = 5.0%).
- Bookings: 7 (conversion rate per delivered 0.36%; per clicker 7.1%).
- Revenue from bookings: $1,050 (revenue per email delivered = $0.54).
- Unsubscribes: 6 (0.31%). Complaints: 1 (0.05%, below Google's 0.1% target).
- Reported open rate: 52%, treated as unreliable.
Conclusion: clicks and bookings are the meaningful signals; complaint rate is healthy.
:::

# Chapter 11: Protecting and Maintaining Your List

:::objectives
- Practice list hygiene, including bounce handling and sunsetting.
- Run a re-engagement campaign.
- Protect subscriber data and your ESP account.
- Back up and document your list.
:::

## List Hygiene

**List hygiene** is the routine removal of addresses that no longer belong on your list. Most ESPs automatically suppress **hard bounces** (permanent failures such as non-existent addresses). **Soft bounces** (temporary failures such as a full mailbox) should be suppressed if they persist. Unengaged subscribers, those who have not clicked or purchased in a long period, can harm deliverability because mailbox providers observe engagement.

## Sunsetting and Re-Engagement

A **sunset policy** defines when an inactive subscriber is removed. A common approach:

1. Define inactivity (for example, no clicks or purchases in 120–180 days, excluding unreliable opens).
2. Send a two- or three-email re-engagement series: "Do you still want these emails?" with a single click to stay.
3. Remove those who do not respond.

Gmail's guidance encourages senders to send at a volume recipients expect and to monitor spam rates closely ([Google, Top 10 Gmail Sender Issues](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/15256272?hl=en)); removing disengaged contacts supports both.

## Data Protection

Your list contains personal information. Protect it by:

- Enabling multi-factor authentication on your ESP and email accounts.
- Limiting user access to those who need it.
- Collecting only data you will use.
- Never exporting lists to unsecured personal devices or shared drives.
- Publishing a clear privacy policy that describes what you collect and how you use it.

The Verkada matter discussed in Chapter 2 included, alongside the CAN-SPAM penalty, an order requiring a comprehensive information security program and regular third-party assessments after alleged data security failures ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/295m-penalty-and-permanent-injunction-resolves-lawsuit-against-verkada-inc-alleged-unlawful)). Email programs live inside broader data-security obligations.

## Backups and Documentation

Export your list and consent records periodically (securely) so you can move providers or recover from account problems. Keep a record of where and when each subscriber opted in; your ESP usually stores this, but documentation helps if consent is ever questioned.

:::example Illustrative: A Sunset Campaign
A boutique with 3,200 subscribers identifies 900 with no clicks or purchases in 180 days.

- Email 1: "Still want our new-arrival emails?" One button: "Yes, keep me subscribed."
- Email 2 (five days later): "Last chance to stay on the list."
- Result: 140 click to stay. 760 are removed.
- After: the list is 2,580 subscribers; click rate per delivered rises because inactive addresses no longer dilute it, and spam complaints fall.
The smaller list costs less to host and performs better.
:::

:::checklist List Protection
- Hard bounces suppressed automatically.
- Persistent soft bounces suppressed.
- Sunset policy written with an inactivity definition.
- Re-engagement automation active.
- Multi-factor authentication enabled on ESP and email.
- User access limited and reviewed.
- Privacy policy published and current.
- Secure periodic export of list and consent records.
:::

:::summary
Maintain list hygiene by suppressing bounces and sunsetting inactive subscribers after a re-engagement attempt. Protect subscriber data with multi-factor authentication, access limits, data minimization, and a clear privacy policy, and keep secure backups of your list and consent records.
:::

# Chapter 12: Email for Service Businesses

:::objectives
- Use transactional email to support service delivery.
- Design review and referral requests that respect recipients.
- Connect email with appointment and front-office systems.
- Build a customer lifecycle email map.
:::

Many small businesses are service businesses: contractors, clinics, salons, agencies, and professional firms. For them, email's greatest value often lies not in newsletters but in the messages that support the customer journey.

## Transactional Email as Service

Appointment confirmations, reminders, preparation instructions, and follow-ups are transactional or relationship messages under CAN-SPAM when their primary purpose is to facilitate or complete an agreed transaction ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)). Keep them focused on the transaction. If you add promotional content, keep it secondary and below the transactional content, or the message may become commercial.

## The Customer Lifecycle Map

| Stage | Email | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Inquiry | Acknowledgment with next steps and response time | Relationship |
| Booking | Confirmation with date, time, address, preparation | Transactional |
| Reminder | 24–48 hours before | Transactional |
| Completion | Thank you, invoice or receipt, care instructions | Transactional |
| Feedback | Short survey or reply request | Relationship/commercial (treat as commercial if it promotes) |
| Review request | Invitation to leave an honest review | Treat as commercial; include opt-out |
| Retention | Maintenance reminders, seasonal tips | Commercial |
| Referral | Invitation to refer friends | Commercial |

## Reviews and Referrals, Ethically

Ask every customer, not only satisfied ones, for honest reviews; do not offer incentives conditioned on positive reviews; and do not write reviews yourself. Referral programs should disclose any rewards clearly.

## Connecting the Front Office

Email works best when it is connected to the systems where customer interactions begin: phone calls, web forms, and scheduling tools. When an inquiry arrives by phone or form, an acknowledgment email that confirms what was requested and what happens next reduces no-shows and repeat calls. Automate these acknowledgments, but ensure a human reviews exceptions.

:::example Illustrative: A Plumbing Company's Lifecycle
- Inquiry form: instant acknowledgment ("We received your request about a leaking water heater; we'll call within two business hours").
- Booking: confirmation with technician arrival window and parking instructions.
- Day before: reminder with a reschedule link.
- After service: receipt, warranty information, and a one-question feedback request.
- Three days later: review request with an opt-out link.
- Six months later: water heater flush reminder.
Each message has a single job and arrives when it is useful.
:::

# Chapter 13: Copywriting Frameworks and Storytelling

:::objectives
- Apply three classic copywriting frameworks to email.
- Use storytelling to make educational content memorable.
- Write calls to action that are specific and honest.
- Develop a consistent brand voice across emails.
:::

Chapter 6 covered the mechanics of a readable email. This chapter addresses structure at the level of argument: how to sequence ideas so a reader moves from attention to understanding to action. Copywriting frameworks are not formulas that guarantee results; they are checklists that help a writer avoid omitting something the reader needs.

## Framework 1: Problem, Agitate, Solve (PAS)

**PAS** names a problem the reader recognizes, explores its consequences so the reader feels its importance, and then presents a solution. It suits short promotional and educational emails. The ethical boundary lies in the "agitate" step: describe real consequences accurately, without exaggeration or fear tactics.

## Framework 2: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action (AIDA)

**AIDA**, one of the oldest models in advertising, moves the reader through four states. In email, the subject line and first sentence earn attention; the body builds interest with specifics; desire comes from showing the outcome and fit; and the call to action requests one step.

## Framework 3: Before, After, Bridge (BAB)

**BAB** describes the reader's current situation, a realistic picture of how things could be, and the bridge (your method, product, or service) that connects them. It suits case-based content and service descriptions.

| Framework | Best use | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| PAS | Short offers, problem-focused tips | Overstating the problem |
| AIDA | Launch announcements, product emails | Weak specifics in the interest step |
| BAB | Service explanations, documented cases | Promising an unrealistic "after" |

## Storytelling for Teaching

Stories help readers remember principles. A useful teaching story has a person with a goal, an obstacle, a decision, and a consequence, followed by the principle it illustrates. In your emails, distinguish carefully among three kinds of story:

1. **Your own experience,** told truthfully.
2. **Documented public cases,** attributed with a source, such as the Nathan Barry launches described in this book.
3. **Illustrative scenarios,** clearly labelled as hypothetical.

Never present a hypothetical as a real customer, and never imply that a documented case represents typical results.

## Calls to Action

A **call to action (CTA)** should tell the reader exactly what will happen when they act. "Learn more" is vague; "See the three package options and prices" is specific. Match the CTA's commitment to the reader's readiness: early in a relationship, ask for a reply or a click to an article; later, ask for a booking or purchase.

## Brand Voice

**Voice** is the consistent personality of your writing. Define it with three adjectives and their boundaries, for example "warm but not casual, expert but not technical, direct but not pushy." Write a short voice guide with sample sentences so anyone who drafts emails for your business sounds consistent.

:::example Illustrative: One Message, Three Frameworks
A bookkeeping firm wants to promote its monthly bookkeeping service.

- PAS: "Tax season shouldn't start with a shoebox of receipts. When books fall behind, owners lose deductions and spend weekends reconstructing records. Our monthly service reconciles your accounts every month so your books are ready when your accountant asks."
- AIDA: Subject "What clean books look like in April." Interest: three specifics of what monthly reconciliation includes. Desire: "Your accountant receives organized reports on day one." Action: "Book a 15-minute fit call."
- BAB: Before: "Receipts in three places and a bank account you haven't reconciled since summer." After: "A monthly report you can read in five minutes." Bridge: "Our monthly bookkeeping service."
Each version is truthful, specific, and asks for one action.
:::

- "Re:" or "Fwd:" when the email is not a reply or forward.
- "Your account" or "Invoice" on promotional messages.
- Urgency that is not real ("Last chance!" for an offer that does not end).
- All capitals and excessive punctuation.

# Glossary

**A/B test.** A comparison of two versions of one element sent to random list segments.

**Automation.** Email sent automatically when a subscriber meets a condition.

**Bounce (hard/soft).** Undeliverable email; hard bounces are permanent, soft bounces temporary.

**CAN-SPAM Act.** U.S. federal law governing commercial email.

**CASL.** Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation, which requires consent for commercial electronic messages.

**Cadence.** Sending frequency.

**Call to action (CTA).** The specific step an email asks the reader to take.

**Click rate.** Unique clickers divided by delivered emails.

**Commercial message.** Email whose primary purpose is advertising or promoting a product or service.

**DKIM.** A cryptographic signature verifying a message's domain and integrity.

**DMARC.** A policy telling receivers how to handle authentication failures and providing reports.

**Double opt-in.** A subscription confirmed by clicking a link in a confirmation email.

**Deliverability.** The likelihood that email reaches the inbox.

**ESP.** Email service provider.

**Lead magnet.** A free resource offered in exchange for an email address.

**List hygiene.** Removing addresses that no longer belong on the list.

**Mail Privacy Protection (MPP).** Apple feature that preloads remote content and hides IP addresses, inflating opens.

**PAS, AIDA, BAB.** Copywriting frameworks: Problem-Agitate-Solve; Attention-Interest-Desire-Action; Before-After-Bridge.

**Segmentation.** Dividing a list into groups for different messages.

**SPF.** A DNS record listing servers authorized to send mail for a domain.

**Spam complaint rate.** Complaints divided by delivered emails.

**Subscription promise.** A statement of what subscribers will receive, how often, and why.

**Sunset policy.** Rules for removing inactive subscribers.

**Transactional message.** Email that facilitates or completes an agreed transaction.
