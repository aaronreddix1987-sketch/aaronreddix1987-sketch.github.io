# How to Use This Book

Most small-business owners wear five hats at once: marketer, salesperson, scheduler, bookkeeper, and chief executive. The promise of "AI agents" is that some of the repetitive typing, sorting, and reminding inside those roles can be drafted by software while the owner keeps every decision.

The kit is organized around a simple picture: four AI-assisted desks (Marketing, Sales and Follow-Up, Operations and Money, and a CEO desk that summarizes the others) with a human owner who approves anything that leaves the building.

By the end, you should be able to describe which repetitive tasks in your business are suitable for AI drafting, draw a one-page org chart that shows where human approval is required, write clear handoff cards for your first three AI jobs, run a five-minute morning report, and name the decisions that must always stay with a person.

# Chapter 1: The Four-Desk Picture

:::objectives
- Explain what an "AI agent" means in a small business, in plain terms.
- Group your recurring work into four desks.
:::

## An agent is a job description, not a robot

At the start, an AI "agent" in a small business does not need to be sophisticated software. It is three things: one clearly written job description, one saved prompt or tool that does the first draft, and one human checklist that decides whether the draft goes out. If any of the three is missing, you do not have an agent; you have an experiment that nobody is supervising.

## The four desks

**Marketing desk.** Drafts social posts, newsletter outlines, website updates, and responses to common questions. The owner approves anything published.

**Sales and Follow-Up desk.** Drafts first replies to inquiries, quote cover notes, and polite follow-ups that stop when a buyer says no. The owner approves prices and commitments.

**Operations and Money desk.** Drafts schedules, job checklists, invoice reminders, and expense summaries. The owner approves payments, refunds, and anything involving bank details.

**CEO desk.** Reads short outputs from the other three and produces a one-page morning summary: new leads, open quotes, today's jobs, money in and out, and decisions waiting. The owner makes the decisions.

:::case Evidence on AI assistance at work
Economists Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond studied the staggered rollout of a generative AI assistant to 5,172 customer-support agents at a software company. Access to the tool increased the number of customer issues resolved per hour by about 15 percent on average, with the largest gains among newer and less experienced workers and little effect for the most experienced [1]. The tool suggested responses; human agents decided what to send. The lesson: the most credible evidence so far comes from setups where AI drafts and people decide. The company's results are its own, not TTI's, and your results may differ.
:::

## The four-desk framework

**Step 1: List every recurring task** you did in the last two weeks, however small.

**Step 2: Sort each task into a desk** (Marketing, Sales and Follow-Up, Operations and Money, CEO) or mark it "Human only."

**Step 3: Mark each task as drafting, sorting, reminding, or deciding.** Drafting, sorting, and reminding are candidates for AI help; deciding stays with you.

**Step 4: Estimate the minutes per week** each task takes.

**Step 5: Circle low-risk, high-minute tasks** for Chapter 3.

:::example Illustrative: a two-person cleaning company
A hypothetical cleaning company lists 22 recurring tasks. Sorting them shows 6 marketing, 5 sales, 8 operations and money, and 3 owner decisions. Of these, 11 involve drafting, sorting, or reminding and together take an estimated 7 hours a week. The owner circles three: drafting replies to quote requests (90 minutes), writing the weekly schedule summary (60 minutes), and drafting overdue-invoice reminders (45 minutes). All figures are invented.
:::

|---|---|---|
| Marketing | Post drafts, FAQ answers, newsletter outlines | Anything published |
| Sales and Follow-Up | First replies, quote notes, follow-ups | Prices, promises, discounts |
| Operations and Money | Schedules, checklists, reminders | Payments, refunds, bank changes |
| CEO | Morning summary of the other desks | Every decision |

# Chapter 2: The One-Page Org Chart and Approval Gates

:::objectives
- Draw an org chart that shows who drafts and who approves.
- Define approval gates for messages, money, and promises.
:::

An approval gate is where an AI draft stops until a named person reviews it. The most useful gates sit where errors are costly: anything a customer will read, anything involving money, and anything that commits the business to a price, date, refund, or policy.

:::case Air Canada and its chatbot
In 2024, a British Columbia tribunal ordered Air Canada to pay a customer about $812 after its website chatbot gave him wrong bereavement-fare information. The tribunal rejected the airline's argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own statements and held the company responsible for all information on its website [2][3]. An AI draft becomes your statement once a customer sees it. Air Canada's experience is its own, not TTI's.
:::

## Building the org chart

**Step 1: Put yourself at the top** as owner and final approver.

**Step 2: Draw the four desks beneath you** with each desk's tool or saved prompt and its outputs.

**Step 3: Assign a gate and run time to each output,** such as "owner approves, 8:00 a.m. daily," and post the chart.

:::example Illustrative: gates for a handyman business
A hypothetical handyman requires approval for quote replies and spot-checks one in five template confirmation texts. In week one he edits 9 of 20 quote drafts, mostly guessed prices, so he gives the tool a price sheet rather than removing the gate. All figures are invented.
:::

| Gate level | Use for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Owner approves | Customer messages, money, promises | Quote replies, refund notes |
| Owner spot-checks | Fixed templates with low risk | Appointment confirmations |
| No review | Internal drafts only | Tomorrow's materials list |
| Human only | Decisions AI must not make | Signing contracts, paying bills |

Gates require a named person to review drafts before they reach customers or touch money. You are responsible for what your AI tools say; an org chart makes gates visible.

# Chapter 3: Choosing and Handing Off Your First Three Jobs

:::objectives
- Audit your week to find good first AI jobs.
- Write a handoff card with inputs, outputs, and checks.
- Use a simple starter prompt for each job.
:::

## Start small and boring

Good first jobs are repetitive, low-risk, and easy to check. Drafting a reply to a common question is a good first job; deciding whether to give a customer a refund is not. Starting with three jobs, rather than ten, keeps the review load manageable and lets you learn how the tools behave before you trust them with more.

:::case Dan Martell's time audit and handoffs
Entrepreneur and author Dan Martell describes a "Buyback Loop" for delegation: audit where your time goes, transfer the tasks that drain you, and fill the freed time with higher-value work. One method he describes is to record yourself doing a repetitive task so that the recording becomes the training material for whoever takes it over [4]. He has also described a "1-3-1" rule for team members: bring one problem, three possible options, and one recommendation, rather than an open question [5]. Both ideas translate well to AI handoffs: a recorded or written example of the task, and a request for options with a recommendation that the owner then decides on. Martell's results are his own, not TTI's.
:::

## The handoff method

**Step 1: Review a typical week** using your task list from Chapter 1.

**Step 2: Score each candidate** from 1 to 5 on repetition, minutes, and ease of checking, and from 1 to 5 on risk (5 means risky).

**Step 3: Choose three jobs** with high repetition and minutes, easy checking, and low risk.

**Step 4: Record or write one good example** of each job done well.

**Step 5: Fill in a handoff card** listing inputs, the expected output, the gate, and what "done" looks like.

**Step 6: Save a starter prompt** for each job and test it on five real past examples before using it live.

:::example Illustrative: scoring three candidates
A hypothetical bookkeeper scores "draft monthly client reminder emails" at repetition 5, minutes 4, ease 5, risk 1. "Summarize unread emails each morning" scores 5, 3, 4, 2. "Decide which clients get a fee waiver" scores 2, 1, 2, 5 and is marked human only. The first two become her first AI jobs, along with "draft a weekly social post from a blog article." All numbers are illustrative.
:::

## A starter handoff prompt

> You are helping [business name], a [type of business] in [area]. Your job is to [task]. Use only the information I provide below and the rules sheet. If information is missing, write "[NEEDS OWNER]" instead of guessing. Do not include prices, dates, or promises unless they appear in the information provided. Give me one draft of no more than [length], then list anything you were unsure about.

| Handoff card field | What to write | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Job name | Short label | Quote-reply draft |
| Inputs | What the tool receives | Inquiry text, price sheet |
| Output | What you get back | Reply under 120 words |
| Gate | Who approves | Owner approves |
| Done means | How you judge quality | Correct facts, friendly tone |

Done means: ___

:::

:::summary
Good first AI jobs are repetitive, low-risk, and easy to check. A handoff card states inputs, outputs, gates, and what done means. Delegation ideas from Dan Martell, such as recording a task and asking for options with a recommendation, translate well to AI-assisted work.

# Chapter 4: The Morning One-Page Report

:::objectives
- Design a five-minute morning report from the four desks.
- Separate reversible decisions from hard-to-reverse ones.
- Keep meetings and reports short.
:::

## One page, every morning

The CEO desk's job is to turn yesterday's activity into a single page you can read with your first coffee. A good report has five sections: new leads, open quotes, today's jobs, money in and out, and decisions waiting for you. Each line should be short and link back to its source so you can check it, because summaries can drop or misstate details.

The report is useful only if it leads to decisions. Many owners find it helpful to sort the "decisions waiting" section into two groups: decisions that are easy to reverse, which can be made quickly, and decisions that are hard to reverse, which deserve more care.

:::case Amazon's two types of decisions
In his 2016 letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos distinguished between "Type 1" decisions, which are consequential and hard to reverse, and "Type 2" decisions, which are changeable and reversible. He argued that most decisions are Type 2 and should be made quickly by individuals or small groups, and that many decisions can be made with about 70 percent of the information one might wish for [6]. In the same spirit of protecting focus, Shopify in early 2023 used software to remove recurring meetings with three or more people from employee calendars; its chief operating officer told NPR that about 322,000 hours of meetings were deleted in the first month [7]. These are large companies, and their results are their own, not TTI's, but the principles of quick reversible decisions and fewer meetings scale down well to a morning report.
:::

## Building the report

**Step 1: Choose the five sections** listed above.

**Step 2: Decide the sources** for each section, such as your inbox, calendar, and invoicing tool.

**Step 3: Write a morning brief prompt** that asks for no more than one page and links to sources.

**Step 4: Mark each decision** as reversible or hard to reverse.

**Step 5: Spend five minutes** reading, deciding the reversible items, and scheduling time for the rest.

**Step 6: Spot-check one item a day** against its source.

:::example Illustrative: a Tuesday report
A hypothetical landscaper's report shows 4 new leads, 6 open quotes (2 older than seven days), 3 jobs today, $1,850 in payments received, and $640 in bills due. Decisions waiting: approve two follow-up drafts (reversible), choose a new mower supplier (hard to reverse). He approves the drafts in two minutes and blocks 30 minutes on Thursday for the supplier decision. All figures are invented.
:::

> Morning brief prompt: Using only the notes pasted below, write a one-page summary with five headings: New leads, Open quotes, Today's jobs, Money in and out, Decisions waiting. Under Decisions waiting, label each item "reversible" or "hard to reverse." Do not invent numbers; if a figure is missing, write "[CHECK]".

| Section | Source | Typical line |
|---|---|---|
| New leads | Inbox, phone log | Name, service, received time |
| Open quotes | Quote tracker | Amount, days open, next touch |
| Today's jobs | Calendar | Time, address, crew |
| Money in and out | Invoicing, bank alerts | Received, due, overdue |
| Decisions waiting | All desks | Item, reversible or hard |

:::summary
A one-page morning report from the CEO desk turns scattered activity into decisions. Sorting decisions into reversible and hard-to-reverse groups, an idea Bezos described publicly, speeds up routine choices while protecting important ones. Spot-checking keeps AI summaries honest.

# Chapter 5: What AI Cannot Do, and a Five-Day Rollout

:::objectives
- Name decisions and actions that must stay with a person.
- Protect customer data and guard against payment fraud.
- Put the kit to work over five days.
:::

## Human-only lines

Some actions should never be delegated to an AI tool, no matter how capable it seems: signing contracts, filing legal or tax documents, verifying identity or bank details, changing where payments go, making licensed professional judgments, and approving refunds or discounts. These are the places where mistakes are expensive, hard to reverse, and clearly your responsibility.

Customer information also needs care. The Federal Trade Commission's guidance for businesses recommends knowing what personal information you hold, keeping only what you need, protecting it, and disposing of it properly [8]. Before pasting customer details into any AI tool, check the tool's data settings and remove anything you do not need, such as full account numbers.

Phone and text outreach has its own rules. The Federal Communications Commission has confirmed that the Telephone Consumer Protection Act's restrictions apply to calls using AI-generated voices [9]. Any claims you make in marketing, including AI-drafted ones, must be truthful and supported, as the FTC's advertising guidance explains [10].

:::case Business email compromise
The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reported that business email compromise, in which criminals impersonate a trusted contact to redirect payments, accounted for about $2.77 billion in reported losses in 2024 [11]. A simple rule protects a small business: never change payment details based on an email or text alone; confirm by calling a number you already have on file. These figures describe reported crime nationally, not TTI's experience.
:::

## The five-day rollout

**Step 1 (Day 1): Sort your tasks** into the four desks using the Chapter 1 worksheet (20 minutes).

**Step 2 (Day 2): Draw your org chart** and set gates (20 minutes).

**Step 3 (Day 3): Write three handoff cards** and test each prompt on five past examples (20 minutes).

**Step 4 (Day 4): Run your first morning report** and spot-check one item (20 minutes).

**Step 5 (Day 5): Review the week.** Note editing rates, errors caught, and minutes spent, and decide whether to keep, fix, or drop each job (20 minutes).

:::example Illustrative: the Day 5 review
A hypothetical pet groomer reviews her first week. The reply-draft job needed edits on 6 of 15 drafts, mostly tone; she adds two sample replies to the prompt. The schedule summary needed no edits on 4 of 5 days; she moves it to spot-check. The social post job took longer than writing posts herself; she drops it for now. All numbers are invented.
:::

| Action | AI may draft? | Who decides |
|---|---|---|
| Signing a contract | No | Owner |
| Changing payment details | No | Owner, after phone verification |
| Tax or legal filing | No | Owner or licensed professional |
| Refund or discount | Draft note only | Owner |
| Marketing claim | Yes, with review | Owner verifies facts |

:::checklist Human-only and safety checklist
- [ ] Human-only actions are listed on the org chart.
- [ ] Payment changes are verified by phone to a known number.
- [ ] Customer data pasted into AI tools is minimized.
- [ ] Marketing claims are checked for truth and support.
- [ ] Calls and texts follow consent rules.
:::

:::summary
Contracts, filings, identity and bank checks, payment changes, licensed judgments, and refunds stay with people. Protect customer data, verify payment changes by phone, and follow call, text, and advertising rules. A five-day rollout of about 20 minutes a day lets you try the system and keep only what works.
:::

# Appendix A: Copy-Ready Templates

**Desk job description template:**

> Desk: [name]. Purpose: [one sentence]. Inputs: [list]. Outputs: [list]. Gate: [owner approves / spot-check / no review]. Run time: [schedule]. Never: [human-only items].

**Where to go next.** Once three jobs run smoothly for two weeks, consider adding one more job, not five. Revisit your gates monthly; loosen a gate only when the editing rate has stayed low for several weeks, and never loosen gates on money or promises.

# Glossary

**AI agent.** In this kit, a job description, a saved prompt or tool, and a human checklist working together.

**Approval gate.** A point where an AI draft must be reviewed by a named person before going further.

**Business email compromise.** Fraud in which criminals impersonate a trusted contact to redirect payments.

**Desk.** A grouping of related recurring tasks, such as Marketing or Operations and Money.

**Editing rate.** The share of AI drafts a person has to change before use.

**Handoff card.** A short written specification of a job's inputs, outputs, gate, and quality standard.

**Morning report.** A one-page daily summary of leads, quotes, jobs, money, and decisions.

**Reversible decision.** A decision that can be changed easily if it proves wrong (Type 2).

**Spot-check.** Reviewing a sample of outputs rather than every one.
