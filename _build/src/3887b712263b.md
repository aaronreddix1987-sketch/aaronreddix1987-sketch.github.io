# How to Use This Book

Most small businesses do not have a marketing department, an agency retainer, or a large advertising budget. They have an owner with limited hours, a modest amount of cash, and a pressing need for more customers. This book is written for them: solo operators, tradespeople, local service companies, consultants, and early-stage firms that need marketing routines that protect cash flow while producing real inquiries and sales.

The central argument is simple. Marketing on a lean budget is not about finding clever free tricks. It is about earning evidence before spending money. A business that can explain one offer clearly, answer its phone, follow up on every inquiry, and show honest proof of its work will get more from every marketing dollar than a business that buys advertising to promote a confusing offer. Paid traffic amplifies whatever is already there, good or bad.

The U.S. Small Business Administration recommends that every business prepare a marketing plan describing its target market, competitive advantage, sales plan, goals, action plan, and budget, and that owners compare marketing costs to the revenue those efforts generate [1]. This book turns that advice into a practical sequence a busy owner can follow.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Explain the basic economics of customer acquisition, including cost per lead, close rate, gross margin, and payback.
- Write a single, clear offer that a stranger understands in fifteen seconds.
- Audit your website, phone, booking, and payment routes so no inquiry is lost.
- Set up and maintain a complete Google Business Profile.
- Request reviews and referrals in ways that comply with platform policies and FTC rules.
- Choose two low-cost marketing channels and commit to them.
- Create content that answers buyers' real questions.
- Conduct polite, compliant direct outreach and build referral partnerships.
- Build and use a small email list.
- Measure results with a simple channel scoreboard.
- Decide when and how to spend your first $100 on advertising.
- Run a 14-day lean marketing sprint and a 90-day follow-on plan.

# Chapter 1: What Lean Marketing Means

:::objectives
- Define lean marketing as evidence-first customer acquisition.
- Calculate cost per lead, cost per customer, and payback.
- Distinguish activity metrics from evidence of demand.
- Set a realistic marketing budget tied to gross margin.
:::

Lean marketing borrows an idea from lean management: eliminate waste by learning quickly from real customer behavior. In marketing, waste takes familiar forms: advertising that sends people to a page that does not explain the offer; social posts with no call to action; agency retainers paid before the business knows what customers want; and inquiries that go unanswered for days. A lean approach fixes those leaks first, then spends money where evidence shows it will return.

## The Economics of Acquiring a Customer

Every marketing decision eventually reduces to a few numbers:

- **Cost per lead (CPL).** Marketing spend divided by the number of qualified inquiries.
- **Close rate.** The percentage of qualified inquiries that become paying customers.
- **Cost per customer (CAC).** Marketing spend divided by new customers, or CPL divided by close rate.
- **Gross profit per customer.** Revenue from a customer minus the direct costs of serving them.
- **Payback.** Whether the gross profit from a customer covers the cost of acquiring them, and how quickly.

A channel is worth expanding only if gross profit per customer comfortably exceeds cost per customer. Repeat business and referrals improve the picture, because a customer acquired once may buy many times.

## Activity Versus Evidence

Likes, views, followers, and impressions measure activity. They are not demand. Evidence of demand is a buyer action that costs the buyer something: a phone call, a quote request, a booked appointment, a deposit, or a cleared payment. Lean marketing counts evidence first.

## Setting a Budget

There is no universal rule for marketing spend. A practical approach for a small business is to start with time rather than cash, then commit a small, fixed test budget that you can afford to lose, and only increase it when the scoreboard shows that a channel pays back. The SBA advises including a complete breakdown of marketing costs in the plan and tracking them once the plan is in action [1].

:::case River Pools and Spas: Cutting Ad Spend During a Crisis
In early 2009, River Pools and Spas, a 20-employee installer of fiberglass pools in Virginia and Maryland, saw orders fall from about six a month to barely two, according to The New York Times [2]. Four customers demanded back deposits on pools costing more than $50,000 each, and the company overdrew its bank account three weeks in a row [2]. The company had been spending about $250,000 a year on radio, television, and pay-per-click advertising; owner Marcus Sheridan cut that budget to about a tenth and focused instead on informational blog posts and videos that answered customers' questions [2]. The Times reported that the company recovered to exceed its peak pre-2007 revenue [2]. The case shows that a small company can replace expensive advertising with useful, question-answering content, though it took sustained effort, and River Pools' results are its own.
:::

:::example Teresa's Channel Math (illustrative)
Teresa spent $600 last quarter on a local mailer that produced 15 calls. Of those, 9 were qualified and 4 booked. CPL = $600 / 9 = $66.67. Close rate = 4 / 9 = 44 percent. CAC = $600 / 4 = $150. Gross profit per first job = $285 x 0.55 = $156.75. The mailer barely paid back on the first job ($156.75 vs. $150). However, about half of her customers rebook within a year, so the expected gross profit per customer over twelve months is closer to $235. The mailer is marginal, not a failure, but she has no evidence yet that a cheaper channel would do better. That is what this book's tests will find out.
:::

# Chapter 2: One Offer, One Buyer

:::objectives
- Choose one primary offer to market first.
- Define the buyer, problem, scope, price, and next step.
- Write a one-sentence offer that passes a 15-second clarity test.
- Remove words that confuse or overpromise.
:::

Many small businesses market everything they do to everyone they might serve. The result is a message that no one remembers. On a lean budget, focus is a financial decision: one clear offer to one defined buyer lets every post, call, and dollar reinforce the same idea.

## Step 1: Choose the Lead Offer

Select the offer that is most profitable, most frequently requested, and easiest to explain. It does not need to be your only service; it is the one you lead with. Other services can be offered once the customer relationship begins.

## Step 2: Define the Buyer

Describe the buyer specifically: who they are, where they are, what triggers their need, and what they worry about when choosing a provider. "Homeowners" is too broad. "Owners of two-story homes within 15 miles who want spring window cleaning but do not want to climb ladders" is useful.

## Step 3: Define Problem, Scope, Price, and Next Step

- **Problem:** in the buyer's words.
- **Scope:** what is and is not included.
- **Price:** a price, a starting price, or a clear range.
- **Next step:** exactly what the buyer should do (call, text, book online, request a quote).

Publishing a price or range is uncomfortable for many owners, but buyers look for it. When River Pools began answering customer questions online, the first question it addressed was how much a fiberglass pool costs [3].

## Step 4: Write the One-Sentence Offer

A useful template: "We help [buyer] [achieve result] with [service], starting at [price]. [Next step]."

## Step 5: Run the 15-Second Clarity Test

Show the sentence, or your homepage, to five people who do not know your business for 15 seconds. Ask: What do we sell? Who is it for? What does it cost? What should you do next? If fewer than four of five can answer, revise.

:::example Before and After (illustrative)
Teresa's old homepage headline read: "Brightline: Excellence in Exterior Property Care Solutions." In a 15-second test, two of five people guessed she did landscaping. Her revised headline reads: "Streak-free window and gutter cleaning for two-story homes in [City]. Most homes $245 to $345. Text a photo for a quote in one hour." Five of five people answered all four questions correctly. Nothing about her service changed; only the clarity of the message did.
:::

| Element | Weak version | Clear version |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer | Everyone | Two-story homeowners within 15 miles |
| Problem | Property care | Dirty windows and clogged gutters; ladder risk |
| Scope | Full service | Interior and exterior glass, screens, gutter clearing |
| Price | Call for pricing | Most homes $245 to $345 |
| Next step | Contact us | Text a photo for a quote in one hour |

## Words to Remove

Remove vague superlatives ("best," "world-class"), jargon, and promises you cannot substantiate. The FTC requires that advertising be truthful and not misleading, and objective claims need evidence. "Licensed and insured" is a checkable fact; "the best in town" is an opinion that adds little.

:::summary
Focus on one lead offer for one defined buyer. Specify problem, scope, price, and next step, and write a one-sentence offer. Test it on strangers for 15 seconds and revise until four of five understand it. Publishing prices and removing vague or unsubstantiated words makes the message clearer and more credible.

# Chapter 3: The Owned Foundation

:::objectives
- Identify the owned assets every business needs before promoting itself.
- Audit the website, phone, booking, and payment routes end to end.
- Set up a lead log and a response standard.
- Fix leaks before sending traffic.
:::

Owned assets are the parts of your marketing you control: your website, phone number, email address, booking or quote form, payment link, and customer list. Social platforms and directories are useful, but they can change rules or reach at any time. Before spending time or money to attract attention, make sure the owned foundation captures every inquiry and turns it into a conversation.

## Step 1: The Website or Landing Page

A small business does not need a large website. It needs a page that loads quickly on a phone, states the one-sentence offer, shows proof of real work, lists the service area, displays price guidance, and makes the next step obvious. Contact details should be tappable on mobile.

## Step 2: The Phone and Text Route

Many local buyers call or text. Every missed call is a potential lost customer. Set a voicemail that tells callers when they will hear back, check messages at set times each day, and consider a call-forwarding or answering arrangement during jobs. If you use automated texting, follow consent rules; the Federal Communications Commission explains that autodialed and prerecorded calls and texts to mobile phones generally require the recipient's prior consent [4].

## Step 3: The Booking or Quote Route

Whether buyers book online, submit a form, or text photos, test the route. Does the form submit? Where does it go? Does someone receive a notification? Does the buyer get a confirmation?

## Step 4: The Payment Route

If you collect deposits or payments online, run a test transaction. Check that the amount, description, receipt, and refund route are correct.

## Step 5: The Lead Log and Response Standard

Record every inquiry in a simple log: date, name, contact, source, service requested, status, and outcome. Set a response standard, for example: every inquiry receives a reply within one business hour during working hours. Speed matters; buyers who contact several providers often choose the first to respond helpfully.

:::example Foundation Audit (illustrative)
Teresa asks a friend to act as a buyer. The friend finds that (1) the website takes eight seconds to load on a phone because of large photos; (2) the contact form sends to an old email address no one checks; (3) voicemail says only "leave a message"; and (4) the online deposit link shows the wrong business name. Teresa compresses photos (load time drops to under three seconds), redirects the form to her current inbox and phone, records a voicemail promising a text reply within one hour, and fixes the payment profile. She later finds 11 form submissions from the previous four months in the old inbox, roughly $3,100 in potential jobs at her average ticket. None of these fixes cost money.
:::

| Asset | Test | Pass standard |
|---|---|---|
| Website | Open on a phone over cellular data | Loads in about 3 seconds; offer visible without scrolling |
| Phone | Call during a job | Clear voicemail with reply time; message checked |
| Text | Send a test text | Reply within the response standard |
| Form | Submit a test inquiry | Owner notified; buyer gets confirmation |
| Payment | Make a small test payment | Correct name, amount, receipt |
| Lead log | Review last 10 inquiries | Every inquiry has source and outcome |

:::checklist Owned Foundation Audit
- One-sentence offer visible at the top of the homepage.
- Phone number and text option tappable on mobile.
- Service area and price guidance stated.
- Photos of real work, with permission where people appear.
- Contact form tested and routed to a monitored inbox.
- Voicemail states when callers will hear back.
- Payment or deposit link tested.
- Lead log set up with source and outcome columns.
- Response standard written and shared with anyone who answers inquiries.
- Texting practices reviewed for consent requirements.
:::

:::summary
Owned assets are the parts of marketing you control. Before attracting attention, test the website, phone, text, booking, and payment routes end to end, and keep a lead log with a written response standard. Many leaks can be fixed at no cost, and fixing them improves the return on every later marketing effort.
:::

# Chapter 4: Google Business Profile

:::objectives
- Explain how Google ranks local results.
- Set up and complete a Google Business Profile.
- Maintain the profile with photos, updates, and review replies.
- Track profile performance.
:::

For local service businesses, a Google Business Profile is often the highest-value free marketing asset available. It allows a business to appear in Google Search and Maps results when nearby customers search for a service. It costs nothing to create, but it rewards completeness and maintenance.

## How Local Ranking Works

Google states that local results are based mainly on relevance, distance, and prominence [5]. Relevance is how well a profile matches what someone searches for; distance is how far the business is from the searcher; prominence is how well known the business is, based partly on information such as how many websites link to it and how many reviews it has [5]. Google also states that there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [5].

The practical implication: you cannot control distance, but you can improve relevance by providing complete and accurate information, and you can build prominence over time through reviews, links, and a genuine reputation.

## Step 1: Claim and Verify

Claim the profile and complete Google's verification process. Google notes that verification tells it you are authorized to represent the business and makes the profile more likely to appear in results [5].

## Step 2: Complete Every Relevant Field

Google advises that businesses with complete and accurate information are more likely to appear in local results [5]. Complete: business name (as used in the real world, without added keywords), primary and secondary categories, service area or address, hours, phone, website, services with descriptions and price guidance, and attributes.

## Step 3: Add Photos and Updates

Add real photos of your work, team, and vehicles. Google suggests photos and videos help tell the business's story [5]. Post occasional updates such as seasonal offers, completed projects, or answers to common questions.

## Step 4: Respond to Reviews

Google notes that replying to reviews shows customers you value their feedback [5]. Reply to every review, positive or negative, promptly and professionally. Thank positive reviewers specifically. For negative reviews, acknowledge the concern, avoid arguing, and offer to resolve it offline.

## Step 5: Track Performance

Review profile performance monthly: searches, views, calls, website clicks, and direction requests. Compare these with inquiries in your lead log.

:::example Profile Rebuild (illustrative)
Teresa's profile listed only a phone number, the category "Cleaning service," and three blurry photos. She changes the primary category to "Window cleaning service," adds "Gutter cleaning service" as a secondary category, defines a service area of 12 nearby ZIP codes, lists four services with price ranges, uploads 24 before-and-after photos, and replies to all 17 existing reviews. Over the next 60 days, calls from the profile rise from 9 to 21 per month, according to her profile's performance data. She notes that spring is her busy season, so part of the increase is seasonal; she will compare with the same months next year before drawing firm conclusions.
:::

:::case Google's Own Guidance as the Primary Source
Many vendors sell "local SEO" services with confident claims about ranking secrets. Google's published guidance is more modest: complete and accurate information, verification, review responses, photos, and genuine prominence [5]. It explicitly states that ranking cannot be bought and that algorithm details are kept confidential to keep the system fair [5]. For a lean-budget business, this is useful: the most important actions are free and within the owner's control. Be cautious of anyone guaranteeing a specific ranking.
:::

| Profile element | Action | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Verification | Complete Google's process | More likely to appear in results |
| Categories | Choose the most specific accurate category | Relevance |
| Service area or address | Accurate and specific | Distance matching |
| Services and prices | List with descriptions | Relevance; buyer clarity |
| Photos | Real work, regularly added | Trust; engagement |
| Reviews | Request ethically; reply to all | Prominence; trust |
| Hours | Keep current, including holidays | Avoid frustrated buyers |

:::checklist Profile Completion Check
- Profile claimed and verified.
- Business name matches real-world signage and documents.
- Most specific primary category selected.
- Service area or address accurate.
- Hours current, including holidays.
- Services listed with descriptions and price guidance.
- At least 10 real photos uploaded.
- All existing reviews answered.
- Monthly performance review scheduled.
:::

:::summary
A Google Business Profile is a free, high-value asset for local businesses. Google ranks local results by relevance, distance, and prominence and states that ranking cannot be bought. Verify the profile, complete every relevant field, add real photos, reply to all reviews, and review performance monthly against your lead log.
:::

# Chapter 5: Reviews and Referrals, Honestly

:::objectives
- Explain the rules governing reviews and testimonials.
- Build a compliant routine for requesting reviews.
- Design a simple, honest referral program.
- Use testimonials in marketing without misleading buyers.
:::

Reviews and referrals are among the most persuasive forms of marketing because they come from customers rather than the business. They are also regulated, both by platforms and by law. A lean business should build a steady, honest flow of both.

## The Rules

Google's policy for Maps reviews prohibits fake engagement and rating manipulation. It does not allow businesses to offer incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods or services in exchange for reviews, to discourage negative reviews, or to selectively solicit positive reviews from customers [6]. It does allow businesses to ask customers for genuine reviews [6].

The FTC's Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, effective October 21, 2024, prohibits fake or false reviews and testimonials, buying reviews, conditioning incentives on a review expressing a particular sentiment, and certain review suppression practices [7]. The FTC's Endorsement Guides reflect the basic principle that endorsements must be honest and not misleading and must reflect the honest opinions of the endorser [8].

In practice:

- Ask all customers for honest reviews, not only happy ones.
- Do not offer discounts or gifts in exchange for Google reviews.
- Never write reviews yourself or ask staff, family, or friends to post them.
- Do not pressure customers or tell them what to write.
- Respond to negative reviews professionally rather than trying to have them removed for being negative.

## Building a Review Routine

1. Deliver excellent work and confirm the customer is satisfied.
2. Within 24 hours, send a short message thanking them and including a direct link to your review page.
3. Send one polite reminder after a few days if they have not responded.
4. Thank every reviewer publicly in your reply.

Because Google prohibits selectively soliciting positive reviews, send the request to every completed customer, not only those you expect to be happy [6].

## Referral Programs

Referrals differ from reviews. A referral program rewards customers for introducing new customers, not for posting public opinions. A simple program might offer a credit to the referring customer when a referred friend books. Be clear about the terms and make sure referrers who post publicly about the reward disclose it, consistent with the FTC's emphasis on disclosing material connections [8].

## Using Testimonials

Use only genuine testimonials from real customers, with their permission. Do not edit them in ways that change their meaning. If a testimonial describes an unusual result, do not present it as typical.

:::example A Compliant Review Routine (illustrative)
Teresa adds a step to her job-completion checklist: technicians ask, "Is there anything we missed?" and fix it on the spot. That evening, an automated text from her scheduling software goes to every customer: "Thank you for choosing Brightline. If you have a minute, we would value an honest review: [link]. Reply STOP to opt out." Customers consented to job texts when booking. Over 90 days, 142 jobs produce 51 reviews (36 percent). Average rating: 4.8. Two reviews mention streaks on one window; Teresa replies, apologizes, and schedules free touch-ups. She does not offer discounts for reviews.
:::

:::case Why Platform and FTC Rules Matter
The FTC's 2024 rule was adopted after years of enforcement concerns about fake and manipulated reviews, and it allows the agency to seek civil penalties against knowing violators [7]. Google states that fake engagement and incentivized reviews are removed from Maps [6]. For a small business, the risk of shortcuts is concrete: lost reviews, damaged trust, and potential legal exposure. The honest route, which is to ask every customer and respond to every review, is also the cheapest.
:::

| Practice | Allowed? | Rule or policy |
|---|---|---|
| Asking all customers for honest reviews | Yes | Google policy [6] |
| Offering a discount for a Google review | No | Google policy [6] |
| Asking only happy customers | No | Google policy [6] |
| Posting reviews written by staff or family | No | FTC rule [7]; Google policy [6] |
| Rewarding referrals with disclosed terms | Generally yes | FTC Endorsement Guides [8] |
| Using genuine testimonials with permission | Yes | FTC Endorsement Guides [8] |

# Chapter 6: Choosing Two Channels

:::objectives
- Classify channels by audience temperature and reach.
- Evaluate channels against your buyer's behavior and your capacity.
- Select two channels for a focused test.
- Avoid spreading effort across too many platforms.
:::

A channel is any route by which buyers learn about you: search, social platforms, email, direct outreach, referral partners, community events, or advertising. Lean businesses fail more often from spreading effort too thin than from choosing the wrong channel. Two channels, worked consistently, usually outperform six worked occasionally.

## Classifying Channels

Channels can be grouped by two dimensions:

- **Warm or cold.** Warm channels reach people who already know you (past customers, your network, email subscribers). Cold channels reach strangers.
- **One-to-one or one-to-many.** One-to-one channels involve personal contact (calls, direct messages, meetings). One-to-many channels reach groups (posts, videos, ads, email newsletters).

Warm, one-to-one channels are usually the cheapest place to start, because trust already exists. One-to-many channels scale better but take longer to produce results.

## Where Buyers Are

Choose channels your buyers actually use. Pew Research Center reported in 2025 that half of U.S. adults use Instagram, with use much higher among adults under 30 [9]. For local services, buyers often begin with search and maps. For business-to-business services, professional networks and referrals may matter more. Ask your recent customers how they found you and where they look for providers.

## Evaluating Capacity

Each channel needs time. Be honest about how many hours per week you can sustain. A channel that requires daily video when you can manage one post per week is the wrong channel for now.

## The Selection

Score each candidate channel from 1 to 5 on four criteria: buyer presence, fit with your offer, your capacity to sustain it, and cost. Choose the two highest scorers. One should usually be a warm or one-to-one channel that can produce results quickly; the other can be a one-to-many channel that builds over time.

:::example Teresa's Channel Selector (illustrative)
Teresa scores six channels (buyer presence, fit, capacity, cost; 5 is best). Google Business Profile: 5, 5, 4, 5 = 19. Neighborhood platforms and local groups: 4, 4, 4, 5 = 17. Past-customer email and text: 4, 5, 5, 5 = 19. Realtor and property-manager partnerships: 3, 4, 3, 5 = 15. Short videos on Instagram: 3, 4, 2, 5 = 14. Paid search ads: 4, 4, 4, 2 = 14. Because her Google Business Profile is already part of her owned foundation, she selects past-customer email and text (warm, fast) and neighborhood groups (one-to-many, local) as her two test channels.
:::

:::case Blendtec: Matching the Channel to the Product
In 2006, Blendtec's marketing director George Wright spent about $50 on a lab coat, marbles, a rake, and other items, and filmed CEO Tom Dickson blending them, launching the "Will It Blend?" video series on YouTube, according to BYU Magazine [10]. Dickson said the videos received 6 million hits in the first week, and the magazine reported that within two years the campaign helped increase retail blender sales 700 percent [10]. The channel fit the product: a blender's power is easy to demonstrate on video. A lean business can learn from the fit, not the virality. Most videos do not go viral, and Blendtec's results are its own; the lesson is to choose a channel where your product's value is visible.
:::

| Cold, one-to-many | Content, search, ads | Slow (organic) or fast (paid) | Low to high |

# Chapter 7: Content That Answers Questions

:::objectives
- Identify the questions buyers ask before purchasing.
- Plan four useful content types.
- Produce content efficiently and reuse it across channels.
- Connect every piece of content to a next step.
:::

Content marketing works best when it helps buyers make decisions. The most reliable source of content ideas is the set of questions customers already ask. Answering them publicly, including uncomfortable questions about price and drawbacks, builds trust and attracts people who are actively searching.

## Step 1: Collect Questions

List every question customers ask before buying, in their words. Sources include phone calls, emails, quote requests, reviews, and conversations on jobs. River Pools built its content program on this principle; Marcus Sheridan's approach was to turn the questions customers asked into blog posts and videos, beginning with the cost of a fiberglass pool [2][3].

## Step 2: Plan Four Content Types

1. **Problem posts.** Explain a problem the buyer has and why it matters. ("Why gutters overflow even when they look clear.")
2. **Process posts.** Show how you do the work. ("What happens during a two-story window cleaning.")
3. **Honest proof posts.** Show real results with permission, without exaggeration. ("Before and after: a 1960s ranch with hard-water stains.")
4. **Direct offer posts.** State the offer, price guidance, and next step plainly.

A useful rhythm is three helpful posts for every direct offer post.

## Step 3: Produce Efficiently

Batch production: take photos and short videos on jobs (with customer permission), write several posts in one session, and schedule them. Reuse one piece of content across channels: a short video can become a Google Business Profile update, a social post, and part of an email.

## Step 4: Add a Next Step

Every piece should tell the reader what to do if they need help: text for a quote, book online, or reply with a question.

:::example Four-Week Content Plan (illustrative)
Teresa lists 22 questions customers have asked. She schedules two posts a week for four weeks in neighborhood groups and on her Google Business Profile. Week 1: "How much does window cleaning cost for a two-story home?" (price ranges and what affects them) and a before-and-after photo. Week 2: "How often should gutters be cleaned near oak trees?" and a 30-second process video. Week 3: "Do you clean screens and tracks?" and a customer's permitted review. Week 4: "Spring booking is open: most homes $245 to $345, text a photo for a quote." The price post generates the most comments and 6 quote requests, more than any other post.
:::

:::case River Pools: They Ask, You Answer
River Pools' content approach centered on answering, in detail, the questions buyers asked, including the cost question that many competitors avoided [3]. The New York Times described the shift from about $250,000 a year in traditional advertising to a budget of about a tenth of that, focused on informational blog posts and videos [2]. Sheridan later popularized the approach under the name "They Ask, You Answer," the title of his book published by Wiley [20]. The approach requires time and candor rather than cash, which makes it suitable for lean budgets. River Pools' recovery reflects its particular market and sustained effort over years; results are its own.
:::

# Chapter 8: Direct Outreach and Partners

:::objectives
- Plan polite, relevant one-to-one outreach.
- Build a list of 20 qualified contacts.
- Write outreach and follow-up scripts that respect recipients.
- Develop referral partnerships with complementary businesses.
:::

Direct outreach is the oldest form of marketing: contacting a specific person who may benefit from your service. It costs time rather than money and produces fast feedback. Done poorly, it is spam. Done well, it is a relevant, respectful offer of help.

## Step 1: Start Warm

Begin with people who already know you: past customers, suppliers, former colleagues, and your personal network. A short, personal message asking whether they or someone they know needs your service is often the fastest source of new work.

## Step 2: Build a List of 20 Qualified Contacts

A qualified contact has a plausible need, the authority to buy or refer, and a reason to hear from you. For a local service business, good partner categories include businesses that serve the same customers at a different moment: realtors, property managers, home inspectors, contractors in adjacent trades, and homeowner associations.

## Step 3: Write a Respectful Script

A good outreach message is short, personal, specific, and easy to decline. It states who you are, why you are contacting this person specifically, what you offer, and a low-pressure next step.

## Step 4: Follow Up Once

Many people intend to reply and forget. One polite follow-up after several days is reasonable. Repeated messages after no response are not.

## Step 5: Follow the Rules

Commercial email must comply with the CAN-SPAM Act. The FTC's compliance guide explains that commercial messages must not use false or misleading header information or deceptive subject lines, must include a valid physical postal address, must tell recipients how to opt out, and must honor opt-out requests within 10 business days [11]. Automated texts and prerecorded calls to mobile phones generally require prior consent under FCC rules [4]. Do not buy contact lists or use automated messaging tools to send unsolicited bulk messages.

## Partnerships

A referral partnership is an ongoing relationship in which two businesses that serve the same customers recommend each other. Good partnerships are reciprocal: you send work to them as well. Make it easy for partners by giving them a short description of your offer, your price guidance, and a way to refer.

:::example Twenty-Contact Outreach (illustrative)
Teresa lists 20 contacts: 8 past customers who own rental properties, 6 realtors who list homes in her service area, 4 property managers, and 2 home inspectors. She sends a personal email to each: "Hi [name], I run Brightline Window and Gutter here in [city]. I noticed you list a lot of two-story homes in [neighborhood]. We offer pre-listing window cleaning, usually $245 to $345, scheduled within five business days. Would a one-page price sheet for your sellers be useful? If not, no reply needed." Her email includes her postal address and an opt-out line. Results after one follow-up: 11 replies, 5 requests for the price sheet, 3 booked jobs within three weeks, and one property manager who schedules quarterly cleanings for 4 buildings.
:::

:::case Dollar Shave Club: A Clear Offer, Directly Delivered
Dollar Shave Club founder Michael Dubin told Inc. that he wrote the script for the company's first video, which a friend shot in one day for $4,500; the site crashed from traffic on the day it went live, and the company received 12,000 orders within 48 hours [19]. Inc. reported that the company took in more than $3.5 million in revenue in 2012 and was acquired by Unilever in 2016 for a reported $1 billion [19]. The relevance to direct outreach is the clarity of the offer: one product, one price, and an obvious reason to act. Whatever the channel, a message works better when the offer is simple. Dollar Shave Club's results reflect unusual creative skill and timing and are not typical.
:::

| Outreach element | Good practice | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Recipient | Specific person with plausible need | Purchased lists |
| Opening | Who you are and why them | Generic greetings |
| Offer | Specific service and price guidance | Vague "services" |
| Next step | Low-pressure, easy to decline | Pressure or false urgency |
| Follow-up | One polite reminder | Repeated messages |
| Compliance | Postal address, opt-out, consent for texts | Hidden identity, no opt-out |

# Chapter 9: Email on a Shoestring

:::objectives
- Explain why an email list is a valuable owned asset.
- Set up a low-cost email system.
- Write useful, compliant emails to customers and prospects.
- Use email to generate repeat business and referrals.
:::

Most small businesses already have the start of an email list: past customers. Yet many never contact them again after the job is done. A simple, useful email program turns past customers into repeat buyers and referrers, and it can be run at little or no cost.

## Why Email

An email list is owned. Unlike social media followers, your subscribers can be reached directly, and the list goes with you if a platform changes. Justin Welsh, who built a solo business selling digital products, reported reaching more than $10 million in total revenue with no paid advertising, and emphasized building an owned audience [13]. His results are his own and not typical, but the principle of owning the relationship applies to any business.

## Step 1: Choose a Tool

Many email services offer free or low-cost tiers. Mailchimp, for example, lists a free plan for up to 250 contacts [12]. Check current limits and features, which change. Choose a service that handles unsubscribes and includes your postal address automatically.

## Step 2: Build the List with Permission

Add customers who agreed to receive emails, ideally through a checkbox at booking. Invite website visitors to subscribe with a useful offer such as a seasonal maintenance checklist. Do not add purchased lists or contacts who did not opt in.

## Step 3: Send Useful Email Regularly

A monthly or seasonal email is enough for many local businesses. Each should contain one useful idea (a maintenance tip, a seasonal reminder), one piece of honest proof or news, and one clear offer or next step.

## Step 4: Follow the Law

Follow CAN-SPAM requirements: accurate sender information, non-deceptive subject lines, a postal address, a clear opt-out, and prompt honoring of opt-outs [11].

## Step 5: Use Email for Repeat Business

Set reminders based on the service cycle. If windows are typically cleaned twice a year, email customers five months after their last service with an easy rebooking link.

:::example Rebooking Email (illustrative)
Teresa imports 610 past customers who agreed to receive emails at booking (her contact count exceeds the free tier, so she pays for a low-cost plan). In March, she sends: subject "Spring window cleaning: book before April 15"; body: one tip on protecting screens, a permitted before-and-after photo, and an offer to book online with price ranges. Results: 58 percent open rate, 71 clicks, 44 bookings at an average of $290. Gross profit at 55 percent: about $7,018. Monthly email software cost: about $20. The figures are illustrative; actual response depends on list quality and timing.
:::

:::case Justin Welsh: Owned Audience, No Paid Ads
Justin Welsh wrote that his solo business passed $10 million in total revenue about five years and nine months after he began, that the revenue was 100 percent organic with no paid advertising, and that his margins were about 89 percent [13]. He built a large following on LinkedIn and an email newsletter, using the platform to meet people and email to maintain the relationship [13]. For a local service business, the scale is different, but the logic is the same: an audience you can reach directly reduces dependence on paid advertising. Welsh's results reflect years of daily writing and are not typical.
:::

| Email type | Purpose | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Welcome | Confirm subscription; set expectations | Immediately |
| Seasonal tip | Provide value; stay remembered | Monthly or seasonal |
| Rebooking reminder | Prompt repeat purchase | Based on service cycle |
| Referral request | Ask for introductions | After a positive job |
| Offer | Announce a time-limited real offer | Occasionally |

:::checklist Email Setup Check

# Chapter 10: The Channel Scoreboard

:::objectives
- Track leads and customers by source.
- Use tracking links to attribute website visits.
- Calculate cost per lead and cost per customer by channel.
- Make keep, fix, or stop decisions using evidence.
:::

What gets measured gets managed, but only if the measures are meaningful. A channel scoreboard tracks the buyer actions that matter (replies, quote requests, bookings, payments) by source, so the owner can decide which channels to keep, fix, or stop.

## Step 1: Record Source for Every Inquiry

Ask every new inquiry, "How did you hear about us?" and record the answer in the lead log. Combine this with any tracking data you have.

## Step 2: Use Tracking Links

Google Analytics supports campaign parameters (UTM parameters) added to the links you share, so you can see which campaigns refer traffic to your website [15]. Use a different tagged link for each channel, such as your Google Business Profile website button, your email, and your social posts.

## Step 3: Count the Right Things

For each channel, count weekly: contacts reached or posts published; replies or inquiries; qualified leads; quotes given; bookings; cleared payments; and money and hours spent.

## Step 4: Calculate Unit Economics

Cost per lead, close rate, and cost per customer, as in Chapter 1. Include the value of your time if a channel takes many hours.

## Step 5: Decide

At the end of a test period, classify each channel: keep (paying back), fix (promising but leaking), or stop (no evidence after a fair test). The SBA recommends comparing marketing costs to the revenue they generate and updating the plan based on what works [1].

:::example Fourteen-Day Scoreboard (illustrative)
Channel A, past-customer email and text: 610 contacted; 92 replies or clicks; 48 qualified; 44 booked; $20 spent; 3 hours. Channel B, neighborhood groups: 8 posts; 23 inquiries; 14 qualified; 6 booked; $0; 5 hours. Outreach to partners: 20 contacted; 11 replies; 5 qualified; 3 booked; $0; 4 hours. Cost per customer: Channel A $0.45 plus time; Channel B $0 plus time; outreach $0 plus time. Using a time value of $40 per hour, effective cost per customer: A = ($20 + $120) / 44 = $3.18; B = $200 / 6 = $33.33; outreach = $160 / 3 = $53.33. All three are well below the mailer's $150. Decision: keep all three, prioritize email.
:::

:::case Measuring What River Pools Measured
The River Pools example is often summarized as "content works," but the more useful lesson is about measurement. The New York Times account describes a company that replaced most of a $250,000 annual advertising budget with content and then tracked the outcome in sales and revenue, recovering to exceed its previous peak [2]. Whatever channels you choose, connect them to revenue rather than attention. Results at River Pools are its own.
:::

| Decision | Evidence | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Keep | Cost per customer well below gross profit per customer | Continue; consider modest expansion |
| Fix | Inquiries but few bookings, or bookings but slow response | Improve offer, response, or follow-up |
| Stop | No qualified leads after a fair test | Reallocate time |

# Chapter 11: Spending Your First $100

:::objectives
- Apply readiness checks before paying for advertising.
- Understand how daily budgets work on major ad platforms.
- Design a small, measurable paid test.
- Evaluate results and decide whether to continue.
:::

Advertising can accelerate a business that already converts inquiries into customers. It cannot fix a confusing offer, an unanswered phone, or a broken form. This chapter explains when to spend your first $100 and how to learn as much as possible from it.

## Six Readiness Checks

Before spending on advertising, confirm:

1. The one-sentence offer passes the 15-second test (Chapter 2).
2. The owned foundation passes its audit (Chapter 3).
3. The Google Business Profile is complete (Chapter 4).
4. At least one free channel has produced paying customers (Chapters 6 to 9).
5. The channel scoreboard is set up (Chapter 10).
6. You know your maximum acceptable cost per customer.

## How Ad Budgets Work

Ad platforms manage spending differently, and owners should understand the mechanics before starting. Google Ads explains that a campaign's actual spend on a given day may exceed its average daily budget by up to two times, which it calls overdelivery [16], and that monthly spend for most campaigns will not exceed 30.4 times the average daily budget [17]. Meta advises that minimum budgets vary by country and objective, and that if you use a cost-per-result goal, your daily budget should be at least five times that goal [18]. Small budgets can therefore produce limited data, and results from a $100 test should be treated as directional.

## Designing the Test

- **One offer, one audience, one channel.** For local services, search ads targeting people actively looking for your service are often the most direct test.
- **A clear destination.** Send traffic to a page that matches the ad and makes the next step obvious.
- **A fixed cap.** Set a total cap you can afford to lose.
- **Tracking.** Use tagged links and ask every inquiry about the source.
- **A decision rule.** Decide in advance what result would justify continuing.

## Honest Ads

Advertising claims must be truthful and substantiated, and endorsements in ads must reflect genuine experiences [8]. Avoid promises such as "guaranteed lowest price" unless you can honor them.

:::example A $100 Search Test (illustrative)
Teresa passes all six readiness checks. Her maximum acceptable cost per customer is $90. She runs a Google search campaign for "gutter cleaning near me" in her service area with a $10 average daily budget for 10 days, capped at $100 total. Results: 1,240 impressions; 38 clicks; average cost per click $2.63; 7 calls or form inquiries; 5 qualified; 2 booked. Cost per customer: $100 / 2 = $50. That is below her $90 limit, but two customers is a tiny sample. She decides to repeat the test for another 20 days at the same budget before increasing spend.
:::

:::case Dollar Shave Club: Creative Before Budget
Dollar Shave Club's launch video cost $4,500 to produce, according to founder Michael Dubin [19]. The company's early growth came from a clear offer and memorable creative that people shared, not from an initial large media budget [19]. For a small business, the transferable lesson is that the quality and clarity of the message determine how much each advertising dollar achieves. Spending more on a weak message only buys more exposure for the weakness. The company's outcome reflects exceptional execution and is not typical.
:::

| Platform mechanic | What it means | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Google Ads overdelivery | Daily spend may reach up to 2 times the average daily budget | Google Ads Help [16] |
| Google Ads monthly limit | Most campaigns will not exceed 30.4 times the average daily budget per month | Google Ads Help [17] |
| Meta minimum budgets | Vary by country and objective | Meta Business Help [18] |
| Meta cost-per-result goal | Daily budget at least 5 times the goal | Meta Business Help [18] |

:::checklist First $100 Readiness Gate
- Offer passes the 15-second test.
- Foundation audit passed.
- Google Business Profile complete.
- A free channel has produced paying customers.
- Scoreboard ready with tracking links.
- Maximum acceptable cost per customer calculated.
- Ad destination page matches the ad.
- Total spend cap set.
- Decision rule written before launch.
:::

:::summary
Spend on advertising only after the offer, foundation, profile, and at least one free channel are working. Understand platform budget mechanics: Google may overdeliver up to twice the daily budget, and Meta recommends minimum budgets that vary by objective. Run small, capped, tracked tests with a predefined decision rule, treat small samples as directional, and keep ads truthful.
:::

# Chapter 12: The 14-Day Lean Sprint

:::objectives
- Combine the book's methods into a two-week sprint.
- Assign daily tasks for setup, outreach, content, and measurement.
- Review results and plan the next 90 days.
:::

The 14-day sprint compresses the book into a practical schedule. It is designed for an owner with about one hour per day. The goal is not to transform a business in two weeks; it is to fix leaks, test two channels, and produce evidence for the next decisions.

## Days 1 to 7: Setup and First Batch

- **Day 1.** Calculate acquisition numbers (Chapter 1). Choose the lead offer and write the one-sentence offer (Chapter 2).
- **Day 2.** Run the 15-second clarity test; revise.
- **Day 3.** Audit the owned foundation and fix leaks (Chapter 3).
- **Day 4.** Complete the Google Business Profile (Chapter 4).
- **Day 5.** Set up the review routine; request reviews from recent customers (Chapter 5).
- **Day 6.** Score channels and select two (Chapter 6). Build the list of 20 contacts (Chapter 8).
- **Day 7.** Send outreach batch 1 (10 contacts). Publish the first content post (Chapter 7).

## Days 8 to 14: Second Batch and Review

- **Day 8.** Send an email to past customers (Chapter 9).
- **Day 9.** Send outreach batch 2 (10 contacts). Publish the second post.
- **Day 10.** Follow up on batch 1. Reply to all reviews and inquiries.
- **Day 11.** Publish the third post. Tighten wording based on replies.
- **Day 12.** Follow up on batch 2.
- **Day 13.** Publish the direct offer post.
- **Day 14.** Complete the scoreboard. Make keep, fix, or stop decisions. Decide whether you pass the first-$100 gate (Chapters 10 and 11).

## After the Sprint: The Next 90 Days

Repeat the routines that worked weekly. Each month, review the scoreboard, add one improvement, and consider one new test. Revisit your offer each quarter.

:::example Teresa's Sprint Results (illustrative)
Over 14 days, Teresa fixes four foundation leaks, rebuilds her profile, collects 19 new reviews, sends 20 outreach messages and one customer email, and publishes four posts. Results: 136 replies or inquiries, 67 qualified leads, 53 bookings, and $15,370 in booked revenue at an average of $290. Her time: about 16 hours. Cash spent: $20. Much of the result came from past customers who had never been asked to rebook, a finding that changes her priorities for the next quarter. These numbers are illustrative; your results will differ.
:::

| Days | Focus | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Numbers and offer | Acquisition sheet; tested one-sentence offer |
| 3–5 | Foundation, profile, reviews | Audit passed; profile complete; review routine live |
| 6–7 | Channels and outreach | Two channels; 20 contacts; batch 1; first post |
| 8–13 | Execution | Email; batch 2; follow-ups; posts |
| 14 | Review | Scoreboard; decisions; ad-readiness verdict |

The 14-day sprint applies the book in daily steps: numbers and offer, foundation, profile, reviews, channels, outreach, content, email, and measurement. # Appendix I: Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need a website if I have a Google Business Profile?** A profile is valuable, but a simple website gives you an owned page for your offer, prices, and proof, and a destination for email and ads.

**How long before content produces results?** Content that answers questions can take months to build search traffic. That is why this book pairs it with a faster warm or one-to-one channel.

**Should I hire an agency?** Consider it after you have a proven offer, a working foundation, and evidence about which channels convert. Ask for clear reporting in terms of inquiries and customers, not only impressions.

**Where can I get free help?** SCORE, a nonprofit resource partner of the SBA, offers free mentoring and education through more than 10,000 volunteers [14].

**What if nothing works in 14 days?** Review the scoreboard for where buyers drop off. If few people respond, revisit the offer and audience. If people respond but do not book, look at price, response speed, and follow-up.

# Glossary

**Attribution.** Identifying which channel or campaign produced an inquiry or sale.

**CAN-SPAM Act.** U.S. law setting requirements for commercial email.

**Channel.** A route by which buyers learn about a business.

**Close rate.** The share of qualified leads that become customers.

**Cost per customer (CAC).** Marketing spend divided by new customers acquired.

**Cost per lead (CPL).** Marketing spend divided by qualified leads.

**Endorsement Guides.** FTC guidance requiring endorsements to be honest and material connections to be disclosed.

**Google Business Profile.** A free Google listing that can appear in local Search and Maps results.

**Gross margin.** Revenue minus direct costs, as a percentage of revenue.

**Lead log.** A record of every inquiry, its source, and its outcome.

**Lean marketing.** Evidence-first customer acquisition that fixes leaks before spending.

**Overdelivery.** Google Ads spending above the average daily budget on some days, up to a limit.

**Owned asset.** A marketing asset the business controls, such as a website or email list.

**Prominence.** In Google local ranking, how well known a business is.

**Qualified lead.** An inquiry with a plausible need, ability to buy, and fit with the offer.

**Referral partnership.** A reciprocal arrangement between businesses serving the same customers.

**Relevance.** In Google local ranking, how well a profile matches a search.

**UTM parameters.** Tags added to links so analytics tools can identify campaign sources.

**Warm channel.** A channel reaching people who already know the business.
