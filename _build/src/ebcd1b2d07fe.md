# How to Use This Book

This book is for small-business owners, solo marketers, and team members who have been told they "need to be on TikTok" and want to approach the platform with the same discipline they would apply to any other investment of time and money. It assumes no experience with short-form video. It does assume you will practice: the craft of making a clear, watchable sixty-second video is learned by making many of them.

The chapters move from context to execution. Chapters 1 and 2 explain who uses the platform and how its recommendation system works, based on public research and TikTok's own published explanations. Chapters 3 and 4 cover strategy and listening. Chapters 5 through 8 teach the craft: structure, production, voice, and community. Chapters 9 and 10 cover creator partnerships and paid promotion. Chapter 11 covers measurement, and Chapter 12 addresses risk and provides a ninety-day plan.

Each chapter contains learning objectives, plain-language definitions, a framework, a worked example labelled "Illustrative" with numbers you can check, a real case where one is relevant, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Case studies describe what companies have publicly reported. Their results are their own, not TTI's, and none is a prediction of what your account will do. Viral moments, in particular, are rare and cannot be engineered on demand.

## What you will be able to do

- Decide, with evidence, whether TikTok fits your customers and goals.
- Explain in plain terms how TikTok's recommendation system chooses what to show.
- Write a content strategy with pillars, cadence, and measurable goals.
- Script, film, and edit short videos with strong openings and clear endings.
- Work with creators lawfully, with proper disclosure.
- Measure what matters and connect video activity to business results.

:::callout A note on change
TikTok changes features often, and in January 2026 its U.S. operations were reorganized under a new joint venture. Specific buttons, menus, and ad formats described here may move or be renamed. The principles (audience fit, clear storytelling, honest disclosure, careful measurement) do not change with the interface.
:::

# Chapter 1: The Platform in Context

:::objectives
- Describe who uses TikTok in the United States, using survey data.
- Explain why most users watch rather than post, and what that means for businesses.
- Summarize the 2026 ownership change and its practical implications.
- Decide whether TikTok fits your segment.
:::

Before investing hours a week in any channel, a business should ask whether its customers are there and in what frame of mind. This chapter assembles the best available public evidence.

## Who uses TikTok

Pew Research Center surveyed 5,022 U.S. adults in early 2025 and found that 37 percent say they use TikTok, up from 21 percent in 2021 [1]. In a separate 2025 survey, about a quarter of U.S. adults (24 percent) said they use TikTok daily [1]. Use is strongly related to age: Pew reports that 47 percent of adults aged 18 to 29 use TikTok daily, compared with 5 percent of those 65 and older [1]. TikTok still trails YouTube and Facebook in overall reach, but its share has grown steadily.

| Group (U.S. adults, 2025) | Daily TikTok use |
|---|---|
| All adults | 24% |
| Ages 18–29 | 47% |
| Ages 30–49 | 28% |
| Ages 50–64 | 17% |
| Ages 65+ | 5% |

Source: Pew Research Center, Americans' Social Media Use 2025 [1].

## Why people are there

In a March 2024 survey, Pew found that 62 percent of U.S. adult TikTok users say looking at product reviews or recommendations is a reason they use the site, compared with 44 percent of Instagram users and 37 percent of Facebook users who say the same of those platforms [2]. The share was higher among younger users (74 percent of those aged 18 to 29) and among women (68 percent, versus 52 percent of men) [2]. Entertainment, however, remains the main draw [3]. The implication for businesses is important: people come to be entertained and are open to discovering products, but they do not come to watch advertisements.

## Watchers and posters

A 2024 Pew study that matched survey responses with observed account behavior found that the top 25 percent of U.S. adult TikTok users by posting volume produced 98 percent of publicly accessible videos from that group, and that about half of adult users had never posted a video [4]. For a business, this means two things. First, the audience is mostly viewers, so the bar is the quality of what they watch, not the quality of what their friends post. Second, consistent posters are a small minority, so simply showing up regularly with useful content is already a differentiator.

## The 2026 ownership change

After years of legal uncertainty, ByteDance closed a deal in January 2026 creating a majority American-owned joint venture to run TikTok's U.S. operations, with Oracle, Silver Lake, and MGX as managing investors and ByteDance retaining 19.9 percent [5]. CNN reported that the joint venture would handle U.S. data protection and content moderation, while advertising, e-commerce, and marketing functions would continue to be managed by the global TikTok entity [5]. For a small business, the practical lessons are modest but real: users can keep using the same app, terms of service may change, and any single platform can face sudden policy shifts. Chapter 12 returns to the principle of owning your audience.

:::example Illustrative: does TikTok fit?
A bakery's best customers are, by its sales records, about 60 percent aged 25 to 44, mostly local, buying for celebrations. A tax-preparation firm's best clients are mostly aged 50 to 70 and small-business owners.

Using the daily-use figures above, the bakery's core segment overlaps heavily with daily TikTok users, and baked goods are highly visual. The tax firm's core segment uses TikTok less often (17 percent of 50–64 and 5 percent of 65+ daily). The bakery has a strong reason to test TikTok as a primary channel. The tax firm might test it only for a secondary segment (younger freelancers) or choose YouTube or email instead. Neither conclusion is final; both are testable hypotheses.
:::

:::checklist Platform fit
- I know the age range and buying context of my best customers.
- I have compared that profile with current usage data.
- My product or service can be shown, demonstrated, or explained visually.
- I have a reason to believe my segment uses TikTok for discovery in my category.
- I am prepared to treat TikTok as a test, not a commitment, for the first ninety days.
:::

:::summary
Survey data show TikTok used by 37 percent of U.S. adults in 2025, with daily use concentrated among younger adults. Many users seek product recommendations, but entertainment is the main draw. Most users watch rather than post. A January 2026 joint venture reorganized U.S. operations. Fit depends on your segment's age, habits, and how visual your offering is.

# Chapter 2: How Distribution Works

:::objectives
- Explain the For You feed and why follower count matters less than on other platforms.
- List the signal categories TikTok says its recommendation system uses.
- Define retention, completion rate, and engagement rate.
- Apply this understanding to realistic expectations for a new account.
:::

On many social platforms, what you see is largely determined by whom you follow. TikTok's main feed, called **For You**, works differently. It recommends videos from any account, based on predictions about what each viewer will find interesting. This design is why a new account with no followers can sometimes reach many people, and also why reach from one video to the next is so variable.

## What TikTok says about recommendations

In a public explanation of how For You recommendations work, TikTok described three broad groups of signals [6]:

1. **User interactions**: videos a person likes, shares, comments on, watches in full, or skips; accounts they follow; content they create.
2. **Video information**: details such as captions, sounds, and hashtags.
3. **Device and account settings**: language preference, country, and device type, which TikTok said receive lower weight than the others.

TikTok has said that a strong indicator of interest is whether a user finishes watching a longer video, and that follower count and previous high-performing videos are not themselves direct factors in recommendation [6]. The company does not publish the precise weights, and they likely change. Treat any outside claim about "the algorithm's secret" with skepticism.

## Key definitions

- **View**: a counted play of a video. Definitions vary by platform and can include very short plays.
- **Average watch time**: the mean number of seconds viewers watched.
- **Retention curve**: a graph showing what percentage of viewers are still watching at each second.
- **Completion rate**: the share of viewers who watched to the end.
- **Engagement rate**: interactions (likes, comments, shares, saves) divided by views. Calculate it consistently; different tools define it differently.

## Practical implications

Because the system observes how real viewers respond, the most reliable path to distribution is to make videos that the intended audience chooses to watch to the end and interact with. That sounds obvious, but it rules out several tempting shortcuts: clickbait openings that do not deliver (viewers leave early), irrelevant trending sounds (they attract the wrong viewers), and buying followers (prohibited by the FTC's 2024 rule on fake social-media indicators, as discussed in Chapter 9).

:::example Illustrative: reading two retention curves
A plumbing company posts two 40-second videos.

Video A opens with the company logo and a greeting. At 3 seconds, 45 percent of viewers remain; at 40 seconds, 8 percent. Average watch time: 7 seconds.

Video B opens with a close-up of water spraying from a pipe under a sink and the words "This $4 part prevents this." At 3 seconds, 72 percent remain; at 40 seconds, 31 percent. Average watch time: 18 seconds.

Video B held viewers about 2.5 times longer on average (18 ÷ 7 ≈ 2.6). Its completion rate was nearly four times higher (31 ÷ 8 ≈ 3.9). If TikTok's stated emphasis on completion holds, B is far more likely to be shown to additional viewers. The difference came almost entirely from the first three seconds.
:::

## Realistic expectations

New accounts often see erratic results: a few hundred views on most videos and an occasional outlier. That variability is a feature of interest-based distribution, not proof of failure. Judge progress over batches of ten to twenty videos, not single posts.

| Belief | Reality |
|---|---|
| "You need followers first." | For You recommends from any account; followers help but are not the gate. |
| "Post at the magic hour." | Timing may matter somewhat; content quality and fit matter more. |
| "Use thirty hashtags." | Hashtags are one piece of video information; relevance beats quantity. |
| "One viral video changes everything." | Spikes often bring viewers who do not fit your segment; consistency compounds. |

:::checklist Distribution literacy
- I can explain the three signal categories TikTok has described.
- I know how to find average watch time and retention for my videos.
- I judge results across batches, not single videos.
- I avoid shortcuts that attract the wrong viewers or break rules.
:::

:::summary
TikTok's For You feed recommends videos based on predicted interest, using user interactions, video information, and, with lower weight, device and account settings. Completion is a strong signal. Strong openings and genuine relevance to the intended audience are the most dependable levers. Expect variability and evaluate in batches.
:::

# Chapter 3: Strategy Before Content

:::objectives
- Set a business goal and connect it to measurable TikTok outcomes.
- Define content pillars that serve both audience and business.
- Choose a sustainable posting cadence.
- Write a one-page TikTok strategy.
:::

Many businesses start TikTok by asking "What should I post?" The better first question is "What should TikTok do for the business?" Content follows strategy, not the reverse.

## From business goal to platform goal

Business goals are things like more bookings, more online orders, more applicants, or more foot traffic. Each implies a different TikTok role:

| Business goal | TikTok role | Leading indicator | Lagging indicator |
|---|---|---|---|
| More local foot traffic | Awareness in a geographic area | Views from local audience; profile visits | "Saw you on TikTok" mentions; promo-code use |
| More online orders | Discovery and consideration | Link clicks; saves | Orders attributed to TikTok |
| More consultations | Trust and expertise | Comments asking questions; follows | Bookings citing TikTok |
| More job applicants | Employer brand | Views of workplace videos | Applications citing TikTok |

## Content pillars

A **content pillar** is a recurring theme that your audience values and that connects to your business. Three to five pillars give structure without monotony. Good pillars sit at the intersection of three circles: what your segment cares about, what you know or can show better than most, and what leads toward your offer.

Common pillar types for small businesses:

- **Education**: how-to, mistakes to avoid, explainers.
- **Process**: behind the scenes, how the product is made or the service delivered.
- **Proof**: transformations, before-and-after (with permission), customer stories.
- **Personality**: the people, values, and humor of the business.
- **Response**: answering comments and common questions.

## Cadence

Consistency matters more than volume. A cadence you can sustain for ninety days beats an ambitious schedule abandoned in week three. For most small teams, three videos a week is a realistic starting point; some can manage daily posting by batching.

:::example Illustrative: strategy for a dog-grooming salon
Business goal: fill weekday appointments, currently at 60 percent capacity, to 80 percent within six months.

TikTok role: local awareness and trust.

Pillars: (1) Transformations: before-and-after grooms, with owner permission. (2) Education: coat care tips for common breeds. (3) Process: what happens during a groom, to reduce anxiety for first-time owners. (4) Response: answering comment questions.

Cadence: three videos a week, filmed in one 90-minute session each Monday.

Measurement: weekly views from the metro area, profile visits, link clicks to the booking page, and new bookings where the customer reports TikTok as the source. Target: 10 new TikTok-sourced bookings a month by month four.

Capacity check: 40 weekday slots a week at 60 percent means 16 empty slots a week, about 64 a month. Ten new TikTok-sourced bookings would fill about 16 percent of the gap, a meaningful but not complete answer. Other channels must also contribute.
:::

Pillar 2: ___
Pillar 3: ___
Pillar 4 (optional): ___

Start with a business goal, then define TikTok's role and the leading and lagging indicators that will show progress. Three to five content pillars give structure, sitting where audience interest, your expertise, and your offer overlap. A sustainable cadence, often three videos a week, matters more than volume.

# Chapter 4: Listening Before Speaking

:::objectives
- Define social listening and explain its role in content planning.
- Build a simple weekly listening routine.
- Evaluate trends for fit before joining them.
- Turn audience questions into a content backlog.
:::

**Social listening** means deliberately observing what your audience watches, says, and asks, so that your content responds to real interest rather than guesses. On a platform where culture moves quickly, listening is not optional. It is how you learn the formats, references, and questions that matter to your segment.

:::case Duolingo's listening habit
Duolingo, the language-learning app, became one of the most discussed brand accounts on TikTok through irreverent videos built around its green owl mascot, Duo. TikTok's own case study on Duolingo describes how its social team relied on staying close to platform culture and treating the mascot as a recurring character [7]. Industry publication Contagious, interviewing the team, reported that social media manager Zaria Parvez spent one to two hours a day on social listening, scrolling TikTok to understand trends and audience humor before creating content [8]. According to TikTok's case study, a later campaign in Germany reached about 26.9 million people with an engagement rate of about 12.6 percent [7]. Duolingo's results are its own, not TTI's. The transferable lesson is not "be zany" but "invest time in understanding the culture before you post into it."
:::

## A weekly listening routine

A small business cannot devote two hours a day, but thirty minutes, three times a week, is enough to stay informed:

1. **Search your category.** Use the search bar for terms your customers use ("how to clean grout," "best birthday cake near me"). Note which videos have high engagement and why.
2. **Read comments.** Comments on popular videos in your niche reveal questions, objections, and language.
3. **Watch competitors and adjacent businesses.** Notice formats that work, not to copy, but to understand expectations.
4. **Note sounds and formats.** Which recurring formats (for example, "three mistakes," "day in the life," "reply to comment") appear in your niche?
5. **Record ideas.** Keep a running list in a shared document or notes app.

## Evaluating trends

A **trend** is a format, sound, or topic that many accounts are using at once. Trends can extend reach, but joining an irrelevant trend attracts viewers who will never become customers. Before joining, ask:

| Question | Join if… | Skip if… |
|---|---|---|
| Does it relate to my audience's interests? | Natural connection | Forced connection |
| Can I add something genuine? | My expertise or product adds a twist | I would simply imitate |
| Is it still rising? | Recently emerging | Already saturated or mocked |
| Is it appropriate for my brand? | Fits tone and values | Risky, offensive, or misunderstood |
| Is the sound licensed for business use? | Available in the business music library | Unclear or unavailable |

## From questions to backlog

The most reliable content source for most small businesses is the questions customers already ask. Every question heard in person, by phone, by email, or in comments is a potential video. A **content backlog** is a running list of these ideas, sorted by pillar.

:::example Illustrative: a month of listening for a bike shop
Over four weeks, a bike-repair shop logs 31 questions from customers and TikTok comments. Grouped:

| Theme | Count | Example question |
|---|---|---|
| Flat tires | 9 | "Why do I keep getting flats?" |
| E-bike batteries | 7 | "How long should my battery last?" |
| Bike fit | 6 | "Why do my knees hurt after riding?" |
| Prices | 5 | "Is a tune-up worth it?" |
| Storage | 4 | "Can I leave my bike outside in winter?" |

At three videos a week, these 31 questions provide over ten weeks of content (31 ÷ 3 ≈ 10.3), each grounded in demonstrated interest. Flat tires and e-bike batteries account for 16 of 31 questions (52 percent), suggesting those become the first two series.
:::

# Chapter 5: Anatomy of a Short Video

:::objectives
- Describe the hook, body, and payoff structure of a short video.
- Write openings that earn the next few seconds honestly.
- Script a 30–60 second video using a template.
- Choose an appropriate length for the content.
:::

A short video is a compressed story. In under a minute it must earn attention, deliver value, and end in a way that satisfies the viewer. The structure is simple; executing it well takes practice.

## Hook, body, payoff

**Hook (first 1–3 seconds).** The opening must give the viewer a reason to keep watching. Effective hooks create a question, show a striking image, or name a problem the viewer has. The hook must be honest: if it promises something the video does not deliver, viewers leave and trust erodes.

**Body (the middle).** Deliver what the hook promised, as efficiently as possible. Cut every second that does not move the viewer forward. Visual change (a new angle, a close-up, a caption) every few seconds helps maintain attention.

**Payoff (the end).** Resolve the question, show the result, or give the key takeaway. Optionally add a call to action: follow for part two, visit the link, comment a question.

## Hook patterns

| Pattern | Example | Works best for |
|---|---|---|
| Problem statement | "If your faucet drips like this, here's why." | Education |
| Result first | Show the finished cake, then rewind to the start. | Process, transformation |
| Mistake | "Three mistakes people make painting a room." | Education |
| Question | "Is a $90 tune-up actually worth it?" | Consideration |
| Unexpected visual | A close-up of an unusual tool or ingredient | Curiosity |
| Reply | "Replying to @user who asked…" | Community, response |

## Scripting

Even casual-looking videos benefit from a short script. A script does not need to be read word for word; it ensures the video has a point and ends on time.

:::example Illustrative: a 40-second script for an HVAC company
Topic: why furnace filters matter (Education pillar).

| Time | Visual | Words (spoken or on screen) |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 s | Close-up: technician pulls out a gray, clogged filter | "This is what your furnace has been breathing through." |
| 3–10 s | Side-by-side: dirty filter vs. new filter | "A clogged filter makes your system work harder." |
| 10–25 s | Technician points to the filter slot, shows the size label | "Find the size printed on the edge. Most homes need a new one every one to three months; check your manufacturer's guidance." |
| 25–35 s | Slides in new filter; arrow on frame | "Arrow points toward the furnace. That's it." |
| 35–40 s | Technician to camera | "Set a phone reminder today. Questions? Drop them below." |

Word count: about 75 words. At a natural pace of roughly 150 words per minute, that fills about 30 seconds of speech, leaving room for visual pauses. Note that the script avoids specific energy-savings claims the company cannot substantiate.
:::

## Length

There is no universally best length. The right length is the shortest that delivers the value. A quick tip may need 15 seconds; a process walkthrough may need 90. Watch your retention curves (Chapter 2): if viewers leave halfway through longer videos consistently, tighten them.

:::checklist Video structure
- The first three seconds show or say why the viewer should keep watching.
- The hook is honest; the video delivers what it promises.
- Every second in the body moves the viewer forward.
- Visuals change every few seconds.
- The payoff resolves the hook clearly.
- Claims made are ones I can substantiate.
:::

:::summary
Short videos follow a hook-body-payoff structure. Honest hooks name a problem, show a result, or pose a question. The body delivers efficiently with frequent visual change, and the payoff resolves the promise. Short scripts keep videos focused, and length should be the minimum needed to deliver value.
:::

# Chapter 6: Production on a Small-Business Budget

:::objectives
- Assemble a minimal, effective production kit.
- Apply basic principles of lighting, framing, and sound.
- Edit efficiently with captions and accessibility in mind.
- Batch production to sustain cadence.
:::

Polished production is not required on TikTok, and highly produced videos can sometimes feel like advertisements and underperform. But viewers do leave videos they cannot see or hear clearly. The goal is clarity, not gloss.

## The minimal kit

| Item | Purpose | Approximate cost range |
|---|---|---|
| Recent smartphone | Camera and editing | Already owned |
| Small tripod or phone clamp | Steady shots, hands-free | Low |
| Clip-on or wireless microphone | Clear voice | Low to moderate |
| Window light or one LED panel | Even lighting on faces and products | Low |
| Editing app (built-in or third-party) | Cuts, captions, text | Free to low |

Sound quality usually matters more than image quality. Viewers tolerate slightly soft video; they leave when they cannot understand what is said.

## Lighting, framing, and sound

- **Light**: face a window or place a soft light in front of the subject, slightly to the side. Avoid strong light behind the subject.
- **Framing**: film vertically. Keep important elements in the center of the frame, away from the edges where on-screen buttons and captions appear.
- **Sound**: film in a quiet space, use a microphone close to the speaker, and avoid echoing rooms.

## Editing

- Cut pauses and filler words.
- Add on-screen text for key points; many viewers watch without sound.
- Add captions. Accurate captions make videos accessible to deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers and help everyone watching on mute. Built-in auto-captions are convenient but should be checked for errors, especially names and technical terms.
- Use licensed music only. TikTok provides a music library cleared for commercial use by business accounts; using other popular songs in promotional content can raise copyright problems.

## Batching

**Batching** means producing several videos in one session. It reduces setup time and makes a consistent cadence feasible.

:::example Illustrative: the time math of batching
A boutique owner films each video separately: 15 minutes setup, 10 minutes filming, 20 minutes editing, 5 minutes posting = 50 minutes per video, or 150 minutes for three videos a week.

Batched: 15 minutes setup once, 30 minutes filming three videos, 50 minutes editing (some templates reused), 15 minutes scheduling = 110 minutes for three videos. Savings: 40 minutes a week, about 35 hours a year (40 × 52 ÷ 60 ≈ 34.7). Over a year, that is nearly a full working week returned to the business.
:::

## Showing people and places

If employees or customers appear in videos, get their permission, preferably in writing. Do not film customers' faces, homes, vehicles, or documents without consent. Blur or avoid addresses, license plates, and screens with private information.

:::checklist Production
- Audio is clear; I use a microphone when speaking.
- The subject is lit from the front.
- Video is vertical with key content centered.
- Captions are added and checked for accuracy.
- Music is from the commercial library or otherwise licensed.
- Everyone who appears has consented; private information is not visible.
- I batch-film to sustain cadence.
:::

:::summary
Clarity matters more than polish. A phone, a stabilizer, a microphone, and good light are enough. Sound quality is often the biggest differentiator. Edit tightly, add accurate captions for accessibility, use licensed music, and get consent from anyone who appears. Batching saves substantial time over a year.
:::

# Chapter 7: Voice, Character, and Responsiveness

:::objectives
- Define brand voice and describe it in practical terms.
- Decide whether a recurring character or on-camera person suits the business.
- Respond to unexpected moments quickly and authentically.
- Write a short voice guide that others can follow.
:::

On TikTok, people follow people more readily than they follow logos. A recognizable voice, and often a recognizable face, gives viewers a reason to return. Voice is not about being funny; it is about being consistent and human.

## Brand voice

**Brand voice** is the personality expressed in how a business communicates: word choice, humor, formality, and values. Describe it with three or four adjective pairs, each clarified by what it is not:

- Warm, not syrupy.
- Knowledgeable, not condescending.
- Playful, not mocking.
- Direct, not blunt.

A voice guide with examples ("we say X, we don't say Y") helps anyone on the team post consistently.

## Faces and characters

Many effective small-business accounts center on a person: the owner, a technician, a baker. That person becomes the trusted guide. Other businesses use a recurring character or mascot (as Duolingo did with Duo; see Chapter 4). Choose what is sustainable: if the owner is uncomfortable on camera, a team member, hands-only demonstrations, or voice-over can work.

| Approach | Strengths | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Owner on camera | Authentic, builds personal trust | Dependence on one person |
| Team members rotate | Shows culture, spreads workload | Inconsistent voice without a guide |
| Hands-only or product-focused | Comfortable for camera-shy owners | Less personal connection |
| Mascot or character | Distinctive, memorable | Requires creative skill; can feel forced |

## Responsiveness

Sometimes a business is mentioned in a video it did not create, positively or negatively. How quickly and genuinely it responds can matter more than anything it planned.

:::case Ocean Spray and the skateboarding video
In late September 2020, Nathan Apodaca, a warehouse worker in Idaho, posted a short video of himself longboarding to work while drinking Ocean Spray cranberry juice and lip-syncing to Fleetwood Mac's "Dreams." The video spread widely. The Associated Press reported that the 22-second clip passed 33 million views within weeks, that thousands of people posted their own versions, including Fleetwood Mac co-founder Mick Fleetwood, and that streams of the 1977 song soared [9]. AP also reported that Ocean Spray "latched on to the moment" and gave Apodaca a new truck filled with cranberry juice [9]; the *Los Angeles Times* covered the same gift [10]. Ocean Spray did not create the moment; it recognized it and responded generously and quickly, in a way that matched the spirit of the original. Ocean Spray's outcomes are its own, not TTI's, and such moments cannot be planned.
:::

For small businesses, the scale is different but the principle holds. When a customer posts about you, thank them publicly (if they are comfortable), share or stitch their video with permission, and respond in your own voice. When criticism appears, respond calmly, acknowledge the issue, and move detailed resolution to a private channel.

:::example Illustrative: responding to a customer post
A local taco truck is tagged in a video by a customer praising its birria, viewed 18,000 times. Within two hours, the owner comments a thank-you and asks permission to stitch the video. With permission, the owner posts a stitch showing the birria being made, captioned "For everyone who asked where this was."

Over the next week, the truck sees the stitch reach 12,000 views and records 23 customers mentioning TikTok at the window, against a typical 3 per week, an increase of 20 (illustrative figures). At an average ticket of $16, that is about $320 of additional revenue in a week from roughly 30 minutes of work. The owner logs the mentions, not just the views.
:::

| Misunderstanding | Clarify politely with facts |
| Hostile or abusive comments | Do not engage; use moderation tools to hide or report |
| Possible legal issue (injury, discrimination) | Do not debate publicly; consult appropriate advisers |

# Chapter 8: Community and Customer-Created Content

:::objectives
- Distinguish audience from community.
- Use comments, replies, stitches, and duets to build two-way relationships.
- Encourage customer-created content ethically.
- Prepare operations for sudden demand.
:::

An **audience** watches. A **community** participates: comments, asks, shares, and creates. Community is slower to build than audience but far more durable, and on TikTok, community activity also sends the interaction signals that support distribution.

## Tools for two-way conversation

- **Comment replies**: respond to questions and thoughtful comments, especially in the first hours after posting.
- **Video replies**: answer a comment with a new video; the original comment appears on screen. This turns questions into content (Chapter 4).
- **Stitch**: include a clip of another person's video at the start of yours, then respond.
- **Duet**: place your video side-by-side with another.
- **LIVE**: real-time video with live comments, useful for Q&A, launches, or demonstrations, subject to account eligibility.

## Customer-created content

**Customer-created content**, often called user-generated content (UGC), is content made by customers about your product. It is persuasive because it comes from peers. It can be encouraged, but must not be faked or bought without disclosure.

:::case Little Moons and the "big Tesco" trend
In early 2021, during a U.K. lockdown, TikTok users began posting videos of trips to large Tesco supermarkets to find Little Moons mochi ice cream. The *Grocery Gazette* reported that Tesco saw sales of Little Moons rise by 700 percent in a week [11]. TikTok's own case study describes how the brand then engaged with the trend and built on it with its own content and paid activity [12]. Inside Retail later reported that Little Moons was already growing about 50 percent a year before the trend, meaning distribution and product were in place when attention arrived [13]. The results are Little Moons's own, not TTI's. Two lessons stand out: the content that drove demand was made by customers, not the brand; and the brand could benefit because the product was already available in stores where viewers could find it.
:::

## Encouraging customer content ethically

1. **Make the product or experience worth filming.** Packaging, presentation, a memorable moment.
2. **Invite it plainly.** A card in the box or a sign at the counter: "Share your [product] and tag us."
3. **Celebrate it.** Comment, thank, and (with permission) feature it.
4. **Never fake it.** Posting staged "customer" videos as if they were spontaneous is deceptive. If you pay or give free products in exchange for posts, the poster must disclose that connection (Chapter 9).

## Preparing for demand

Attention without fulfillment wastes goodwill. When Dollar Shave Club released its 2012 launch video, *The New York Times* reported that 12,000 orders arrived in the first 48 hours and the company's servers could not keep up [18]. Before a campaign, ask what happens if demand doubles overnight.

:::example Illustrative: a demand stress test for a candle maker
Current capacity: 150 candles a week. Current sales: 110 a week. Inventory: 200 candles.

Scenario: a customer's video brings 600 orders in three days.

- Inventory covers 200 orders immediately.
- Remaining 400 orders at 150 a week (with current sales also continuing at 110) means about 400 ÷ (150 − 110) = 10 weeks to clear the backlog if regular sales continue, or about 400 ÷ 150 ≈ 2.7 weeks if all production is redirected.

Pre-decided responses: (1) post an honest shipping-time notice on the site and in a pinned video; (2) enable preorders with clear dates; (3) line up a temporary helper to raise output to 250 a week, which would clear the backlog in about 400 ÷ (250 − 110) ≈ 2.9 weeks while maintaining regular orders. The owner writes these into a one-page plan before it is needed.
:::

| Community activity | Effort | Typical benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Replying to comments | Low | Goodwill, interaction signals, content ideas |
| Video replies | Moderate | Turns questions into content |
| Stitching customer videos | Moderate | Social proof, gratitude |
| LIVE Q&A | Higher | Direct relationship, real-time objections handled |
| UGC invitations | Low | Peer proof over time |

:::checklist Community readiness
- I reply to comments, especially in the first hours after posting.
- I turn good questions into video replies.
- I invite customers to share and celebrate those who do.
- I never stage fake customer content.
- I have a one-page plan for sudden demand.
:::

:::summary
Community, not just audience, is the durable asset. Comment replies, video replies, stitches, duets, and LIVE sessions create conversation. The Little Moons case shows customer-created content driving demand for a product that was already available. Encourage customer content ethically, never fake it, and plan operations for sudden demand.
:::

# Chapter 9: Creators, Partnerships, and Disclosure

:::objectives
- Explain when partnering with creators makes sense.
- Select creators based on audience fit, not follower count alone.
- Structure a fair agreement covering deliverables, rights, and disclosure.
- Comply with FTC endorsement guidance and TikTok's disclosure settings.
:::

A **creator** (also called an influencer) is a person whose content attracts an engaged audience. Partnering with creators lets a business borrow trust and reach. Done well, it is efficient; done carelessly, it wastes money and can break the law.

## When to partner

Creator partnerships make sense when a creator's audience overlaps your segment, when the creator can authentically use and explain your product, and when you have the budget and processes to manage the relationship. For many local businesses, **micro-creators**, with smaller but locally concentrated audiences, are a better fit than large national accounts.

## Selecting creators

| Criterion | What to check |
|---|---|
| Audience fit | Location, age, interests (ask for audience data) |
| Content quality | Clarity, consistency, tone compatible with your brand |
| Engagement | Comments that show genuine conversation, not just emojis |
| Authenticity | Would they plausibly use your product? |
| History | Past disclosures done properly; no controversies that conflict with your values |

Be wary of accounts whose follower counts look inflated. The FTC's 2024 final rule on fake reviews and testimonials prohibits selling or buying fake indicators of social-media influence, such as followers or views generated by bots or hijacked accounts, for commercial purposes [14].

## Disclosure: the law and the platform

The FTC's Endorsement Guides say that if there is a material connection between an endorser and a marketer that the audience would not expect, such as payment, free products, or a family or employment relationship, it should be disclosed clearly and conspicuously [15]. FTC staff guidance for influencers advises that disclosures be placed with the endorsement itself, be easy to notice, and use plain language such as "ad" or "sponsored" [16]. Both the business and the creator have responsibilities; businesses should tell creators what to disclose and monitor that they do.

TikTok requires users to turn on its commercial content disclosure setting when posting content that promotes a brand, product, or service; the setting adds a "Paid partnership" label for third-party branded content or a promotional label for content about your own brand [17]. TikTok warns that undisclosed commercial content may be removed or restricted from the For You feed [17].

:::warning Your own employees count
If employees post about your business on their personal accounts, that is a material connection. They should disclose it ("I work here"). The same applies to relatives and business partners.
:::

## The agreement

A short written agreement prevents misunderstandings. Cover:

1. Deliverables: number of videos, format, posting dates.
2. Creative approval: what you may review, and what the creator controls.
3. Claims: what may and may not be said, especially about results, health, or safety.
4. Disclosure: how the connection will be disclosed, including the platform setting.
5. Usage rights: whether you may reuse the content in your own ads, and for how long.
6. Payment: amount, timing, and any product provided.
7. Termination: what happens if either side withdraws.

:::example Illustrative: comparing two creator offers
A coffee roaster considers two creators.

Creator A: 250,000 followers, national audience, quotes $2,000 for one video. Past videos average 40,000 views. Estimated local share of audience: 2 percent.

Creator B: 18,000 followers, 70 percent local, quotes $300 for one video plus product. Past videos average 6,000 views.

Estimated local views: A ≈ 40,000 × 0.02 = 800; B ≈ 6,000 × 0.70 = 4,200.
Cost per thousand local views: A ≈ $2,000 ÷ 0.8 = $2,500; B ≈ $300 ÷ 4.2 ≈ $71.

For a roaster that sells mostly at its café, Creator B is roughly 35 times more cost-efficient on local reach. If online national sales were the goal, the comparison would change. The roaster writes an agreement with Creator B specifying the disclosure setting, no health claims about caffeine, and 90-day usage rights for the video in its own ads.
:::

:::checklist Creator partnership
- Audience fit is verified with data, not assumed from follower count.
- Engagement looks genuine.
- A written agreement covers deliverables, claims, disclosure, rights, and payment.
- The creator will use TikTok's commercial content disclosure setting and a plain-language disclosure.
- I will monitor posts for proper disclosure.
- Employees and relatives who post about the business disclose their connection.
:::

:::summary
Creator partnerships borrow trust and reach when audience fit is real. Evaluate creators on audience overlap, quality, engagement, and authenticity. The FTC expects clear disclosure of material connections, and TikTok requires its commercial content disclosure setting for promotional posts. Fake followers are prohibited under the FTC's 2024 rule. A short written agreement protects both parties.
:::

# Chapter 10: Paid Promotion

:::objectives
- Explain when paid promotion should complement organic content.
- Describe the main structure of a TikTok ad campaign.
- Set a test budget and evaluate results with cost-per-result metrics.
- Recognize creative fatigue and plan refresh cycles.
:::

Organic content builds community and credibility, but its reach is unpredictable. **Paid promotion** buys predictable distribution to defined audiences. The two work best together: organic content reveals what resonates; paid promotion amplifies the proven winners to people likely to buy.

## When to start paying

A reasonable rule: do not pay to promote content until you have organic evidence that it resonates (above-average watch time and engagement for your account) and a landing page or offer that converts. Paying to amplify untested content or to send traffic to a weak page buys expensive lessons.

## Campaign structure

TikTok's advertising tools, like most platforms, organize promotion in layers:

| Layer | What you decide |
|---|---|
| Campaign | The objective (for example, reach, traffic, video views, leads, or website conversions) and overall budget |
| Ad group | Audience (location, age, interests), placement, schedule, and budget or bid |
| Ad | The creative: the video, caption, call to action, and destination |

TikTok also offers ways to promote organic posts, including posts from creators who authorize it, as ads. Using a post that already performed well organically is often a sensible starting point because it has passed an audience test. Menu names and features change; consult TikTok's current help center when setting up.

## Objectives and metrics

Choose the objective that matches your business goal, and judge the campaign on the corresponding cost-per-result:

- **Reach or awareness**: cost per thousand impressions (CPM); frequency.
- **Traffic**: cost per click (CPC); landing-page view rate.
- **Leads**: cost per lead; lead quality downstream.
- **Conversions**: cost per acquisition (CPA); return on ad spend (ROAS) = revenue attributed ÷ ad spend.

For conversion objectives, a tracking tool (TikTok calls it a pixel, with server-side options) records actions on your website. Installing it involves collecting data about visitors, so update your privacy policy accordingly and follow applicable privacy laws.

## Test budgets

A **test budget** is a deliberately limited amount spent to learn, not to scale. Define what you want to learn ("Which of three videos produces the cheapest booking?"), set a cap you can afford to lose entirely, and decide in advance how long the test runs.

:::example Illustrative: a three-video test for a yoga studio
Budget: $450 over 10 days, split evenly across three videos ($150 each), targeting adults within 8 miles of the studio. Objective: leads (free first-class signups).

| Video | Spend | Signups | Cost per signup | Attended | Became members |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: Class walkthrough | $150 | 12 | $12.50 | 7 | 2 |
| B: Instructor story | $150 | 6 | $25.00 | 5 | 2 |
| C: "First class nerves" FAQ | $150 | 15 | $10.00 | 11 | 4 |

Cost per member: A = $75; B = $75; C = $37.50. Video C wins on every stage. If a membership yields $70 a month in gross profit and members stay an average of 7 months (lifetime gross profit ≈ $490), a $37.50 acquisition cost is comfortably affordable, and so is $75. The studio scales Video C gradually, raising its daily budget by about 20 to 30 percent every few days while watching cost per member, and begins producing variations of the "nerves" theme.
:::

## Creative fatigue

**Creative fatigue** occurs when an audience has seen an ad too often and performance declines: rising cost per result, falling click-through rate, rising frequency. Short-form audiences tire of creative faster than on some other platforms. Plan to refresh creative regularly, using new hooks on proven themes.

| Signal | Possible cause | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Cost per result rising steadily | Fatigue or audience saturation | New hooks; broaden audience |
| High clicks, few conversions | Landing page mismatch | Align page with ad promise |
| Low watch time on paid views | Hook weak for cold audiences | Test new openings |
| Good results, small volume | Audience too narrow | Expand radius or interests |

:::warning Advertising rules still apply
Paid ads must follow TikTok's advertising policies and the law. Certain categories (for example, financial services, health products, and alcohol) carry extra restrictions. Claims must be truthful and substantiated; "results not typical" fine print does not cure a misleading testimonial.
:::

:::checklist Paid readiness
- I have organic evidence that the content resonates.
- My landing page or offer converts at an acceptable rate.
- I have chosen an objective that matches my business goal.
- My test budget is capped at an amount I can afford to lose.
- I know my maximum affordable cost per customer.
- Tracking is installed and my privacy policy reflects it.
- I have a plan to refresh creative.
:::

:::summary
Paid promotion makes reach predictable; it works best amplifying content proven organically and sending traffic to pages that convert. Campaigns are structured in campaign, ad group, and ad layers. Judge results by cost per result through to customers, not just clicks. Test with capped budgets, scale gradually, and refresh creative to manage fatigue.
:::

# Chapter 11: Measurement That Connects to Revenue

:::objectives
- Distinguish platform metrics from business metrics.
- Build a weekly TikTok scorecard.
- Attribute sales to TikTok using practical methods.
- Run a monthly content review that informs the next month's plan.
:::

TikTok supplies abundant numbers: views, likes, shares, followers, watch time. These are **platform metrics**. They are useful for improving content, but a business is ultimately judged on **business metrics**: leads, customers, revenue, and gross profit. Measurement must connect the two.

## The measurement chain

1. **Content quality**: average watch time, completion rate, retention curve.
2. **Engagement**: comments, shares, saves; profile visits.
3. **Intent**: link clicks, direction requests, messages, calls.
4. **Business outcome**: leads, bookings, orders, revenue, attributed to TikTok.

Each link informs a different decision. Weak content quality calls for better hooks and editing. Strong engagement but weak intent suggests unclear calls to action or a missing link. Strong intent but weak outcomes points to the landing page, offer, or follow-up.

## Attribution methods

Attribution, deciding which channel deserves credit for a sale, is imperfect on every platform. Use several methods together:

| Method | How | Strengths | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tracked links | Unique link (with UTM parameters) in bio or ads | Precise for clicks | Misses people who search you later |
| Promo codes | TikTok-specific code | Easy, works in store | Codes leak to coupon sites |
| "How did you hear?" | Ask at booking or checkout | Captures delayed effects | Memory errors |
| Platform conversion tracking | Pixel or server events | Detailed, automated | Platform-reported; can over-credit |
| Before-and-after comparison | Compare periods | Captures total effect | Confounded by seasonality |

## The weekly scorecard

Keep one row per week: videos posted, total views, average watch time, profile visits, link clicks, TikTok-attributed leads, TikTok-attributed customers, and revenue. Appendix E provides a template.

:::example Illustrative: diagnosing a scorecard
A furniture-restoration shop's four-week scorecard:

| Week | Videos | Views | Avg watch (s) | Profile visits | Link clicks | Leads | Jobs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 9,200 | 11 | 140 | 12 | 2 | 0 |
| 2 | 3 | 14,800 | 14 | 260 | 15 | 2 | 1 |
| 3 | 3 | 11,500 | 13 | 210 | 9 | 1 | 0 |
| 4 | 3 | 16,100 | 15 | 300 | 14 | 2 | 1 |

Profile visits grew from 140 to 300, but link clicks stayed around 9 to 15. Click rate from profile fell from about 8.6 percent (12 ÷ 140) to about 4.7 percent (14 ÷ 300). Diagnosis: content quality and interest are improving; the profile is not converting visitors. Fixes: rewrite the bio to state the service area and offer, pin a video explaining how to get a quote, and replace a generic link with a direct quote form. Measure click rate again in four weeks.
:::

## The monthly content review

Once a month, spend an hour reviewing:

1. **Top five and bottom five videos** by average watch time. What do the top ones share (hook type, pillar, length)?
2. **Pillar performance**: which pillars drive intent, not just views?
3. **Comment themes**: new questions for the backlog.
4. **Business outcomes**: TikTok-attributed leads and customers versus target.
5. **Decisions**: what to do more of, less of, and test next month.

# Chapter 12: Risk Management and a Ninety-Day Plan

:::objectives
- Identify platform, legal, reputational, and operational risks.
- Reduce platform dependence by building owned channels.
- Secure the account and define team permissions.
- Execute a ninety-day plan with weekly milestones.
:::

Every channel carries risk. TikTok's history, including years of legal uncertainty in the United States before the 2026 joint venture (Chapter 1), illustrates that platform conditions can change quickly. Good practice treats TikTok as a valuable rented space, not a foundation.

## Four categories of risk

| Risk | Example | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Platform | Policy change, account restriction, reach decline, regulatory action | Build owned channels (email, website); diversify |
| Legal | Undisclosed endorsements, unlicensed music, false claims, privacy violations | Disclosure settings; commercial music; claim review; privacy policy |
| Reputational | Insensitive trend use, poorly handled criticism | Voice guide; review before posting; response plan |
| Operational | Demand spike, account takeover, key person leaves | Demand plan; account security; shared access |

## Owning your audience

A follower on TikTok is not a customer you can contact directly. Convert attention into owned relationships: invite viewers to join an email list, book directly on your website, or visit in person. A simple, useful lead magnet (a checklist, a discount for first visits, a guide) in your profile link gives viewers a reason to connect off-platform. Follow the email and privacy rules discussed in TTI's other guides and the FTC's guidance.

## Account security

- Use a business account registered to a business email, not one person's personal address.
- Enable two-step verification.
- Use a password manager; do not share passwords by text or email.
- Limit who can post and who can access ad spending; remove access promptly when people leave.
- Review connected third-party apps periodically.

## Content review before posting

A two-minute pre-post check prevents most problems: Is every claim true and supportable? Is the music licensed? Does everyone on screen consent? Is any commercial relationship disclosed? Is there private information visible? Would we be comfortable if this were shown out of context?

## The ninety-day plan

## The decision at day ninety

At the end of ninety days, review the trend in business outcomes, the cost in time and money, and what was learned. Three outcomes are legitimate: continue and scale what works; adjust pillars or formats and run another ninety days; or pause TikTok and redirect effort to channels with better returns. Pausing is not failure; it is the disciplined use of a test.

# Appendix C: Script Templates

Each template is a starting structure. Replace bracketed text and keep total speech under about 150 words per minute of video.

**C2. Before and after (Proof, 15–30 seconds)**

- 2–5 s, Text: "Here's how we got here."

**C3. Reply to a comment (Response, 20–45 seconds)**

- 0–3 s, On-screen comment plus "Great question."

- 30–35 s, Payoff: "Keep the questions coming; I answer one every week."

**C4. Behind the scenes (Process, 30–60 seconds)**

- 0–3 s, Hook: "What happens to your [item] after you drop it off."
- 3–50 s, Follow the item through three to five steps, with captions.
- 50–60 s, Payoff: finished item, customer pickup (with consent), or the team.

**C5. Myth versus fact (Education, 20–40 seconds)**

- 0–3 s, Hook: "'[Common belief].' Not quite."
- 3–25 s, Explain what's actually true, with a demonstration.
- 25–35 s, Payoff: one practical takeaway. (Check that your "fact" is supportable; cite a source on screen if helpful.)

**C6. Meet the team (Personality, 20–40 seconds)**

# Glossary

**Attribution**: The process of assigning credit for a sale or lead to a marketing channel.

**Average watch time**: Mean number of seconds viewers watched a video.

**Batching**: Producing several videos in one session to save setup time.

**Brand voice**: The consistent personality expressed in a business's communication.

**Commercial content disclosure setting**: TikTok's setting that labels promotional or branded posts.

**Community**: Viewers who participate by commenting, sharing, and creating, as distinct from a passive audience.

**Completion rate**: Share of viewers who watch a video to the end.

**Content backlog**: A running list of video ideas, sorted by pillar.

**Content pillar**: A recurring theme that serves both audience interest and business goals.

**Cost per acquisition (CPA)**: Ad spend divided by customers or conversions acquired.

**Creative fatigue**: Declining ad performance as an audience sees the same creative repeatedly.

**Creator**: A person whose content attracts an engaged audience; sometimes called an influencer.

**Duet**: A TikTok feature placing a video side-by-side with another.

**For You feed**: TikTok's main recommendation feed, personalized for each viewer.

**Hook**: The opening seconds of a video, designed to earn continued attention.

**Material connection**: A relationship between an endorser and a marketer that could affect how an audience weighs the endorsement.

**Micro-creator**: A creator with a smaller, often more concentrated audience.

**Retention curve**: A graph of the percentage of viewers still watching at each second.

**ROAS (return on ad spend)**: Revenue attributed to ads divided by ad spend.

**Social listening**: Deliberately observing what an audience watches, says, and asks.

**Stitch**: A TikTok feature that places a clip of another video at the start of yours.

**Test budget**: A capped amount spent to learn which content or audience works.

**User-generated content (UGC)**: Content created by customers about a product or business.
