# How to Use This Book

This book is written for owners and managers of small service businesses (trades, clinics, salons, law and accounting offices, home services, and shops) who are considering an AI receptionist: software that answers the phone, holds a conversation in natural language, collects information, and passes it to a person. It assumes no technical background. It does assume that you care about your callers, that you will read the terms of any service you buy, and that you are willing to test the system before customers hear it.

AI voice tools have improved quickly, and vendors now market them to the smallest businesses. Used well, an AI front desk can answer calls you would otherwise miss, capture details accurately, and route urgent matters to the right person. Used carelessly, it can give wrong answers with confidence, make promises you cannot keep, violate phone and privacy laws, and frustrate the people you most want to serve. This book takes the position of a careful buyer: the technology is a tool, and the business that deploys it remains responsible for what it says and does.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this book you should be able to:

- Measure how many calls your business misses and why response speed matters.
- Explain what current AI receptionists can and cannot do reliably.
- Write a job description and a list of limits for the AI before choosing a product.
- Build a short, accurate knowledge base and a script with clear escalation paths.
- Disclose AI use honestly and design for callers who want a human.
- Identify the phone, recording, and consent rules that apply to AI voice calls.
- Protect caller information and evaluate a vendor's privacy and security practices.
- Compare vendors with a scorecard and resist exaggerated claims.
- Test the system with realistic and adversarial calls before launch.
- Run handoffs and callbacks reliably and track a small set of measures each week.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, defines key terms, and presents a step-by-step framework. Every chapter includes a worked example with real arithmetic, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Each chapter also contains a case study drawn from public sources: research studies, regulatory rulings and enforcement actions, tribunal decisions, company announcements, and credible journalism. Many cases describe failures, because failures show most clearly where the risks lie. The results in those cases belong to the people and organizations involved, not to Total Transformation Inc. (TTI).

The worked examples follow one fictional business, Ramirez Family Plumbing, owned by a fictional entrepreneur named Luis Ramirez in San Antonio, Texas. The company has four technicians and one part-time office coordinator, and its average service call is worth $420 in revenue. Ramirez Family Plumbing and Luis are illustrative only; they are not TTI customers, and their figures are not benchmarks or promises.

:::callout Scope and limits
This book is educational. It is not legal, telecommunications, privacy, or technical advice, and it promises no increase in calls, bookings, revenue, or any business outcome. Laws on robocalls, call recording, automated disclosures, and data privacy differ by state and country and change often. Confirm requirements with qualified counsel and the relevant agencies before deploying any AI phone system. Total Transformation Inc. is a for-profit company that offers an AI front desk pilot; this book is written so that you can apply it to any provider, or decide not to use one at all.
:::

# Chapter 1: Why Calls Still Matter

:::objectives
- Explain why response speed affects whether an inquiry becomes a customer.
- Measure your missed calls and their likely value.
- Distinguish missed calls by cause.
- Decide whether a front desk problem exists before buying a solution.
:::

Despite email, web forms, and messaging apps, the telephone remains the channel many customers use when the need is urgent or the purchase is significant. A burst pipe, a broken air conditioner, a toothache, or a legal problem usually prompts a phone call. When nobody answers, many callers simply try the next business.

## The research on response speed

The best-known study of response speed was published in the Harvard Business Review in 2011. The authors audited 2,241 U.S. companies by submitting web-generated test leads and timing the responses: 37 percent responded within an hour, 16 percent within one to 24 hours, 24 percent took more than 24 hours, and 23 percent never responded; among companies that responded within 30 days, the average response time was 42 hours [1]. In a separate analysis of 1.25 million sales leads at 42 companies, firms that tried to contact a potential customer within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as those that waited even an hour longer, and more than 60 times as likely as companies that waited 24 hours or more [1]. The study concerned web leads rather than phone calls, but the principle carries over: interest decays quickly.

## Definitions

A missed call is an inbound call that does not reach a person or an equivalent response. An abandoned call is one where the caller hangs up before being answered, often while on hold. A callback is a return call, ideally within a defined time. A qualified inquiry is a caller with a genuine need your business can serve. Speed to lead is the time between an inquiry and the first meaningful response.

## Why calls are missed

Calls are missed for predictable reasons: the owner or technician is working with their hands; the office line is busy; calls arrive after hours; staff are at lunch or in a meeting; or the phone system routes calls poorly. Each cause suggests a different fix. A business that misses calls mainly after hours may need an answering service or AI; one that misses them mainly at midday may need better staffing or a second line.

| Cause | Typical sign | Possible fixes |
|---|---|---|
| Hands busy in the field | Missed calls during job hours | Office coverage, callback blocks, AI front desk |
| Single line busy | Calls go to voicemail while on another call | Additional line, call queue, overflow routing |
| After hours | Missed calls evenings and weekends | Answering service, AI front desk, on-call rotation |
| Staff breaks | Clusters at lunch | Staggered breaks, overflow routing |
| Poor routing | Calls ring the wrong phone | Phone system configuration |

## A framework for measuring the problem

1. Pull call logs from your phone provider for the last 30 days.
2. Count total inbound calls, answered calls, and missed calls.
3. Remove spam and known non-customer calls.
4. Group missed calls by hour and day.
5. Call back a sample of missed numbers and record what they wanted.
6. Estimate the share of missed calls that were qualified inquiries.
7. Estimate the value of a qualified inquiry using your booking rate and average job value.
8. Decide which cause to address first.

:::case The Short Life of Online Sales Leads (real, cited)
James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington reported their findings in the Harvard Business Review in March 2011 [1]. Their audit of 2,241 U.S. companies found that fewer than four in ten responded to a web inquiry within an hour, nearly a quarter never responded at all, and the average response among those who did respond within a month took 42 hours [1]. Their analysis of 1.25 million leads found that contact attempts within an hour were associated with dramatically higher qualification rates than later attempts [1]. The findings come from the published article and describe the companies studied, not TTI. The study measures association rather than proving that speed alone causes sales, and it predates modern AI tools. Its lasting lesson is that many businesses respond far more slowly than they believe, and that measuring your own response time is the first step.
:::

:::example Ramirez Family Plumbing's missed calls (illustrative)
Luis pulls 30 days of call logs: 412 inbound calls, of which 298 were answered and 114 were missed. He removes 31 spam or vendor calls, leaving 83 missed calls from potential customers. He calls back 30 of them; 12 had already hired someone else, 9 booked with him, 5 were existing customers with questions, and 4 did not answer. Of the 26 he reached, 21 had a new job need (12 lost plus 9 booked), or about 81 percent. Applying that share to all 83 missed calls suggests about 0.81 × 83 = 67 missed calls with a job need in a month. If his normal booking rate on answered job inquiries is 60 percent and the average job is $420, the revenue at stake is roughly 67 × 0.60 × $420 = $16,884 per month. Luis treats this as an upper estimate, since some callers would have booked after a callback and some would never have booked at all. Grouping by hour shows that 58 percent of missed calls arrive between 9 a.m. and 2 p.m., when his coordinator is also answering texts and scheduling, and 30 percent arrive after 5 p.m.
:::

# Chapter 2: What AI Receptionists Do

:::objectives
- Describe how an AI receptionist processes a call.
- Distinguish rule-based phone menus from conversational AI.
- Identify tasks AI handles well and tasks that require humans.
- Recognize the main failure modes of large language models.
:::

An AI receptionist is software that answers a phone call, converts the caller's speech to text, uses a language model to decide what to say, converts that response to synthetic speech, and takes actions such as recording details, sending a text summary, booking an appointment, or transferring the call. Older interactive voice response (IVR) systems used fixed menus ("press 1 for billing"). Conversational systems let callers speak naturally and can handle varied phrasing.

## How a call flows

A typical call has five stages: greeting and disclosure; identification of the caller's need; collection of required details (name, number, address, problem, urgency); action (booking, message, transfer, or information); and closing with a confirmation of what happens next. Behind the scenes, the system draws on instructions written by the business (often called a prompt or configuration), a knowledge base of approved facts, and integrations with calendars or customer records.

## Strengths and weaknesses

Conversational AI is good at answering at any hour, handling many calls at once, collecting structured details consistently, and producing written summaries. It is weaker at handling unusual situations, understanding heavy background noise or some accents, reading emotion, exercising judgment, and knowing what it does not know. Large language models can produce fluent but false statements, sometimes called hallucinations. They can also be manipulated by callers who give them unusual instructions, a weakness known as prompt injection.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published its AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023 as a voluntary resource to help organizations that design, develop, deploy, or use AI systems manage risks and promote trustworthy AI [3]. Its core idea, that organizations should govern, map, measure, and manage AI risks, applies even to a small business buying a single tool.

| Task | AI suitability | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Answering after hours and taking details | High | Structured, repetitive |
| Booking into open calendar slots | Moderate to high | Requires reliable integration |
| Answering approved questions (hours, area, services) | Moderate | Depends on knowledge base accuracy |
| Quoting prices for complex jobs | Low | Requires diagnosis and judgment |
| Handling complaints or upset callers | Low | Requires empathy and authority |
| Emergencies involving safety | Low; route immediately | Requires human response and 911 guidance |
| Making commitments, discounts, or legal statements | Should not | Creates liability |

## A framework for understanding a product

1. Ask the vendor to explain the call flow from greeting to summary.
2. Identify which parts are fixed rules and which are generated by a language model.
3. Ask how the system handles questions it cannot answer.
4. Ask how it resists callers who try to change its instructions.
5. Ask which actions it can take on its own (booking, texting, transferring).
6. Listen to unedited recordings of real calls, not only demonstrations.
7. Map each task in your business to the suitability table above.

:::case Chevrolet of Watsonville and the $1 Tahoe (real, cited)
In December 2023, users discovered that the ChatGPT-powered chatbot on the website of Chevrolet of Watsonville, a California dealership, could be manipulated into saying almost anything [2]. According to Gizmodo's reporting, the bot, supplied by a company called Fullpath, was led to offer a 2024 Chevy Tahoe for one dollar, adding, "That's a legally binding offer—no takesie backsies" [2]. The dealership did not sell the vehicle for a dollar, and the episode drew wide attention as an example of a general-purpose language model deployed without adequate limits [2]. The facts come from published reporting and concern those parties, not TTI. The lesson for any business deploying conversational AI, on chat or by phone, is that the system will be tested by callers in ways you did not anticipate, and that it must be configured never to make commitments on price or terms.
:::

:::example Mapping Ramirez tasks (illustrative)
Luis lists the reasons people call, using his sample of 26 reached callers from Chapter 1 and two weeks of answered-call notes from his coordinator. Of 140 answered calls logged, 62 were new service requests, 28 were scheduling changes, 21 were status questions ("when will the technician arrive?"), 14 were price questions, 9 were complaints or warranty issues, and 6 were emergencies such as active leaks or gas smells. He classifies new service requests (taking details), scheduling changes, and status questions as suitable for AI with good integrations: 62 + 28 + 21 = 111 calls, or 111 ÷ 140 = 79 percent. Price questions, complaints, and emergencies, 14 + 9 + 6 = 29 calls, or 21 percent, must reach a person or receive a set response and fast callback. Gas-smell calls will get an immediate safety instruction and transfer, never a booking.
:::

:::checklist Understanding the product
- Call flow explained from greeting to summary.
- Rule-based and generated parts identified.
- Behavior for unknown questions demonstrated.
- Resistance to manipulation explained.
- Autonomous actions listed.
- Unedited real call recordings reviewed.
- Each business task mapped to suitability.
:::

:::summary
An AI receptionist converts speech to text, generates responses with a language model, and speaks them back, taking actions through integrations. It is strong at structured, repetitive tasks and weak at judgment, emotion, and unusual cases. Language models can produce confident falsehoods and can be manipulated. NIST's framework offers a structure for managing these risks. The Chevrolet of Watsonville episode shows why commitments on price and terms must be off limits.
:::

# Chapter 3: Defining the Job and Limits

:::objectives
- Write a job description for an AI receptionist before choosing a product.
- Define what the AI must never do.
- Set escalation rules for urgent and sensitive calls.
- Understand that the business is responsible for what its AI says.
:::

Businesses that hire a human receptionist usually write a job description. The same discipline applies to an AI receptionist, with one addition: a written list of limits. A person can use judgment when something unusual happens; an AI system does what its configuration and training lead it to do, which may be wrong in ways no one predicted. The safest approach is to give the AI a narrow job and firm boundaries.

## The job description

A job description for an AI front desk states when it answers (all calls, overflow only, or after hours), what it does on each call (greets, discloses, identifies the need, collects details, books or takes a message, confirms next steps), what information it may share (only approved facts from the knowledge base), what actions it may take (booking into specific calendar slots, sending a text summary to staff, transferring to a named number), and how quickly a human follows up.

## The limits list

Limits are statements of what the AI must never do. Typical limits include: never quote a price except approved fixed fees; never promise arrival times outside the booking system; never offer discounts, refunds, or exceptions; never give legal, medical, financial, or safety advice beyond a short approved instruction; never claim to be human; never collect payment card numbers by voice unless the system is specifically designed and certified for it; and never argue with a caller.

## Escalation rules

Escalation means handing the call to a person, or flagging it for immediate callback. Define triggers in plain language: any mention of gas smell, fire, flooding near electrical panels, injury, or threats to safety; any caller who asks for a human; any complaint or mention of a lawyer or a review; any caller who seems confused or distressed; and any question the AI cannot answer from the knowledge base. For life-safety emergencies, the AI should instruct the caller to contact 911 or the appropriate utility and should not attempt to manage the situation.

| Call type | AI action | Human follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| New service request | Collect details; book if slots open | Confirm within 1 business hour |
| Gas smell or electrical hazard | Give approved safety instruction; transfer to on-call | Immediate |
| Caller asks for a person | Transfer or promise callback with time | Within 15 minutes in business hours |
| Complaint or warranty issue | Take details; no promises | Owner callback same day |
| Price question | Share approved fixed fees only | Estimator callback |
| Question not in knowledge base | Say it will check; take details | Callback same day |

## Responsibility stays with the business

Courts and tribunals have begun to address who is responsible when an AI system gives wrong information. The answer so far is clear: the business that deploys it.

## A framework for defining the job

1. Decide when the AI answers: all calls, overflow, or after hours.
2. List the tasks it performs, in order.
3. List the information it may share, linked to an approved knowledge base.
4. List the actions it may take, with any limits.
5. Write the limits list in short "never" statements.
6. Write escalation triggers and the human response time for each.
7. Assign a named person responsible for the AI's performance.
8. Review and sign the job description before evaluating vendors.

:::case Moffatt v. Air Canada: the chatbot is part of the business (real, cited)
In November 2022, after his grandmother died, Jake Moffatt used a chatbot on Air Canada's website while booking flights; the chatbot suggested he could apply for bereavement fares retroactively, within 90 days of ticketing, although Air Canada's own bereavement webpage said the policy did not apply after travel was completed [4]. Relying on the chatbot, Moffatt bought two one-way tickets for $794.98 and $845.38, then applied for the reduced fare; an Air Canada representative later admitted the chatbot had provided "misleading words" [4]. Before British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal, Air Canada argued it could not be held liable for information provided by the chatbot [4]. The tribunal member called this "a remarkable submission," found that the chatbot was part of Air Canada's website and that the airline was responsible for all information on it, and awarded $650.88 in damages for negligent misrepresentation, plus interest and fees [4]. The facts are from the published 2024 decision and concern those parties, not TTI. For any business deploying an AI receptionist, the decision is a reminder that what the AI tells callers may be treated as what the business told them.
:::

:::example Ramirez job description (illustrative)
Luis decides the AI will answer only overflow calls (when the coordinator does not pick up within four rings) during business hours and all calls after 5 p.m. and on weekends. From Chapter 1, overflow and after-hours calls account for about 83 missed customer calls per month. The AI will collect name, callback number, address, problem description, and urgency; book into open "diagnostic visit" slots; and text a summary to the coordinator and the on-call technician. It may share only five approved facts: service area ZIP codes, business hours, the $89 diagnostic fee that is credited toward repairs, licensing status, and accepted payment types. If Luis expects an AI to handle 83 calls per month and 6 percent of all calls are emergencies (from Chapter 2), he plans for about 0.06 × 83 = 5 emergency transfers per month to the on-call technician, and he confirms the on-call phone can receive transfers at night.
:::

# Chapter 4: Building the Knowledge Base

:::objectives
- Explain why AI accuracy depends on approved source material.
- Build a short knowledge base of verified facts.
- Write a script with greeting, disclosure, questions, and closing.
- Keep the knowledge base current with an owner and review date.
:::

A knowledge base is the set of approved facts the AI may draw on: services, hours, service area, fees, policies, and answers to common questions. Most AI receptionist products let a business upload documents or type answers. The quality of those inputs largely determines the quality of the answers. A knowledge base that is long, outdated, or contradictory invites errors; one that is short, current, and specific limits them.

## Principles for a good knowledge base

Include only facts you are willing to have repeated to every caller. Write each fact as a short, direct statement. Remove anything that requires judgment, such as "usually" or "it depends." For topics that require judgment, write an instruction to take details and promise a callback. Assign each fact an owner and a review date. Keep legal, regulatory, and safety content minimal, and have it reviewed by a qualified person.

## The script

The script is the structure of the conversation. It should include a greeting with the business name; a clear disclosure that the caller is speaking with an automated assistant; an offer to reach a person; the questions needed to help, in order; a confirmation that reads back key details; and a closing that states exactly what happens next and when.

| Script element | Example wording | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Greeting | "Thanks for calling Ramirez Family Plumbing." | Confirms the caller reached the right business |
| Disclosure | "I'm the company's automated assistant." | Honesty; legal compliance in some places |
| Human option | "If you'd like a person, say 'team member' at any time." | Respect and accessibility |
| Need | "What's going on with your plumbing today?" | Opens the conversation |
| Details | Name, number, address, problem, urgency | Enables follow-up |
| Read-back | "I have you at 210-555-0142 at 14 Oak Street. Is that right?" | Prevents errors |
| Closing | "A coordinator will call you by 10 a.m. tomorrow." | Sets expectation |

## A framework for the knowledge base and script

1. List the 15 to 25 questions callers ask most often, from call notes.
2. Write a short approved answer for each, or a "take details" instruction.
3. Verify each answer against your current policies and prices.
4. Remove anything that requires judgment or legal interpretation.
5. Assign an owner and a quarterly review date to each answer.
6. Write the script elements in order, using plain words.
7. Read the script aloud and time it; aim for a greeting under 15 seconds.
8. Record every change with a date.

:::case New York City's MyCity chatbot (real, cited)
In October 2023, New York City launched an AI-powered chatbot, built with Microsoft technology, to help people with information on starting and operating a business in the city [5]. In March 2024, The Markup, working with partner newsrooms, reported that the chatbot often gave incomplete or wrong answers, including telling users that employers could take a cut of workers' tips and that landlords did not need to accept tenants with rental assistance, although source-of-income discrimination is illegal in the city with limited exceptions [5]. A housing expert who tested it found that it also said it was legal to lock out a tenant [5]. In January 2026, The Markup reported that the new mayor planned to take the chatbot down, calling it "functionally unusable" and noting it had cost about half a million dollars [6]. The facts come from The Markup's reporting and concern the city, not TTI. The lesson for a small business is that an AI answering from broad or unverified sources can sound authoritative while being wrong; a short, verified knowledge base and a "take details and call back" rule for anything else reduce that risk.
:::

:::example Ramirez knowledge base (illustrative)
Luis's coordinator reviews two weeks of call notes and lists 22 common questions. Luis writes approved answers for 14 of them, such as service ZIP codes, hours, the $89 diagnostic fee credited toward repairs, and whether they service tankless water heaters (yes). For the other 8, including "How much to replace a water heater?" and "Is this covered by my home warranty?", he writes a "take details and promise an estimator callback" instruction. That is 14 ÷ 22 = 64 percent answered directly and 36 percent routed to a person. He reads his script aloud: greeting, disclosure, and human option take 12 seconds. He sets quarterly review dates and assigns the coordinator to update the knowledge base whenever a price changes.
:::

:::checklist Knowledge base and script
- 15 to 25 common questions listed from real calls.
- Each answer verified and written in one or two sentences.
- Judgment questions routed to a callback.
- Each answer has an owner and review date.
- Script includes greeting, disclosure, human option, questions, read-back, closing.
- Greeting under 15 seconds when read aloud.
- Change log kept.
:::

:::summary
The AI is only as accurate as its approved sources. Build a short, verified knowledge base, route judgment questions to people, and write a script that greets, discloses, offers a human, collects details, reads them back, and states next steps. New York City's MyCity chatbot shows how an authoritative-sounding system can give unlawful advice when its answers are not tightly controlled.
:::

# Chapter 5: Disclosure and Trust

:::objectives
- Explain why callers should know they are talking to an AI.
- Identify laws that require or encourage bot disclosure.
- Design for callers who prefer a human.
- Make the system accessible to callers with disabilities.
:::

Trust is the front desk's real product. Callers share their names, addresses, and problems because they believe they are dealing with the business. An AI that pretends to be human, even convincingly, puts that trust at risk once the caller discovers the truth. Disclosure also matters because many people are wary of AI.

## Public attitudes

The Pew Research Center reported that in a June 2025 survey, half of U.S. adults said the increased use of AI in daily life made them feel more concerned than excited, 10 percent said more excited than concerned, and 38 percent said they felt both equally; in 2021, 37 percent had said they were more concerned than excited [10]. A business deploying AI on its phones should assume that some callers will be uneasy and should make the human option easy.

## Disclosure laws

Some jurisdictions require disclosure. California's bot disclosure law makes it unlawful to use a bot to communicate or interact with a person in California online with the intent to mislead the person about its artificial identity in order to incentivize a commercial transaction or influence a vote, and it provides that a person using a bot is not liable if the bot's nature is disclosed clearly and conspicuously [9]. That statute addresses online communication; whether and how it applies to voice calls depends on the facts. Other states have adopted or are considering AI disclosure rules, and the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) has addressed AI voices under robocall law (Chapter 6). A simple disclosure on every call satisfies the spirit of these rules and avoids the question.

## Designing for the human option

Offer a human early and honor the request quickly. If no one is available, say so honestly and give a specific callback time. Do not loop callers back into the AI after they have asked for a person.

## Accessibility

Some callers use relay services. The FCC explains that Telecommunications Relay Service allows people with hearing or speech disabilities to place and receive calls through a communications assistant, and that dialing 711 connects to relay service anywhere in the United States [25]. Relay calls may include pauses and unusual phrasing; your AI should not hang up on silence too quickly. Provide alternatives such as a text line and web form, and follow the Department of Justice's guidance on web accessibility for any online booking you offer [26].

| Practice | Why it matters | How to implement |
|---|---|---|
| Disclose AI at the start | Trust; legal risk reduction | One short sentence after greeting |
| Offer a human early | Respect for preferences | "Say 'team member' at any time" |
| Honor requests quickly | Prevents frustration | Transfer or specific callback time |
| Allow longer silences | Relay and speech differences | Raise silence timeout |
| Offer other channels | Accessibility | Text line, web form |

## A framework for disclosure and trust

1. Write a one-sentence AI disclosure and place it immediately after the greeting.
2. Write a human-option sentence and test that the request works every time.
3. Set transfer and callback rules for when no one is available.
4. Adjust silence timeouts and test with slow speech and relay-style calls.
5. Provide a text line and web form as alternatives.
6. Check state disclosure rules with counsel.
7. Review complaints about the AI monthly.

:::case Google Duplex and the backlash over a human-sounding bot (real, cited)
In May 2018, Google demonstrated Duplex, an AI system that could phone a business to book an appointment; in the demo, which included a call to a hair salon, the voice used natural pauses and filler words such as "um," and there was no indication to the person answering that the call came from a computer [7]. The demonstration drew criticism; technology sociologist Zeynep Tufekci called it "ethically lost" [7]. Within days, Google said it was "designing this feature with disclosure built-in" and would make sure the system was appropriately identified [7]. In June 2018, when Google let journalists receive test calls in restaurants, the system first identified itself as a bot [8]. The facts come from published reporting and concern Google, not TTI. The episode shows that a voice good enough to pass as human creates a duty to disclose, and that the public reacts strongly when that duty is ignored.
:::

:::example Ramirez disclosure test (illustrative)
Luis writes the opening: "Thanks for calling Ramirez Family Plumbing. I'm the company's automated assistant. If you'd like a team member at any point, just say so." He runs 20 test calls in which the caller asks for a person in different ways ("can I talk to someone," "real person," "operator," "human"). The system recognizes 17 of 20, or 85 percent. He adds the three missed phrases as triggers and retests; it recognizes 20 of 20. He then runs 5 calls with a 6-second pause before answering a question, simulating relay timing; the system hung up on 2. # Chapter 6: Phone and Recording Laws

:::objectives
- Explain how the TCPA applies to AI-generated voices.
- Distinguish answering inbound calls from placing outbound calls.
- Identify consent requirements for calls, texts, and recordings.
- Build a compliance checklist with counsel.
:::

Phone communications are heavily regulated in the United States. Most of the rules were written for robocalls and telemarketing, but they now apply to AI voices. An AI that only answers inbound calls carries less legal risk than one that places outbound calls or sends texts, but recording, transcription, and follow-up messaging all raise questions.

## AI voices under the TCPA

In February 2024, the FCC issued a declaratory ruling confirming that the Telephone Consumer Protection Act's restrictions on the use of an "artificial or prerecorded voice" encompass current AI technologies that generate human voices, so calls using such technologies require the prior express consent of the called party, subject to the law's exemptions [11]. The FCC's rules page for callers explains that a caller must obtain consent before making an informational autodialed or prerecorded call or text to a wireless number, and describes exceptions such as emergencies [12]. The FCC's consumer guide adds that prerecorded telemarketing calls require prior express written consent [27].

In practice, an AI that answers calls customers place to you is not making robocalls. But if the system calls back missed callers with an AI voice, sends automated texts, or places reminder calls, consent rules apply. Get advice on what consent you have and how to document it.

## Recording and transcription

Most AI receptionists record and transcribe calls. The Digital Media Law Project explains that federal law permits recording with the consent of at least one party, but that some states require the consent of all parties to the communication, and it advises checking the law of your state before recording [13]. Because callers may be in a different state, many businesses play a short notice ("this call may be recorded") to every caller.

| Activity | Main rule to check | Practical step |
|---|---|---|
| AI answers inbound calls | State recording consent; disclosure rules | Recording notice at start |
| AI voice calls back missed callers | TCPA consent for artificial voice | Get counsel; document consent |
| Automated text follow-ups | TCPA consent for texts | Collect consent; honor STOP |
| Appointment reminder calls | TCPA; informational call rules | Confirm consent at booking |
| Marketing calls or texts | Prior express written consent | Avoid unless counsel approves |

## A framework for compliance

1. List every communication the system will make: answer, call back, text, remind, market.
2. For each, identify whether it uses an artificial or prerecorded voice or automated text.
3. With counsel, determine the consent required and how to record it.
4. Add a recording notice at the start of every call.
5. Configure opt-out handling: STOP for texts, "don't call me" for calls.
6. Keep consent records with date, method, and wording.
7. Avoid marketing calls and texts until counsel approves the program.
8. Review rules annually, since the FCC and states update them often.

:::case AI voice cloning and the New Hampshire robocalls (real, cited)
In January 2024, voters in New Hampshire received robocalls using an AI-generated voice that imitated President Joe Biden and urged them not to vote in the state's primary [14]. The FCC announced in August 2024 that Lingo Telecom, the voice service provider that transmitted the spoofed calls, agreed to pay a $1 million civil penalty and adopt strict caller-authentication and customer-verification measures; the calls had been directed by a political consultant, Steve Kramer [14]. In September 2024, Reuters reported that the FCC finalized a $6 million fine against Kramer [15]. FCC Chairwoman Jessica Rosenworcel said, "If AI is being used, that should be made clear to any consumer, citizen, and voter who encounters it" [14]. The facts are from the FCC and Reuters and concern those parties, not TTI. A small business is unlikely to face facts like these, but the case shows how seriously regulators treat AI voices on phone networks, and why disclosure and consent belong at the center of any AI phone deployment.
:::

:::example Ramirez communications map (illustrative)
Luis's vendor offers three features: inbound answering, an automatic AI callback to missed callers within two minutes, and text reminders the day before appointments. He maps each with his attorney. Inbound answering requires a recording notice. The AI callback is an outbound call using an artificial voice, so counsel advises against enabling it until consent practices are documented; instead, the system will send a text summary to staff for a human callback. Reminder texts require consent collected at booking, so the AI will ask, "May we text you a reminder about this appointment? You can reply STOP anytime." If 70 of the 83 monthly AI-handled calls result in a booking or message and 80 percent agree to texts, Luis expects about 0.80 × 70 = 56 consented reminder recipients per month, each recorded with date and wording.
:::

:::checklist Phone compliance
- Every communication type listed.
- Artificial voice and automated text uses identified.
- Consent requirements confirmed with counsel.
- Recording notice at start of every call.
- STOP and do-not-call requests honored automatically.
- Consent records stored with date and wording.
- No marketing calls or texts without counsel approval.
- Annual review of rules scheduled.
:::

:::summary
The FCC has confirmed that AI-generated voices are artificial voices under the TCPA, so AI calls generally require prior express consent, with exemptions. Answering inbound calls is lower risk than placing outbound AI calls or texts. Recording consent rules differ by state, so play a notice. The New Hampshire AI robocall enforcement shows how seriously regulators treat AI voices on phone networks.
:::

# Chapter 7: Privacy and Data Security

:::objectives
- Identify the personal information an AI front desk collects.
- Apply the FTC's five principles for protecting personal information.
- Recognize when health privacy rules may apply.
- Write data rules for vendors and staff.
:::

An AI receptionist is a data collection system. Every call may produce a recording, a transcript, a summary, and records of names, phone numbers, addresses, and descriptions of problems. Some callers will volunteer sensitive details: a medical condition, a divorce, a financial hardship, a gate code. Before launch, decide what to collect, where it goes, who can see it, and when it is deleted.

## The FTC's five principles

The Federal Trade Commission's guide for businesses on protecting personal information organizes good practice around five principles: take stock of what personal information you have and where it is; scale down by keeping only what you need; lock it by protecting what you keep; pitch it by properly disposing of what you no longer need; and plan ahead by creating a plan to respond to security incidents [16]. These principles translate directly to AI phone systems.

| Principle | Question for an AI front desk | Example practice |
|---|---|---|
| Take stock | What does each call create, and where is it stored? | Map recordings, transcripts, summaries, CRM entries |
| Scale down | Do we need full recordings, or just summaries? | Collect only fields needed for the job |
| Lock it | Who can access call data? | Role-based access; two-factor login |
| Pitch it | How long do we keep it? | Delete recordings after 90 days unless needed |
| Plan ahead | What if the vendor is breached? | Incident contacts and steps written down |

## Health information

Businesses covered by HIPAA, such as many medical, dental, and therapy practices, have additional duties. The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services explains that when a covered entity uses a business associate to perform functions involving protected health information, the HIPAA rules require a written contract or other arrangement that establishes what the business associate may do with the information and requires it to protect the information [17]. A healthcare practice should not use an AI receptionist vendor that will not sign an appropriate business associate agreement. Even businesses outside HIPAA should treat health details with care, as the case below shows.

## A framework for data protection

1. Map every data element a call creates and every system it touches.
2. Remove fields you do not need; prefer summaries over full recordings if possible.
3. Set role-based access and require two-factor authentication.
4. Set retention periods and confirm the vendor deletes data on schedule.
5. Read the vendor's terms on training AI models with your data; opt out if possible.
6. Sign required agreements, such as business associate agreements, before launch.
7. Instruct the AI not to request sensitive data (card numbers, Social Security numbers, health details) unless necessary and approved.
8. Write an incident plan with names and phone numbers.

:::case BetterHelp and the sharing of sensitive data (real, cited)
In March 2023, the FTC announced a proposed order against BetterHelp, an online counseling service, alleging that it shared consumers' sensitive data, including health information, with Facebook, Snapchat, and others for advertising purposes after promising to keep such data private [18]. Under the order, BetterHelp agreed to pay $7.8 million to provide partial refunds to consumers, and it was to be banned from sharing consumers' health data for advertising [18]. The facts come from the FTC's announcement and concern BetterHelp, not TTI, and BetterHelp did not use an AI receptionist. The lesson applies because AI call systems collect similar information: what callers say, and what you promise about it, creates obligations. Check where call data flows, including analytics and advertising tools, and make sure your practices match your privacy statements.
:::

:::example Ramirez data map (illustrative)
Luis maps each call: the vendor stores a recording and transcript; a summary goes by text to the coordinator and on-call technician; the booking goes into his scheduling software. He decides he needs summaries and bookings but not long-term recordings. He sets recordings to delete after 60 days, enough to resolve disputes, and confirms in writing that the vendor does so. At 83 calls per month, that limits stored recordings to about 83 × 2 = 166 at any time instead of about 1,000 after a year. He restricts recording access to himself and the coordinator, turns on two-factor login, and adds a script rule: "Never ask for card numbers; say payment is collected by the technician."
:::

# Chapter 8: Choosing a Vendor

:::objectives
- Evaluate AI receptionist vendors against your job description.
- Recognize unsupported or deceptive AI marketing claims.
- Compare total costs, not just monthly fees.
- Negotiate terms that protect the business.
:::

The market for AI receptionists is crowded and changes quickly. Vendors range from features inside phone systems to stand-alone services. Prices may be per minute, per call, per month, or a combination. Evaluating them requires the job description from Chapter 3, a skeptical view of marketing claims, and a structured trial.

## Evaluating claims

The FTC has warned businesses that make AI claims to avoid exaggerating what their products can do, to avoid promising that an AI product does something better than a non-AI product without adequate proof, and to be aware of reasonably foreseeable risks [20]. Buyers can use the same standard: ask vendors for evidence of every claim. "Books 90 percent of calls" means nothing without knowing whose calls, what kind of business, and how "booked" was measured.

## Total cost

Total cost includes setup fees, monthly fees, per-minute or per-call charges, integration costs, phone number porting, staff time to build the knowledge base and review calls, and the cost of exiting. Compare total monthly cost with the value at stake from Chapter 1.

| Evaluation area | Questions to ask | Evidence to request |
|---|---|---|
| Fit to job description | Can it handle overflow-only routing, transfers, your calendar? | Live demo with your scenarios |
| Accuracy | How does it prevent made-up answers? | Test calls during trial |
| Disclosure and consent | Can we set disclosure and recording notices? | Configuration screens |
| Data | Where is data stored, for how long, and is it used for training? | Written data terms |
| Pricing | What is the full monthly cost at our volume? | Written quote |
| Exit | Can we export data and port our number? | Contract terms |
| Support | Who helps when it fails? | Support hours; references |

## A framework for vendor selection

1. Shortlist three vendors that support your required features.
2. Send each your job description and limits list.
3. Ask for written answers on data, pricing, and exit terms.
4. Request evidence for any performance claims.
5. Run a two-week trial using the test plan in Chapter 9.
6. Score each vendor on the scorecard in Appendix E.
7. Calculate total monthly cost at expected volume.
8. Negotiate a month-to-month or short initial term.

:::case Operation AI Comply and DoNotPay (real, cited)
In September 2024, the FTC announced "Operation AI Comply," a law enforcement sweep with five actions against companies that used AI to deceive consumers or sold AI tools used for deception [19]. One action targeted DoNotPay, which claimed to offer "the world's first robot lawyer"; the FTC alleged that the company did not test whether its AI matched the level of a human lawyer and had not hired or retained any attorneys, and that its tool claiming to check small business websites for legal violations was not effective [19]. # Chapter 9: Testing Before Launch

:::objectives
- Design a test plan with ordinary, difficult, and adversarial calls.
- Set pass criteria before testing.
- Test with diverse voices, accents, and conditions.
- Decide when the system is ready to go live.
:::

Testing is where most problems should be found. A system that works perfectly in a vendor demo can fail with real callers who speak quickly, call from noisy job sites, have strong accents, change their minds, or try to confuse the system. A test plan should be written before testing starts, with clear pass criteria.

## Types of test calls

Ordinary calls are common requests handled correctly. Difficult calls include background noise, poor connections, accents, fast or slow speech, interruptions, and callers who change details partway through. Adversarial calls deliberately try to get the AI to break rules: demanding a discount, asking for a guarantee, trying to get it to agree to something absurd, or asking it to say it is human. Safety calls test emergency triggers.

## Pass criteria

Set criteria in advance. For example: 100 percent of safety calls receive the correct instruction and transfer; 100 percent of requests for a human are honored; 95 percent of ordinary calls collect correct details; zero calls in which the AI gives unapproved prices or promises; and zero calls in which the AI claims to be human.

| Test category | Number of calls | Pass criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary requests | 10 | 95% correct details and booking |
| Difficult conditions | 8 | 85% correct details; graceful failure otherwise |
| Adversarial | 6 | 0 unapproved promises |
| Safety triggers | 4 | 100% correct instruction and transfer |
| Human requests | 2 | 100% honored |

## A framework for testing

1. Write about 30 test scenarios across the five categories (Appendix F).
2. Set pass criteria for each category before testing.
3. Recruit testers with different voices, ages, and accents.
4. Test from different phones and locations, including noisy ones.
5. Record results in a log with the call ID and what went wrong.
6. Fix problems by adjusting the script, knowledge base, or limits.
7. Retest failed scenarios plus a sample of passed ones.
8. Go live only when all criteria are met, starting with a limited window.

:::case McDonald's and Taco Bell drive-through AI (real, cited)
In June 2024, CNBC reported that McDonald's would end its test of automated order-taking with IBM in more than 100 U.S. restaurants by late July 2024; the partnership had begun in 2021, and people familiar with the matter said the technology had trouble interpreting different accents and dialects, affecting order accuracy [21]. The BBC later reported that Taco Bell was rethinking its use of AI in drive-throughs after viral videos of mishaps, including a customer who ordered 18,000 cups of water, even though the system had been rolled out at more than 500 U.S. locations since 2023 [22]. The facts come from CNBC and the BBC and concern those companies, not TTI. Both examples come from large companies with significant resources, and both show why testing with real-world speech and adversarial behavior matters before, and after, launch.
:::

:::example Ramirez test results (illustrative)
Luis runs 30 test calls. Results: ordinary 10 of 10 correct; difficult 6 of 8 (75 percent, below the 85 percent target), with failures on a call from a noisy truck and a caller with a strong accent who spoke quickly; adversarial 5 of 6, with one failure where the AI said "we can probably do that for less" when pressed on price; safety 4 of 4; human requests 2 of 2. # Chapter 10: Handoffs and Measurement

:::objectives
- Design reliable handoffs from AI to staff.
- Choose weekly metrics that show whether the system works.
- Review calls regularly and improve the system.
- Keep the human option permanent.
:::

The AI receptionist does not finish the job; it starts it. A captured call is worth nothing if no one calls back. The handoff from AI to staff is therefore as important as the AI itself. Measurement tells you whether the whole system, AI plus people, is capturing and converting more calls than before.

## Handoffs

A good handoff includes a summary sent immediately to the right person, with the caller's name, number, address, need, urgency, and what the AI promised. It includes a clear owner and a response deadline. It also includes a fallback: if the owner does not respond within the deadline, the summary goes to a second person.

## Metrics

| Metric | Definition | Example target |
|---|---|---|
| Answer rate | Calls answered by staff or AI ÷ total calls | 98% |
| AI containment | Calls fully handled by AI without transfer | Track; no fixed target |
| Callback speed | Median time from AI call to staff callback | Under 1 hour in business hours |
| Booking rate | Bookings ÷ customer calls with a job need | At or above pre-AI rate |
| Transfer success | Transfers completed ÷ transfers attempted | 95% |
| Error rate | Calls with wrong information or broken rule ÷ reviewed calls | Under 2% |
| Complaints | Complaints mentioning the AI | Review each |

Containment is easy to over-value. A system that handles every call without a person may be preventing callers from reaching help they need. Booking rate, callback speed, and errors matter more.

## Review routine

Each week, review a sample of calls, including every transfer, every complaint, and every call flagged as low confidence. Update the knowledge base and script, record changes, and retest anything significant.

## A framework for handoffs and measurement

1. Define the summary fields and who receives each call type.
2. Set response deadlines and a fallback recipient.
3. Record baseline metrics from your Chapter 1 audit.
4. Track the seven metrics weekly (Appendix G).
5. Review a sample of 10 to 20 calls each week, plus all transfers and complaints.
6. Update and retest after each significant change.
7. Compare results with baseline monthly.
8. Keep the human option permanent, regardless of metrics.

:::case Klarna's AI assistant and the return of human agents (real, cited)
In February 2024, Klarna announced that its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, two-thirds of its customer service chats, doing work it said was equivalent to 700 full-time agents, with customer satisfaction on par with human agents; the company noted that customers could still choose to speak with a human [23]. In May 2025, CX Dive reported that Klarna was recruiting human customer service workers again so that customers would always have the option to speak to a person, quoting CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski: "there will be always a human if you want" [24]. The figures are Klarna's own and concern Klarna, not TTI. The arc of the story is useful: even a company reporting strong AI results chose to reinforce the human option. A small business should measure the whole system and keep a person within reach.
:::

:::example Ramirez first month results (illustrative)
In the first month after full launch, Ramirez records 420 calls. Staff answer 312, the AI handles 101, and 7 are abandoned before the greeting, for an answer rate of (312 + 101) ÷ 420 = 98.3 percent, compared with (412 − 114) ÷ 412 = 72.3 percent in the Chapter 1 audit. Of the 101 AI calls, 74 are customers with a job need; 41 book directly and 22 more book after a staff callback, so the booking rate is 63 ÷ 74 = 85 percent. The coordinator's median callback time is 38 minutes. Reviewing 20 calls, Luis finds one error, a wrong ZIP code answer, which is 5 percent of the sample; he corrects the knowledge base. If the extra bookings are about 63 at an average job of $420, they represent roughly $26,460 in work, though Luis notes that some of those callers might have called back or booked anyway, so he treats the estimate as an upper bound, not a guaranteed result.
:::

:::checklist Weekly review
- Summary fields and recipients confirmed.
- Response deadlines and fallback in place.
- Seven metrics recorded.
- 10 to 20 calls reviewed plus all transfers and complaints.
- Knowledge base and script updated.
- Changes logged and retested.
- Human option tested.
:::

:::summary
The AI starts the job; people finish it. Design handoffs with summaries, owners, deadlines, and fallbacks. Track answer rate, callback speed, booking rate, transfer success, errors, and complaints, and do not over-value containment. Review calls weekly. Klarna's experience shows that even strong AI results do not remove the need for a human option.

| 12. Booking or message | "I can offer [slot] or [slot]." or "I'll pass this to our team." | Only approved slots |
| 13. Text consent (if used) | "May we text you about this appointment? You can reply STOP anytime." | Record yes or no |
| 14. Read-back | "Let me confirm: [name], [number], [address], [need], [time]." | Correct errors |
| 15. Closing | "[Person or team] will call you by [time]. Thanks for calling." | Specific time |

Fallback lines:

- When the AI does not know: "I don't have that information, but I'll make sure a team member calls you back by [time]."
- When asked for a price outside approved fees: "Our estimator will give you an accurate price after looking at the job. I'll have them call you."
- When asked "Are you a real person?": "No, I'm an automated assistant for [Business Name]. Would you like me to connect you with a team member?"
- When the caller is upset: "I'm sorry this has been frustrating. Let me get a team member to help you." Then transfer or flag for immediate callback.

# Glossary

AI receptionist: Software that answers phone calls, converses in natural language, collects information, and passes it to staff.

After-hours coverage: Answering calls outside normal business hours, by a person, service, or AI.

All-party consent: A legal rule in some states requiring every party's consent to record a call.

Answer rate: Calls answered by staff or AI divided by total inbound calls.

Artificial voice: Under the TCPA as interpreted by the FCC, includes AI-generated voices that imitate human speech.

Business associate agreement: A HIPAA-required contract between a covered entity and a vendor handling protected health information.

Containment: The share of calls an AI handles without transferring to a person.

Disclosure: A clear statement that the caller is speaking with an automated assistant.

Escalation: Handing a call to a person or flagging it for immediate human follow-up.

Hallucination: An AI response that is fluent but false or unsupported.

Handoff: The transfer of call information and responsibility from the AI to staff.

Knowledge base: The approved set of facts and instructions an AI may use to answer callers.

Limits list: Written "never" statements defining what the AI must not do.

One-party consent: A legal rule allowing a call to be recorded with the consent of one participant.

Overflow routing: Sending calls to the AI only when staff do not answer within a set number of rings.

Prior express consent: Permission required under the TCPA before certain automated or artificial-voice calls and texts.

Read-back: Repeating key details to the caller to confirm accuracy.

Retention period: How long recordings, transcripts, and records are kept before deletion.

Speed to lead: The time between a customer's inquiry and the business's first response.

TCPA: The Telephone Consumer Protection Act, the federal law governing autodialed, prerecorded, and artificial-voice calls and texts.

Telecommunications Relay Service: A service that lets people with hearing or speech disabilities place calls through a communications assistant, reachable by dialing 711.
