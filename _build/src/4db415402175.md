# How to Use This Book

The limited liability company (LLC) is the most common choice of legal structure for new small businesses in the United States, and for good reason: it is flexible, relatively inexpensive to form, and offers owners a shield against many business debts. It is also widely misunderstood. Owners frequently believe that forming an LLC automatically reduces taxes, that it protects them from every lawsuit, or that once the paperwork is filed nothing more is required. Each of those beliefs is wrong in ways that can be expensive.

This book explains how an LLC actually works, how to form one correctly, and, just as importantly, how to keep it working. It is written for first-time owners, freelancers converting from sole proprietorships, and partners starting a business together.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Compare the LLC with sole proprietorships, partnerships, and corporations on liability, tax, cost, and formality.
- Decide whether an LLC fits your situation and which state to form in.
- Choose and clear a name, appoint a registered agent, and file formation documents.
- Draft or review an operating agreement that addresses the issues that cause most disputes.
- Obtain an EIN directly from the IRS and understand your LLC's federal tax classification options.
- Identify state taxes, licenses, and permits, and understand the current federal beneficial ownership reporting status.
- Run the business in a way that preserves the liability shield.
- Build an annual compliance calendar and handle changes in membership or dissolution.

## How Each Chapter Is Built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, explains concepts in plain language with defined terms, presents a step-by-step procedure, and works through a numerical example labelled "illustrative." Where real cases, court decisions, or government actions illustrate a point, a case study box summarizes the public record with a citation. Each chapter closes with a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. The appendices contain templates and calendars.

## State Law Varies

LLCs are creatures of state law. Filing fees, annual reports, taxes, naming rules, and even the name of the formation document differ by state. This book uses California and Delaware as recurring examples because both publish clear official guidance, but you must confirm every requirement with your own state's Secretary of State (or equivalent) and revenue department.

It does not guarantee that a filing will be approved, that you will save money, or that you will be protected from liability. Consult a licensed attorney and tax professional for decisions specific to your circumstances.
:::

# Chapter 1: What an LLC Is and Is Not

:::objectives
- Define an LLC and identify its two defining features.
- Compare the LLC with other common business structures using the SBA's framework.
- Explain the difference between legal classification and tax classification.
- Identify three common misconceptions about LLCs.
:::

A **limited liability company** is a business entity created under state statute that combines the liability protection of a corporation with the flexible management and tax treatment of a partnership. The IRS describes the LLC simply as "an entity created by state statute" whose federal tax treatment depends on the number of members and any elections it makes ([IRS, LLC](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc)).

## Two Defining Features

**1. Limited liability.** The owners, called **members**, are generally not personally responsible for the LLC's debts and obligations. The U.S. Small Business Administration explains that LLCs "protect you from personal liability in most instances," so that personal assets such as a vehicle, house, and savings "won't be at risk in case your LLC faces bankruptcy or lawsuits" ([SBA, Choose a Business Structure](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure)). The phrase "in most instances" matters, and Chapter 8 examines the exceptions.

**2. Flexible taxation.** By default, an LLC's profits pass through to the owners' personal tax returns. An LLC may also elect to be taxed as a corporation. The SBA notes that members of an LLC are generally considered self-employed and must pay self-employment tax ([SBA](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure)).

## Comparing Business Structures

The SBA summarizes the main structures as follows (simplified; rules vary by state):

| Structure | Ownership | Personal liability | Taxation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | One person | Unlimited | Personal tax; self-employment tax |
| General partnership | Two or more | Unlimited | Personal tax; self-employment tax |
| LLC | One or more | Generally not personally liable | Personal tax or corporate tax (by election) |
| C corporation | One or more | Generally not personally liable | Corporate tax; shareholders taxed on dividends |
| S corporation | Up to 100 eligible shareholders | Generally not personally liable | Personal tax (pass-through) |

Source: adapted from [SBA, Choose a Business Structure](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure).

## Legal Classification Versus Tax Classification

A critical concept for every LLC owner is that the entity has two separate identities:

- **Legal classification** is determined by state law. Your LLC is an LLC because you filed formation documents with a state.
- **Tax classification** is determined by federal tax rules. The IRS may treat your LLC as a "disregarded entity" (taxed as part of its owner), a partnership, a C corporation, or an S corporation, depending on the number of members and elections made ([IRS, LLC](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc)).

An "S corp" is therefore not a different kind of company from an LLC; it is a tax election that an LLC (or a corporation) can make. Confusing these two layers is the source of many mistakes.

## Three Misconceptions

| Misconception | Reality |
|---|---|
| "An LLC lowers my taxes." | By default, a single-member LLC is taxed exactly like a sole proprietorship. Tax savings, if any, come from elections and planning, not from the LLC itself. |
| "An LLC protects me from everything." | It generally does not protect you from your own negligence, personal guarantees, or misuse of the entity. |
| "Once filed, I'm done." | Most states require annual reports or taxes, and failure can lead to penalties or loss of good standing. |

:::example Illustrative: Same Business, Two Structures
Jordan runs a mobile dog-grooming business with $70,000 in revenue and $25,000 in expenses, netting $45,000.

- As a sole proprietor: reports $45,000 on Schedule C; pays income tax and self-employment tax on net earnings.
- As a single-member LLC with no elections: reports the same $45,000 on the same Schedule C; tax result is the same.
- What changed: Jordan now has a separate legal entity that can sign contracts and hold a bank account, and, if properly maintained, a shield between business debts and personal assets. Jordan has also taken on state filing fees and annual compliance obligations.
:::

:::checklist Concept Check
- I can explain the two defining features of an LLC.
- I understand that legal classification and tax classification are separate.
- I know that a single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship by default.
- I understand that limited liability has exceptions.
- I know my state likely has ongoing filing requirements.
:::

:::summary
An LLC is a state-created entity offering limited liability and flexible taxation. Its legal classification comes from state law; its tax classification comes from federal rules and elections. An LLC does not automatically reduce taxes, protect against all liability, or eliminate ongoing obligations.
:::

# Chapter 2: Is an LLC Right for You?

:::objectives
- Apply a five-factor framework to decide whether to form an LLC.
- Estimate the first-year and ongoing cost of an LLC in your state.
- Recognize situations where an LLC may not be the best choice.
- Document your decision and its reasoning.
:::

Not every business needs an LLC on day one, and some businesses are better served by another structure. This chapter provides a structured decision process.

## The Five-Factor Framework

The FDIC's Money Smart for Small Business curriculum identifies five factors that make business structure important: taxation, liability and risk, management control, continuity and transferability, and expense and formality ([FDIC, Module 3](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/mssb-m3-pg.pdf)). We adapt them into a decision framework.

**1. Liability and risk.** How likely is it that the business could incur debts or claims larger than it can pay? Businesses that interact physically with customers, sign leases, employ people, or handle others' property carry more risk. The SBA notes that LLCs "can be a good choice for medium- or higher-risk businesses" and for owners with significant personal assets ([SBA](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure)).

**2. Taxation.** Will the LLC change your taxes? By default, no. If profits are substantial, an S corporation election may be worth evaluating (Chapter 6).

**3. Management control.** Will there be more than one owner? A multi-owner business benefits greatly from the written rules an LLC operating agreement provides.

**4. Continuity and transferability.** Do you expect to add owners, bring in investors, or sell the business? LLC interests can be transferred under the operating agreement. If you plan to raise venture capital, investors often prefer a C corporation; consult counsel.

**5. Expense and formality.** Can you afford the state filing fee, any annual tax or report fee, and the time to keep records separate?

## Cost Estimation

LLC costs fall into three categories: formation costs (state filing fee, optional name reservation, publication requirements in a few states), annual costs (annual report fees or franchise taxes), and operating costs (registered agent service if you use one, bookkeeping, separate bank account fees).

Some states impose significant annual taxes. California's Franchise Tax Board states that "every LLC that is doing business or organized in California must pay an annual tax of $800," which remains due "even if you are not conducting business, until you cancel your LLC," with an additional fee for LLCs with California income of $250,000 or more ([California FTB](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/business/types/limited-liability-company/)). Delaware requires every LLC formed or registered there to pay an annual tax by June 1; the Division of Corporations' current tax-instructions page lists the amount as $400, while some older Division pages still show $300, so confirm the current figure before you budget ([Delaware Division of Corporations](https://corp.delaware.gov/alt-entitytaxinstructions/)).

:::example Illustrative: Five-Year Cost Comparison
Assume a single-member service business. (Fees are illustrative; check your state.)

- State A: $100 filing fee; $50 annual report. Five-year cost: $100 + (5 x $50) = $350.
- California: $70 filing fee (illustrative) plus $800 annual tax each year. Five-year cost: $70 + (5 x $800) = $4,070.
- Plus, in either state: a commercial registered agent at, say, $125 per year adds $625 over five years if you choose not to serve as your own agent.
The difference between states can exceed $3,700 over five years, which is why the choice of state (Chapter 3) and a realistic budget matter.
:::

## When an LLC May Not Be the Best Choice

- **Very early testing.** If you are testing an idea with minimal risk and income, operating as a sole proprietor with good insurance may be reasonable until the business is validated. Weigh this against your risk exposure.
- **Venture-backed startups.** Institutional investors frequently require a C corporation.
- **Licensed professions.** Some states require doctors, lawyers, accountants, and other licensed professionals to use a professional LLC (PLLC) or professional corporation, and liability protection for professional malpractice is generally limited.
- **High-cost states with low income.** If annual taxes are a large share of expected profit, consider timing.

## Insurance Is Not Optional

An LLC protects personal assets from business debts; it does not pay claims. A business with no insurance and a valid claim against it can still lose all of its assets. General liability, professional liability, and commercial auto coverage, where relevant, complement the LLC; they do not substitute for it, and it does not substitute for them.

# Chapter 3: Choose Your State and Your Name

:::objectives
- Decide between forming in your home state and forming elsewhere.
- Explain foreign qualification and its cost implications.
- Choose a compliant LLC name and check its availability.
- Recognize trademark risk in business naming.
:::

## Home State or Elsewhere?

You may form an LLC in any state, but if you do business in another state, typically your home state, you will generally need to register there as well. That registration is called **foreign qualification**, and the LLC is then called a **foreign LLC** in that second state ("foreign" means out-of-state, not overseas). The SBA notes that LLCs and other entities will "probably need to register with any state where you conduct business activities" ([SBA, Register Your Business](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/register-your-business)).

For most small businesses that operate in one state, forming in the home state is simpler and cheaper. Forming in Delaware or another state and then qualifying at home means paying two sets of fees and filing two sets of annual obligations.

| Factor | Form in home state | Form elsewhere (e.g., Delaware) |
|---|---|---|
| Filing fees | One state | Two states (formation + foreign qualification) |
| Annual obligations | One state | Two states |
| Registered agent | Can often be yourself | Must have one in the formation state |
| Body of case law | Home state | Delaware has extensive LLC case law |
| Typical fit | Local and regional small businesses | Businesses seeking investors or with multistate complexity |

California's FTB, for example, states that the $800 annual tax applies to LLCs "doing business in California" or registered with the Secretary of State, which includes foreign LLCs registered there ([California FTB](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/business/types/limited-liability-company/)). Forming elsewhere does not avoid home-state taxes if you do business at home.

## Naming Rules

State naming rules typically require:

1. **A designator** such as "Limited Liability Company," "LLC," or "L.L.C."
2. **Distinguishability** from names already on file with the state.
3. **No restricted words** (such as "Bank," "Insurance," or "Corporation") without approval.
4. **No implication** that the LLC is a government agency.

## Availability and Trademark Clearance

A name available at the Secretary of State is not necessarily safe to use. State name availability only checks the state's business records; it does not check trademarks. Before committing to a name:

1. Search your state's business entity database.
2. Search the USPTO trademark database for identical or similar marks in related goods or services.
3. Search the web, social platforms, and domain registries.
4. Consider whether you will need a **DBA** ("doing business as," also called an assumed or fictitious name) if you will trade under a name other than the legal LLC name.

:::example Illustrative: Name Clearance
Priya plans "Brightline Bookkeeping LLC." State search: no exact match; "Bright Line Accounting LLC" exists. Her state's distinguishability rule treats spacing differences as non-distinguishing, so the name may be rejected. USPTO search: a registered "BRIGHTLINE" mark exists for financial software. Decision: choose a different name, "Ledgerwell Bookkeeping LLC," which clears state, trademark, and domain searches. Cost of the search: two hours. Cost avoided: a forced rebrand after printing, signage, and marketing.
:::

:::checklist State and Name Selection
- I know where my business will actually operate.
- I understand foreign qualification costs if I form outside my home state.
- My name includes the required designator.
- The name is distinguishable in my state's records.
- I searched the USPTO database for conflicting marks.
- I checked domain and social handle availability.
- I know whether I need a DBA.
:::

:::summary
Most single-state small businesses should form at home; forming elsewhere usually adds a second set of fees and filings through foreign qualification. Choose a name with the correct designator that is distinguishable in state records, and clear it against trademarks and domains, because state availability is not trademark clearance.
:::

# Chapter 4: The Formation Filing

:::objectives
- Identify the information typically required in articles of organization.
- Explain the role of a registered agent and the organizer.
- Choose between member-managed and manager-managed structures.
- File formation documents and verify acceptance.
:::

## Articles of Organization

The document that creates an LLC is usually called the **articles of organization** (Delaware calls it a **certificate of formation**). The SBA describes it as "a simple document that describes the basics of your LLC," including the company name, address, member names, and registered agent ([SBA, Register Your Business](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/register-your-business)). Typical fields include:

| Field | What it means | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| LLC name | Exact legal name with designator | Typos that become the legal name |
| Principal office address | Where the business is located | Using an address you cannot receive mail at |
| Registered agent | Person or company to receive legal papers | Listing someone unreliable |
| Management structure | Member-managed or manager-managed | Choosing without understanding |
| Organizer | Person who signs and files | Confusing organizer with owner |
| Effective date | When the LLC comes into existence | Not delaying to January 1 when beneficial |
| Purpose | Often "any lawful purpose" | Unnecessarily narrow purpose |

## The Registered Agent

A **registered agent** is the person or company designated to receive service of process (lawsuits) and official state notices. The agent must have a physical address in the state and be available during business hours. You may usually serve as your own agent if you have an in-state address, but consider privacy (the address becomes public), availability, and the consequences of missing a lawsuit. Delaware's Division of Corporations, for example, sends tax notices to the registered agent ([Delaware Tax FAQ](https://corp.delaware.gov/taxfaq/)), so an unreliable agent can cause missed deadlines.

## Member-Managed or Manager-Managed?

In a **member-managed** LLC, all members participate in management and can typically bind the company. In a **manager-managed** LLC, members appoint one or more managers (who may or may not be members) to run the business, and passive members do not have management authority. Most small owner-operated businesses are member-managed. Manager-managed structures suit LLCs with passive investors.

## The Filing Procedure

1. Confirm name availability immediately before filing.
2. Decide on the registered agent and obtain consent.
3. Decide management structure.
4. Choose the effective date. Some owners choose January 1 of the next year when forming late in the year to avoid a short first tax year with full annual fees; check your state's rules.
5. File online with the Secretary of State and pay the fee.
6. Save the stamped or certified copy and the filing receipt.
7. Check whether your state requires newspaper publication (a small number do) and complete it on time.

:::case Delaware's Annual Tax Notice Goes to the Agent
Delaware's Division of Corporations explains that tax notices are printed in December and sent to the registered agent, and that an LLC that fails to pay its annual tax faces a $200 penalty plus 1.5 percent monthly interest ([Delaware Tax FAQ](https://corp.delaware.gov/taxfaq/); [Delaware Division of Corporations](https://corp.delaware.gov/alt-entitytaxinstructions/)). The Delaware Division of Revenue further states that an LLC that fails to pay the annual tax for three years "shall be deemed to be cancelled" ([Delaware Division of Revenue](https://financefiles.delaware.gov/docs/lp_llc.pdf)). The practical lesson is that choosing a reliable agent, and keeping your own calendar rather than relying on notices, is part of formation, not an afterthought.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Effective-Date Timing
Marcus is ready to file on December 10 in a state with a $300 annual fee assessed for any calendar year in which the LLC exists. If he files effective December 10, he owes the fee for a year in which he operates three weeks. If his state permits a delayed effective date and he chooses January 1, he avoids one year's fee ($300), at the cost of operating as a sole proprietor for three weeks. Whether this is wise depends on his liability exposure in those weeks.
:::

:::checklist Formation Filing
- Name re-checked on the filing date.
- Registered agent chosen and consent obtained.
- Management structure decided.
- Effective date decided.
- Articles filed and fee paid.
- Stamped copy and receipt saved in the company records.
- Publication requirement checked and calendared if applicable.
:::

:::summary
Articles of organization (or a certificate of formation) create the LLC and list its name, address, registered agent, management structure, and organizer. The registered agent receives legal papers and notices and must be reliable. Most owner-operated LLCs are member-managed. Keep the stamped filing and calendar any publication requirement.
:::

# Chapter 5: The Operating Agreement

:::objectives
- Explain why an operating agreement matters even when a state does not require one.
- Identify the core provisions every operating agreement should address.
- Draft buy-sell, deadlock, and departure provisions for multi-member LLCs.
- Adapt the agreement for a single-member LLC.
:::

An **operating agreement** is the internal contract among the members that governs how the LLC is owned, managed, financed, and ended. The SBA notes that while not every state requires one, "an operating agreement is a key document that you should create because it can help protect your business's limited liability status, prevent financial misunderstandings, and ensure that your business is run the way you want" ([SBA, Register Your Business](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/register-your-business)).

## Why It Matters

Without an agreement, your state's LLC statute supplies **default rules**. Default rules may divide profits per capita instead of by contribution, require unanimous consent for decisions you would rather delegate, or leave the departure of a member unaddressed. The SBA also observes that in some states an LLC may need to be dissolved and re-formed when a member joins or leaves unless the agreement provides otherwise ([SBA](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/choose-business-structure)).

## Core Provisions

| Provision | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Members and ownership percentages | Who owns what? |
| Capital contributions | Who put in what, and must anyone contribute more later? |
| Allocations and distributions | How are profits and losses divided, and when is cash paid out? |
| Management | Who makes which decisions, and what requires a vote? |
| Voting thresholds | Which decisions need a majority, supermajority, or unanimity? |
| Compensation | Are members paid for work, and how? |
| Transfers of interest | Can a member sell or give away an interest? |
| Buy-sell (departure) | What happens on death, disability, divorce, bankruptcy, or exit? |
| Valuation | How is a departing member's interest priced? |
| Deadlock | What happens when members cannot agree? |
| Dissolution | How is the LLC wound up and assets distributed? |
| Records and accounting | Who keeps books, and what can members inspect? |

## Distinguishing Ownership, Profit Share, and Vote

Ownership percentage, profit share, and voting power can differ. A member who contributes $50,000 in cash and a member who contributes full-time labor may agree to a 50/50 ownership split but a preferred return of capital to the cash investor before profits are shared. Write this down explicitly; ambiguity here is the root of many disputes.

## Buy-Sell Provisions

A buy-sell clause specifies **triggering events** (death, disability, voluntary withdrawal, termination for cause, divorce, bankruptcy), **who may or must buy** (the company or remaining members), **the price** (a formula, an annual agreed value, or an independent appraisal), and **payment terms** (lump sum or installments). Life insurance is sometimes used to fund purchase on death.

## Deadlock Provisions

In a two-member 50/50 LLC, every disagreement is a potential deadlock. Common mechanisms include mediation followed by arbitration, a designated tie-breaker, or a "shotgun" clause in which one member names a price and the other must either buy or sell at that price.

:::example Illustrative: The Unwritten Partnership
Two friends form a 50/50 LLC without an agreement. Ana contributes $40,000; Ben contributes no cash but works full-time. After two years, Ben leaves. Questions with no written answer: Does Ana get her $40,000 back first? Is Ben owed a payout for his 50 percent? At what value? Can Ben sell his interest to a competitor? Under default state rules, the answers may surprise both. Had they adopted an agreement with a capital-return preference, a buy-sell clause valued at book value plus a multiple, and a transfer restriction, the departure would have been a calculation rather than a lawsuit.
:::

## The Single-Member Agreement

A single-member LLC also benefits from an operating agreement. It documents that the LLC is a separate entity, names a successor or provides for continuity on death or incapacity, and demonstrates formality that may help if anyone ever challenges the separateness of the company (Chapter 8).

:::checklist Operating Agreement Essentials
- Members and percentages listed.
- Initial capital contributions and any future contribution obligations specified.
- Allocation and distribution rules written, including tax distributions.
- Management structure and voting thresholds defined.
- Member compensation addressed.
- Transfer restrictions included.
- Buy-sell triggers, valuation, and payment terms defined.
- Deadlock mechanism chosen.
- Dissolution procedure written.
- Signed by all members and stored with company records.
:::

:::summary
The operating agreement replaces state default rules with terms the members choose. It should define ownership, contributions, distributions, management, voting, transfers, buy-sell, deadlock, and dissolution. Single-member LLCs benefit too, for continuity and as evidence of separateness. Have an attorney review the final document.
:::

# Chapter 6: EIN and Federal Tax Classification

:::objectives
- Obtain an Employer Identification Number directly and free from the IRS.
- Identify the default federal tax classification for your LLC.
- Explain Forms 8832 and 2553 and their timing rules.
- Evaluate the S corporation election, including the reasonable compensation requirement.
:::

## The Employer Identification Number

An **Employer Identification Number (EIN)** is a nine-digit federal tax ID. The SBA notes that most businesses need one, including any business that has employees, operates as a corporation or partnership, or files employment, excise, or alcohol, tobacco, and firearms returns ([SBA, Get Tax IDs](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/get-federal-state-tax-id-numbers)). Banks commonly require an EIN to open a business account even for a single-member LLC.

The IRS issues EINs at no cost and warns: "Beware of websites that charge for an EIN" ([IRS, Get an EIN](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number)). Apply online directly at IRS.gov after the LLC is formed, using the exact legal name.

## Default Classification

The IRS assigns a default classification based on the number of members ([IRS, LLC](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc)):

| Members | Default classification | Primary federal return |
|---|---|---|
| One | Disregarded entity (taxed with owner) | Schedule C (individual owner) |
| Two or more | Partnership | Form 1065, with K-1s to members |

IRS Publication 3402 adds that a single-member LLC treated as disregarded for income tax is still treated as a separate entity for employment tax and certain excise tax purposes ([IRS Pub. 3402](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p3402)). If your LLC has employees, it reports and pays employment taxes under its own EIN.

## Elections

An LLC may elect corporate tax treatment:

- **Form 8832, Entity Classification Election**, lets an eligible LLC elect to be taxed as a corporation. The IRS states the election can take effect no more than 75 days before or 12 months after the date the form is filed ([IRS, LLC](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc)). After changing classification, an LLC generally cannot change again for 60 months ([IRS Pub. 3402](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p3402)).
- **Form 2553, Election by a Small Business Corporation**, lets an eligible LLC elect S corporation status. An LLC filing Form 2553 is generally treated as having also elected corporate classification, so it need not file Form 8832 separately ([IRS Pub. 3402](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p3402)).

## Understanding the S Corporation Election

Under default treatment, an LLC owner who works in the business generally pays self-employment tax on net earnings. The IRS sets the combined self-employment tax rate at 15.3 percent (12.4 percent Social Security up to the annual wage base, plus 2.9 percent Medicare) ([IRS, Self-Employment Tax](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes?lang=en)).

With an S election, an owner who works in the business becomes a shareholder-employee. The IRS requires that "S corporations must pay reasonable compensation to a shareholder-employee in return for services that the employee provides to the corporation before non-wage distributions may be made" ([IRS, S Corporation Compensation](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporation-compensation-and-medical-insurance-issues)). Wages carry payroll taxes; distributions beyond reasonable wages do not carry employment taxes. The potential saving lies only in the portion above a reasonable salary, and it is offset by payroll costs, an additional business return (Form 1120-S), and in some states additional taxes.

The IRS lists factors courts consider in setting reasonable compensation, including training and experience, duties and responsibilities, time devoted, payments to non-shareholder employees, and what comparable businesses pay for similar services ([IRS, Wage Compensation for S Corporation Officers](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/wage-compensation-for-s-corporation-officers)).

:::case Watson: When a Low Salary Was Recharacterized
In David E. Watson, P.C. v. United States, an accountant's S corporation paid him a salary of $24,000 a year while distributing much larger amounts as dividends. The court agreed with the IRS that a reasonable salary for the years at issue was $91,044 and reclassified $67,044 of distributions each year as wages, making the corporation liable for payroll taxes on those amounts, and the Eighth Circuit upheld the result ([The Tax Adviser, 2011](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2011/aug/nitti-aug2011/); [The Tax Adviser, May 2011](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2011/may/clinic-may2011-story-08/)). The case is a widely cited reminder that the S election rewards documented, defensible compensation, not minimal salaries.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Evaluating the S Election
An LLC owner nets $90,000 and determines, using salary data for comparable roles, that reasonable compensation is $60,000.

- Default treatment: self-employment tax applies to roughly 92.35 percent of $90,000 = $83,115; at 15.3 percent, about $12,717.
- S election: payroll taxes of 15.3 percent on $60,000 wages = $9,180 (employer and employee shares combined).
- Gross difference: about $3,537.
- Less: additional payroll service and tax preparation costs, say $1,500, plus any state-level costs.
- Net potential difference: roughly $2,000, before considering effects on retirement contributions and the qualified business income deduction.
The numbers are illustrative only. The decision requires a tax professional's analysis of your specific facts.
:::

:::summary
Obtain your EIN free from the IRS. Single-member LLCs default to disregarded status and multi-member LLCs to partnership status. Form 8832 elects corporate treatment and Form 2553 elects S status, with timing and 60-month rules. # Chapter 7: State Taxes, Licenses, Permits, and Reporting

:::objectives
- Identify state and local tax registrations an LLC may need.
- Build an inventory of required licenses and permits.
- Understand the current status of federal beneficial ownership reporting.
- Register for employer accounts if you hire.
:::

## State Tax Registrations

Beyond federal taxes, an LLC may need state registrations for:

- **Sales and use tax**, if it sells taxable goods or services.
- **State income or franchise tax**, which some states impose on LLCs regardless of federal classification. California's annual $800 tax and gross-receipts-based fee, reported on Form 568, are examples ([California FTB](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/business/types/limited-liability-company/)).
- **Employer withholding and unemployment insurance**, if it hires employees.

The SBA notes that whether you need a state tax ID depends on whether your business must pay state taxes, and that requirements vary by state ([SBA, Get Tax IDs](https://legacy.sba.gov/business-guide/launch-your-business/get-federal-state-tax-id-numbers)).

## Licenses and Permits

Licensing exists at federal, state, county, and city levels. Building a license inventory prevents fines and forced closures.

| Level | Examples | Where to check |
|---|---|---|
| Federal | Firearms, alcohol, aviation, broadcasting, some agriculture | Relevant federal agency |
| State | Professional and occupational licenses, contractor licenses | State licensing boards |
| County/city | General business license, zoning, home occupation permit | City hall or county clerk |
| Industry | Health permits, signage, fire inspection | Local health and fire departments |

## Beneficial Ownership Reporting: Current Status

The Corporate Transparency Act originally required most small LLCs to report their beneficial owners to the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN). In March 2025, FinCEN issued an interim final rule that exempts entities created in the United States, and their beneficial owners, from beneficial ownership information (BOI) reporting; under the rule, only entities formed under foreign law and registered to do business in a U.S. state must report ([FinCEN, BOI](https://www.fincen.gov/boi); [FinCEN news release](https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/fincen-removes-beneficial-ownership-reporting-requirements-us-companies-and-us)). Because this area has changed repeatedly, check FinCEN's site at formation and annually.

:::case EIN and Filing Solicitations
In April 2025, the FTC warned consumers about official-looking letters and websites charging fees to obtain an EIN, noting that the IRS provides EINs for free ([FTC Consumer Alert](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/04/dont-pay-get-your-employer-identification-number-ein)). Delaware's Division of Corporations similarly posts consumer alerts about tax, annual report, and corporate records service scams ([Delaware Division of Corporations](https://corp.delaware.gov/frtax/)). New LLC filings are public, so owners should expect solicitations and verify any request against the official government source before paying.
:::

:::example Illustrative: License Inventory for a Home Bakery LLC
- State: cottage food registration (if the state has a cottage food law) and sales tax permit.
- County: food handler certification.
- City: home occupation permit and general business license.
- Federal: none for direct sales of permitted cottage foods.
- Total registrations identified: five. Estimated first-year fees: obtained from each agency's posted schedule.
:::

:::checklist Registrations and Licenses
- Determined whether I must collect sales tax and registered if so.
- Identified state income, franchise, or gross receipts taxes on LLCs.
- Checked city and county business license and zoning requirements.
- Checked state occupational licensing.
- Checked federal licensing for my industry.
- Registered for employer accounts before first payroll, if hiring.
- Checked current FinCEN BOI status.
:::

:::summary
An LLC may need state registrations for sales tax, state entity taxes, and employer accounts, plus licenses at multiple levels of government. As of FinCEN's March 2025 interim final rule, U.S.-formed LLCs are exempt from BOI reporting, but the rules have changed before. Expect solicitations and verify them with official sources.

# Chapter 8: Protecting the Liability Shield

:::objectives
- Define veil piercing and the alter-ego doctrine.
- Identify the factors courts examine when deciding whether to pierce an LLC's veil.
- Implement practices that keep business and personal affairs separate.
- Recognize liabilities an LLC cannot shield.
:::

Limited liability is not absolute. Courts may disregard the LLC and hold members personally liable under the doctrine of **piercing the veil**, typically when the entity was the owner's **alter ego** and treating it as separate would be unjust.

## What Courts Look For

Wolters Kluwer's overview of veil piercing notes that courts examine factors such as undercapitalization, commingling of funds, failure to observe formalities, and use of the entity to commit fraud, and observes that single-member LLCs may face greater scrutiny ([Wolters Kluwer](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/expert-insights/piercing-the-veil-of-small-business-what-the-owners-of-llcs-and-corporations-need-to-know)).

:::case Martin v. Freeman (Colorado Court of Appeals, 2012)
In Martin v. Freeman, a creditor obtained a judgment against Tradewinds, a single-member Delaware LLC, and sought to hold its sole member personally liable. The court affirmed piercing the veil, citing commingling of funds, negligible record keeping, thin capitalization, and the member's deposit of proceeds from the sale of an LLC airplane into his personal account. The court applied alter-ego factors including whether the entity was operated as a distinct business, whether funds were commingled, whether adequate records were kept, whether the entity was thinly capitalized, and whether assets were diverted to the owner ([Martin v. Freeman, Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/colorado/court-of-appeals/2012/11ca0145.html)). The lesson for owners is that the shield depends on conduct, not paperwork alone.
:::

## The Separateness Framework

| Factor courts examine | Practice that addresses it |
|---|---|
| Commingling funds | Separate business bank account and card; no personal expenses from business funds |
| Inadequate records | Bookkeeping system; written resolutions for major decisions |
| Undercapitalization | Capitalize for foreseeable obligations; carry insurance |
| Diverting assets | Documented distributions; written loans with terms |
| Not holding out as separate | LLC name on contracts, invoices, website, signage |
| Ignoring formalities | Keep operating agreement current; file annual reports |

## Signing Correctly

Sign contracts in a representative capacity: "Ledgerwell Bookkeeping LLC, by Priya Shah, Managing Member." Signing only your name may make you a party personally.

## Liabilities an LLC Does Not Shield

- Your own negligent or wrongful acts (you remain liable for torts you personally commit).
- Personal guarantees, which lenders and landlords commonly require of small business owners.
- Certain unpaid payroll taxes; responsible persons can be held personally liable for trust fund taxes.
- Professional malpractice by licensed professionals, generally.

:::example Illustrative: Recording a Member Draw
Priya wants $3,000 from her single-member LLC. Wrong: paying her personal credit card bill directly from the business account. Right: transfer $3,000 from the business account to her personal account, record it as an owner's draw in the books, and then pay personal bills from the personal account. The money ends up in the same place, but the record shows a separate entity making a documented distribution.
:::

:::checklist Separateness Practices
- Business bank account opened in the LLC's name with the EIN.
- No personal expenses paid from business accounts.
- Owner draws and contributions recorded.
- Loans between member and LLC documented in writing.
- Contracts signed in representative capacity.
- LLC name used on all public materials.
- Adequate insurance in force.
- Major decisions documented in written resolutions.
:::

:::summary
Courts may pierce an LLC's veil when the company is the owner's alter ego, examining commingling, records, capitalization, and asset diversion, as in Martin v. Freeman. Separate accounts, documented draws, representative signatures, adequate capital and insurance, and written resolutions preserve the shield, which never covers personal torts or guarantees.
:::

# Chapter 9: Money, Bookkeeping, and Records

:::objectives
- Capitalize the LLC appropriately and document contributions.
- Open and operate a business bank account.
- Set up a bookkeeping system and a monthly close routine.
- Maintain a company records binder and follow record-retention guidance.
:::

## Capitalization

**Capitalization** is the money and property the members put into the LLC so it can meet its foreseeable obligations. There is no universal minimum, but Chapter 8 showed that thin capitalization is a recurring factor in veil-piercing cases. Wolters Kluwer's overview describes an undercapitalized agricultural LLC whose owners were held personally liable, illustrating that courts look at whether the business had resources reasonably proportionate to its risks ([Wolters Kluwer](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/expert-insights/piercing-the-veil-of-small-business-what-the-owners-of-llcs-and-corporations-need-to-know)). Document each contribution: date, member, amount or property, and agreed value.

## Contribution or Loan?

Money a member puts in can be an equity contribution or a loan. The distinction affects taxes, repayment priority, and dispute outcomes. If it is a loan, write a promissory note with an interest rate, repayment schedule, and signatures from both the member and the LLC.

## The Business Bank Account

Open the account after formation using the stamped articles, EIN confirmation letter, and, where required, the operating agreement or a banking resolution. The FDIC's Money Smart for Small Business curriculum emphasizes keeping business and personal finances separate as a foundation for financial management ([FDIC, Module 3](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/mssb-m3-pg.pdf)). Route all business income into the account and pay all business expenses from it.

## A Bookkeeping System

| Component | Purpose | Minimum practice |
|---|---|---|
| Chart of accounts | Categories for income and expenses | Align with your tax return lines |
| Accounting software or ledger | Record every transaction | Connect bank feeds; categorize weekly |
| Receipts and invoices | Support deductions | Capture digitally at time of purchase |
| Monthly close | Catch errors early | Reconcile bank and card statements monthly |
| Owner equity accounts | Track contributions and draws | Separate accounts per member |

## Record Retention

The IRS explains that how long you should keep a record depends on the action, expense, or event it documents, and generally recommends keeping records supporting a return for at least three years, with longer periods in some situations, such as employment tax records for at least four years ([IRS, How Long Should I Keep Records](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-long-should-i-keep-records)). Formation documents, the operating agreement, amendments, and ownership records should be kept permanently.

## The Company Records Binder

Keep a physical or digital binder containing:

1. Stamped articles of organization and any amendments.
2. Signed operating agreement and amendments.
3. EIN confirmation letter (IRS CP 575 or equivalent).
4. Tax elections filed (Forms 8832 or 2553) and IRS acceptance letters.
5. Member list with ownership percentages and contribution history.
6. Written resolutions and meeting minutes.
7. Licenses and permits with renewal dates.
8. Annual report confirmations.
9. Insurance policies.
10. Loan documents between members and the LLC.

:::example Illustrative: The Monthly Close in Forty-Five Minutes
On the fifth business day of each month, Jordan (the dog groomer from Chapter 1):

- Downloads the business bank and card statements (5 minutes).
- Confirms every transaction is categorized in the software (15 minutes).
- Reconciles balances to statements (10 minutes).
- Records any owner draws and contributions (5 minutes).
- Reviews the profit and loss statement and sets aside an estimated tax amount, for example 25 percent of net profit, into a separate savings account (10 minutes).
The routine takes about 45 minutes and creates the records that both the IRS and a court would expect to see.
:::

:::checklist Financial Foundations
- Initial capital contributions documented.
- Member loans documented with promissory notes.
- Business bank account and card opened in the LLC's name.
- Bookkeeping software set up with bank feeds.
- Monthly close scheduled.
- Estimated-tax savings habit established.
- Records binder assembled.
- Retention periods noted.
:::

:::summary
Capitalize the LLC for foreseeable risks and document contributions and loans. Operate exclusively through a business bank account, keep books with a monthly close, follow IRS record-retention guidance, and maintain a permanent records binder.
:::

# Chapter 10: Keeping the LLC in Good Standing

:::objectives
- Define good standing and explain its practical importance.
- Build an annual compliance calendar for your LLC.
- Understand annual reports, entity taxes, and registered agent maintenance.
- Recover from administrative dissolution or lapsed status.
:::

**Good standing** means the state's records show the LLC is current on required filings and fees. Losing it has real consequences. Wolters Kluwer notes that a Delaware LLC that does not pay its annual tax by June 1 is no longer in good standing, so the Division will not file documents on its behalf or issue a certificate of good standing, which may be needed to expand into other states, get a loan, or enter new ventures, and the entity cannot bring a lawsuit in a Delaware court until restored ([Wolters Kluwer, Delaware Annual Tax](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/expert-insights/delaware-annual-tax-is-due-june-1)).

## Typical Recurring Obligations

| Obligation | Frequency | Typical authority | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual or biennial report | Annual or every two years | Secretary of State | Updates addresses, managers, agent |
| Entity tax or fee | Annual | State revenue agency | California $800 tax; Delaware annual tax due June 1 |
| Federal income tax return | Annual | IRS | Schedule C, Form 1065, 1120, or 1120-S |
| Estimated taxes | Quarterly | IRS and state | Form 1040-ES for owners |
| Sales tax returns | Monthly, quarterly, or annual | State revenue agency | Based on volume |
| Payroll filings | Per pay period, quarterly, annual | IRS and state | Forms 941, W-2 |
| License renewals | Varies | Licensing agency | City business license |
| Registered agent | Ongoing | Agent | Update if agent changes |
| BOI status check | Annual | FinCEN | Confirm current rule |

## Estimated Taxes

Because LLC profits typically flow to owners' personal returns, owners generally need to make quarterly estimated tax payments. The IRS states that you generally must make estimated payments if you expect to owe at least $1,000 in tax after withholding and credits, and describes safe-harbor percentages based on current- or prior-year tax ([IRS, Estimated Tax](https://www.irs.gov/faqs/estimated-tax)).

## California as a Worked Example

California's FTB explains that the $800 annual tax is due by the 15th day of the 4th month after the beginning of the LLC's tax year, that an additional LLC fee applies when total California income is $250,000 or more (beginning at $900 and rising to $11,790 for income of $5 million or more), and that LLCs file Form 568 ([California FTB](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/business/types/limited-liability-company/)). The FTB also notes the first-year annual tax exemption applied only to LLCs formed between 2021 and 2023 ([FTB Form 3556 guidance](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/forms/misc/3556.html)). An LLC formed in California in 2026 should therefore budget for the tax in its first year.

## Recovering From Lapsed Status

If your LLC falls out of good standing or is administratively dissolved, most states offer **reinstatement**: file overdue reports, pay back fees and penalties, and sometimes file a reinstatement application. Act quickly; in the interim, your name may become available to others and your liability protection may be questioned.

:::example Illustrative: Building the Calendar
A California single-member LLC with a calendar tax year:

- January 15: Q4 federal and state estimated tax.
- April 15: $800 annual tax for the current year (by the 15th day of the 4th month); Q1 estimates; prior-year individual return including Schedule C and Form 568.
- June 15: Q2 estimates.
- September 15: Q3 estimates.
- Every two years (California): Statement of Information.
- Monthly: bookkeeping close.
- Annually: insurance review, license renewals, BOI status check.
(Confirm exact dates each year; due dates shift for weekends and holidays.)
:::

:::checklist Good Standing Habits
- Compliance calendar built with all recurring obligations.
- Reminders set 30 days before each deadline.
- State business search checked annually to confirm active status.
- Registered agent details current.
- Estimated tax payments scheduled.
- Certificate of good standing obtained when needed for loans or expansion.
:::

:::summary
Good standing requires timely annual reports, entity taxes, and agent maintenance; losing it blocks filings, certificates, and in some states access to courts. Build a calendar covering state, federal, payroll, sales tax, and license obligations, including quarterly estimated taxes, and reinstate promptly if status lapses.
:::

# Chapter 11: Growth, Change, and Ending the LLC

:::objectives
- Add or remove members using the operating agreement.
- Expand into other states through foreign qualification.
- Understand tax consequences of changing member count.
- Dissolve and wind up an LLC properly.
:::

## Adding a Member

Adding a member changes ownership, management, and possibly tax classification. A single-member LLC that adds a second member generally becomes a partnership for federal tax purposes by default and must file partnership returns going forward ([IRS, LLC](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/limited-liability-company-llc)). Steps:

1. Confirm the operating agreement's admission procedure.
2. Agree on the new member's contribution and percentage.
3. Amend the operating agreement and member schedule.
4. Determine whether a new EIN is required (the IRS provides guidance on when entity changes require a new number).
5. Update the bank's signature authority and any state filings that list members or managers.
6. Consult a tax professional about the tax effects of the change.

## Removing a Member

Follow the buy-sell provisions: identify the triggering event, apply the valuation method, execute an assignment of interest, pay according to the agreed terms, and amend the agreement. Without written provisions, departures can be contentious and expensive.

## Expanding Into Other States

When the LLC begins "doing business" in another state, it generally must register as a foreign LLC there, appoint a registered agent in that state, and meet that state's annual obligations. What counts as doing business varies; having employees, offices, or inventory in a state commonly qualifies. Check each state's statute and revenue rules, since tax nexus and qualification thresholds differ.

## Dissolution and Winding Up

Ending an LLC is a process, not a single filing.

1. **Approve dissolution** as required by the operating agreement.
2. **Wind up**: complete or assign contracts, collect receivables, sell assets, and notify creditors.
3. **Pay liabilities** in the order required by law, including taxes.
4. **Distribute remaining assets** to members per the agreement.
5. **File final tax returns**, checking the "final return" box where applicable.
6. **File articles of dissolution or cancellation** with the state.
7. **Close accounts, licenses, and registrations**, including foreign qualifications and sales tax permits.

Until the state cancellation is filed, recurring obligations may continue. California's FTB, for instance, says the $800 annual tax is owed until you cancel the LLC ([California FTB](https://www.ftb.ca.gov/file/business/types/limited-liability-company/)).

:::case Pat Flynn and the Value of Clearing a Name
Entrepreneur Pat Flynn has described receiving a cease-and-desist letter over the use of the trademarked term "LEED" in an early business name, and changing course as a result ([Lizerbram Law](https://www.lizerbramlaw.com/blog/trademark-case-study-pat-flynn-of-smart-passive-income/)). Although the case concerns trademarks rather than LLC law, it illustrates why any change, whether a rebrand, a new product line, or expansion into a new state, should trigger the name and trademark clearance process from Chapter 3. His results and experiences are his own, not TTI's.
:::

:::example Illustrative: The Cost of Not Cancelling
A California LLC stops operating in March 2026 but the owner never files cancellation. The $800 annual tax continues to accrue each year, plus penalties for unfiled returns. After three years, the owner faces at least $2,400 in annual taxes alone, plus penalties and interest. Filing the final return and cancellation in 2026 would have stopped future annual taxes.
:::

:::checklist Change and Exit
- Admission procedure followed and agreement amended for new members.
- Tax classification effects reviewed with a professional.
- Bank signatories and state filings updated.
- Foreign qualification completed before doing business in a new state.
- Dissolution approved and winding up completed.
- Final tax returns filed.
- Articles of dissolution or cancellation filed.
- Licenses, permits, and accounts closed.
:::

:::summary
Adding a member to a single-member LLC generally changes its default tax classification to partnership. Remove members using buy-sell terms. Register as a foreign LLC before doing business in new states. Dissolve properly by winding up, paying liabilities, distributing assets, filing final returns, and filing cancellation, or annual obligations may continue.
:::

# Chapter 12: Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

:::objectives
- Recognize the most frequent and costly LLC mistakes.
- Diagnose your own LLC against a mistake inventory.
- Prioritize corrective actions by risk.
- Know when to bring in an attorney or tax professional.
:::

The preceding chapters each described correct procedure. This chapter inverts the view: it catalogs the errors that most often undermine LLCs, organized by the stage at which they occur, so that you can audit your own company.

## Mistakes at Formation

**Forming in the wrong state.** Forming out of state to save money, only to pay foreign qualification and two sets of annual fees at home (Chapter 3).

**Paying for what is free.** The IRS issues EINs at no cost and warns against websites that charge for them ([IRS, Get an EIN](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number)). The FTC has warned specifically about letters and sites charging for EINs ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/04/dont-pay-get-your-employer-identification-number-ein)). Formation services can save time, but understand exactly which fees are government fees and which are optional services.

**Skipping the operating agreement.** Leaving default rules in place, especially in multi-member LLCs (Chapter 5).

**Skipping name clearance.** Using a name that conflicts with a registered trademark (Chapter 3).

## Mistakes in Operation

**Commingling.** Using one account for personal and business, which was central to the court's decision in Martin v. Freeman ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/colorado/court-of-appeals/2012/11ca0145.html)).

**Signing personally.** Signing contracts without indicating representative capacity.

**Undercapitalizing and underinsuring.** Operating with too little money and no insurance relative to risk.

**Making the S election without reasonable compensation.** Taking a minimal salary and large distributions, the pattern rejected in the Watson case ([The Tax Adviser](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2011/aug/nitti-aug2011/)).

**Ignoring estimated taxes.** Failing to make quarterly payments and facing penalties at filing time ([IRS, Estimated Tax](https://www.irs.gov/faqs/estimated-tax)).

## Mistakes in Maintenance

**Missing annual filings.** Losing good standing or being administratively dissolved (Chapter 10).

**Letting the registered agent lapse.** Missing a lawsuit or state notice.

**Responding to look-alike solicitations.** Paying private companies that mimic government notices; Delaware posts consumer alerts on such scams ([Delaware Division of Corporations](https://corp.delaware.gov/frtax/)).

## Mistakes at Change and Exit

**Adding members informally.** Admitting a partner on a handshake without amending the agreement or addressing the tax change.

**Abandoning instead of dissolving.** Walking away while annual taxes continue to accrue (Chapter 11).

## The Mistake Inventory

| Mistake | Risk level | Fix | Who helps |
|---|---|---|---|
| No operating agreement (multi-member) | High | Draft and sign agreement | Attorney |
| Commingled accounts | High | Open separate account; reclassify past transactions | Bookkeeper |
| No insurance | High | Obtain appropriate coverage | Insurance agent |
| S election with low salary | High | Set documented reasonable compensation | CPA |
| Missed annual report | Medium–High | File and reinstate if needed | Secretary of State |
| Personal signatures on contracts | Medium | Re-sign or amend in LLC name going forward | Attorney |
| No estimated tax payments | Medium | Calculate and pay current quarter | CPA |
| Unclear member loans | Medium | Document with promissory notes | Attorney |
| No records binder | Low–Medium | Assemble documents | Owner |
| Unchecked trademark | Medium | Run clearance; rebrand if necessary | Trademark attorney |

Sam's two-member LLC runs the inventory and finds: no operating agreement (high), a shared account used for one personal purchase per month (high), and an annual report filed late last year (medium). Sam ranks the fixes: (1) open a clean account and stop personal use this week; (2) engage an attorney to draft the agreement within 30 days; (3) add annual report reminders to the calendar today. Cost estimate for the attorney: obtained by quote. The point is not perfection but a prioritized, documented correction.

# Appendix B: Operating Agreement Outline and Drafting Questions

Use this outline to prepare for a meeting with your attorney. Answer each question in writing.

**Article 1, Formation.** Name, state, principal office, registered agent, purpose, term.

**Article 2, Members.** Who are the members? What are their percentages? How are new members admitted?

**Article 3, Capital.** What did each member contribute and at what value? Are additional contributions required or optional? What happens if a member fails to contribute? Are member loans permitted?

**Article 4, Allocations and Distributions.** How are profits and losses allocated? Is there a preferred return of capital? Will the LLC make tax distributions so members can pay taxes on allocated income? Who decides on other distributions?

**Article 5, Management.** Member-managed or manager-managed? What decisions require a majority, a supermajority, or unanimity (for example, selling the business, taking on debt above a threshold, admitting members, amending the agreement)?

**Article 6, Compensation and Duties.** Are members paid for services? What time commitment is expected? Are members restricted from competing?

**Article 7, Transfers.** May members transfer interests? Is there a right of first refusal?

**Article 8, Buy-Sell.** What events trigger a buyout? How is value determined? What are payment terms? Is insurance used to fund it?

**Article 9, Deadlock and Disputes.** What happens if members cannot agree? Mediation, arbitration, tie-breaker, or buy-sell mechanism?

**Article 10, Books and Records.** Who keeps the books? What tax year and accounting method? What records may members inspect?

**Article 11, Dissolution.** What events cause dissolution? Who winds up? In what order are assets distributed?

**Article 12, General Provisions.** Governing law, amendments, notices, severability, signatures.

# Glossary

**Administrative dissolution.** Termination of an entity by the state for failure to file reports or pay fees.

**Alter ego.** A doctrine under which a court treats an entity as indistinguishable from its owner, permitting veil piercing.

**Articles of organization.** The document filed with a state to form an LLC; called a certificate of formation in some states.

**Beneficial ownership information (BOI).** Information about individuals who own or control an entity, subject to FinCEN reporting rules that have changed over time.

**Buy-sell provision.** Operating agreement terms governing the purchase of a member's interest on specified events.

**Capitalization.** Money and property contributed to the LLC to fund operations and obligations.

**Commingling.** Mixing personal and business funds.

**DBA.** "Doing business as"; a registered trade name different from the legal name.

**Disregarded entity.** A single-member LLC treated as part of its owner for federal income tax purposes.

**EIN.** Employer Identification Number; a nine-digit federal tax ID issued free by the IRS.

**Foreign LLC.** An LLC registered to do business in a state other than the one where it was formed.

**Good standing.** Status indicating an entity is current on state filings and fees.

**Manager-managed.** An LLC in which designated managers, rather than all members, run the business.

**Member.** An owner of an LLC.

**Member-managed.** An LLC in which all members participate in management.

**Operating agreement.** The internal contract among members governing the LLC.

**Organizer.** The person who signs and files the articles of organization.

**Reasonable compensation.** The wage an S corporation must pay a shareholder-employee for services before non-wage distributions.

**Registered agent.** The person or company designated to receive legal process and official notices.

**Reinstatement.** Restoring an administratively dissolved entity to active status.

**S corporation election.** A federal tax election (Form 2553) allowing pass-through taxation with shareholder-employee wages.

**Veil piercing.** A court's disregard of an entity's separate existence to hold owners personally liable.

**Winding up.** The process of concluding an LLC's affairs before cancellation.
