# How to Use This Book

Most small business owners do not set out to become employers. They start because they are good at a craft, a trade, or a service, and they hire when the work exceeds the hours they have. The first hire is therefore often reactive: a friend's referral, a vague job post, a short conversation, and a hope that the new person will "figure it out." When the hire fails, the owner concludes that good help is impossible to find and returns to doing everything personally.

This book treats hiring as a design problem with learnable methods. It draws on federal guidance from the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), the Office of Personnel Management (OPM), the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), and U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), and on practitioner frameworks, notably Dan Martell's Buy Back Your Time, which offers owner-operators a disciplined way to decide what to hand off first.

## Who This Book Is For

- Owners preparing to make a first or second hire.
- Owners whose past hires did not work out and who want a better process.
- Managers in small companies responsible for hiring and training.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Identify the work you should hand off and estimate what you can afford to pay.
- Define a role by outcomes and calculate its true cost.
- Write a job post that is fair, lawful, and attractive to qualified candidates.
- Run structured interviews and paid work samples that predict performance.
- Complete required new-hire paperwork on time.
- Prepare a first week that sets new people up to succeed.
- Teach work with documented playbooks and delegate without micromanaging.
- Give feedback, run reviews, and handle performance problems fairly.

## How Each Chapter Is Built

Each chapter begins with learning objectives, defines key terms, presents a step-by-step framework, and works through an illustrative example (a hypothetical scenario to show the method). Case studies summarize documented public examples with citations. Each chapter ends with a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions.

:::warning Disclaimer
This book is educational and is not legal, tax, or human resources advice. Employment law varies by state and locality and changes frequently; consult an employment attorney and a tax professional for decisions specific to your business. Case studies describe the documented experiences of named people and companies; their results are their own, not TTI's, and are not typical or guaranteed.
:::

# Chapter 1: Hiring as a Design Problem

:::objectives
- Explain why reactive hiring often fails in small businesses.
- Describe the Buyback Principle and the Buyback Loop.
- Identify the stages of a deliberate hiring system.
- Recognize the owner's role as system designer.
:::

## Why Reactive Hiring Fails

Reactive hiring happens under pressure: the owner is overwhelmed, a customer complaint arrives, or a key person quits. Under pressure, owners skip defining the role, interview informally, and hire whoever seems likable. OPM notes that research "consistently indicates unstructured interviews have little value in predicting job performance" ([OPM, Structured Interview Guide](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/structured-interviews/guide/)). Reactive hiring typically uses exactly those unstructured methods.

## The Buyback Principle

Dan Martell's Buy Back Your Time reframes why an owner hires. His central line is "Don't hire to grow your business, hire to buy back your time," with the freed time then filled with work that energizes the owner and produces more value ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). Martell argues that founders hit a "pain line," which he places at about 12 direct reports and just over a million dollars in revenue, and then tend to sell, sabotage, or stall ([Deven Davis](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)).

His remedy is the **Buyback Loop**: audit your time to find low-value, draining tasks; transfer them to someone else; and fill the freed time with higher-value work, then repeat ([Deven Davis](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)).

:::case Martell's Own Pain Line
Martell describes building his third company, Spheric Technologies, while working 15 to 18 hours a day; the company grew rapidly, but months before his wedding his fiancée left her engagement ring on the counter. He sold Spheric in 2008 and later built and sold Flowtown and Clarity before founding SaaS Academy ([Deven Davis](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time); [Eckfeldt review](https://www.eckfeldt.com/book-reviews-dan-martell-buy-back-your-time)). He presents his later approach to hiring as a correction to the pattern of absorbing all work personally. His experiences and results are his own, not TTI's.
:::

## The Owner as System Designer

In a deliberate system, the owner's job shifts from doing the work to designing how the work is done and who does it. This requires writing things down: what good work looks like, how decisions are made, and how success is measured. Those documents become the job description, interview questions, training materials, and review criteria, so the effort compounds.

:::example Illustrative: Reactive Versus Deliberate
Reactive: A cleaning-company owner, overwhelmed in spring, hires a friend's cousin after a 20-minute chat. No written role, no training plan. Within a month, quality complaints rise and the owner redoes jobs personally.
Deliberate: The owner audits two weeks of time, finds 12 hours a week spent on scheduling and customer calls, defines an "office coordinator" role with three measurable outcomes, posts a fair job ad, runs structured interviews and a paid two-hour scheduling exercise, and prepares a written first-week plan. The new hire has clear standards from day one.
:::

:::

A deliberate system moves through identification, definition, attraction, selection, hiring, onboarding, teaching, management, and retention, with the owner acting as designer.

# Chapter 2: Find the Work to Hand Off

:::objectives
- Conduct a two-week time and energy audit.
- Sort tasks using the DRIP Matrix.
- Calculate your Buyback Rate.
- Choose your first hire using the Replacement Ladder.
:::

## The Time and Energy Audit

For two weeks, record what you do in 15- or 30-minute blocks. For each block, note the task, its approximate value to the business, and whether it gave you energy or drained it. The audit's value lies in its honesty; most owners discover that a significant share of their week goes to tasks someone else could do.

## The DRIP Matrix

Martell sorts tasks on two axes, money produced and energy, creating four quadrants ([Deven Davis](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)):

| Quadrant | Money | Energy | Examples | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Delegation | Low | Drains | Admin, billing, travel, email | Transfer as quickly as possible |
| Replacement | High | Drains | Selling, marketing, onboarding, managing | Build systems, then replace yourself |
| Investment | Low (now) | Energizes | Health, family, learning | Protect time for it |
| Production | High | Energizes | Your highest-value work | Spend as much time here as possible |

Martell calls Replacement the "pitfall quadrant" because founders get stuck doing work that pays but drains them ([Deven Davis](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)).

## The Buyback Rate

To answer "I can't afford help," Martell proposes the **Buyback Rate**: divide what your business pays you annually by 2,000 hours, then divide by four ([Deven Davis](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). His example: an owner earning $200,000 a year earns $100 an hour, so her Buyback Rate is $25 an hour. The intent is that every hour you pay someone else should free an hour of your time worth roughly four times as much.

This is a heuristic, not a law. It assumes you will actually redirect freed time to higher-value work, and it ignores employer costs (Chapter 3). Use it to identify candidate tasks, then verify affordability with a full cost calculation.

## The Replacement Ladder

Martell sequences hires in five rungs: administrative assistant, delivery or operations, marketing, sales, and leadership. Bruce Eckfeldt's review summarizes the book's insistence that order matters and that owners should "always start with an executive assistant," because unresolved inbox and calendar chaos undermines every later hire ([Eckfeldt](https://www.eckfeldt.com/book-reviews-dan-martell-buy-back-your-time)).

For a small service business, the ladder often translates into: (1) office coordinator or virtual assistant; (2) a technician or service provider who delivers the core work; (3) marketing help; (4) sales or estimating; (5) a manager or general manager.

:::example Illustrative: Audit Results for a Bookkeeping Practice
Owner earns $120,000. Buyback Rate: $120,000 / 2,000 = $60 per hour; / 4 = $15 per hour.
Two-week audit (90 hours total):

- Email and scheduling: 14 hours (Delegation).
- Data entry for clients: 22 hours (Delegation/Replacement).
- Client advisory calls: 18 hours (Production).
- Sales calls with prospects: 8 hours (Replacement).
- Invoicing and collections: 6 hours (Delegation).
- Learning and industry reading: 4 hours (Investment).
- Other admin: 18 hours.
Finding: about 60 of 90 hours are Delegation tasks that could be done by someone paid near or modestly above the Buyback Rate, with full employer costs to be calculated next. First hire: part-time administrative assistant for email, scheduling, invoicing, and collections, followed by a data-entry bookkeeper.
:::

# Chapter 3: Define the Role and Its True Cost

:::objectives
- Write an outcome-based role scorecard.
- Determine whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor.
- Calculate the fully loaded cost of an employee.
- Decide between part-time, full-time, and contractor arrangements.
:::

## The Role Scorecard

A **role scorecard** defines a position by the outcomes it owns rather than by a list of duties. It includes:

1. **Mission:** one sentence explaining why the role exists.
2. **Outcomes:** three to five measurable results the role is accountable for.
3. **Competencies:** the skills and behaviors needed to achieve the outcomes.
4. **Working conditions:** schedule, location, tools, physical requirements.

Outcomes make every later step easier: they become interview questions, work samples, training goals, and review criteria.

## Employee or Independent Contractor?

Classification is a legal question, not a preference. The IRS explains that under common-law rules, the facts that indicate the degree of control and independence fall into three categories: **behavioral** (does the company control or have the right to control what the worker does and how?), **financial** (are the business aspects of the job controlled by the payer?), and **type of relationship** (are there written contracts, benefits, an expectation of continuation, and is the work a key aspect of the business?) ([IRS, Independent Contractor or Employee](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee)). The Department of Labor and states apply their own tests for wage and hour and unemployment purposes, and some states use stricter standards. When in doubt, consult a professional; misclassification can lead to back taxes and penalties.

| Indicator | Suggests employee | Suggests contractor |
|---|---|---|
| Instructions | You set hours, methods, sequence | Worker decides how to achieve result |
| Training | You train in your methods | Worker brings own methods |
| Tools and expenses | You provide; you reimburse | Worker provides; bears expenses |
| Opportunity for profit/loss | None | Can profit or lose |
| Services to others | Works only for you | Offers services to the public |
| Relationship | Ongoing, indefinite | Project-based, defined |
| Core business | Work is central to your business | Work is peripheral |

## The True Cost of an Employee

An employee's cost exceeds the wage. The IRS sets the Social Security tax at 6.2 percent for the employer and 6.2 percent for the employee, and Medicare at 1.45 percent each ([IRS Topic 751](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc751)). Federal unemployment tax (FUTA) is 6.0 percent on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages, reduced to 0.6 percent for employers entitled to the maximum 5.4 percent state credit ([IRS Topic 759](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc759)). Add state unemployment tax, workers' compensation insurance, any benefits, paid time off, equipment, software, payroll processing, and the owner's time spent training.

:::example Illustrative: Fully Loaded Cost
Part-time office coordinator: 25 hours per week at $20 per hour, 50 weeks.

- Wages: 25 x $20 x 50 = $25,000.
- Employer Social Security and Medicare (7.65%): $1,913.
- FUTA (0.6% x $7,000): $42.
- State unemployment (illustrative 2.7% on $9,000 wage base): $243.
- Workers' compensation (illustrative rate for clerical, 0.3%): $75.
- Payroll service: $600. Software seat: $360. Equipment: $800 (first year).
- Owner training time: 30 hours valued at the owner's $60/hour = $1,800 (opportunity cost).
Total first-year cost: about $30,833, or roughly $24.67 per hour worked, compared with the $20 wage. Rates for state unemployment and workers' compensation vary by state and industry; obtain your actual rates.
:::

## Choosing the Arrangement

| Arrangement | Best when | Cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Part-time employee | Steady but limited workload; you direct the work | Payroll setup; classification correct |
| Full-time employee | Consistent full workload; core role | Higher fixed cost; benefits expectations |
| Independent contractor | Defined projects; specialist skills; worker controls methods | Must genuinely meet contractor tests |
| Agency or staffing firm | Short-term or seasonal; you want the agency to handle payroll | Higher hourly cost |

# Chapter 4: Write a Fair, Effective Job Post

:::objectives
- Structure a job post that attracts qualified candidates.
- Avoid language that signals unlawful preferences.
- Choose recruiting channels that widen the applicant pool.
- Set up a simple application process that screens for fit.
:::

## Anatomy of an Effective Post

A job post is a sales document addressed to the candidates you want. It should answer the questions a qualified candidate will ask: What will I do? How will success be measured? What will I be paid? What is the schedule? Who will I work with? What happens next?

| Section | Contents | Source in your materials |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | Plain role title, location, schedule | Scorecard |
| About the business | Two or three sentences on what you do and for whom | Your own words |
| Mission of the role | One sentence | Scorecard mission |
| What you will own | Three to five outcomes | Scorecard outcomes |
| What you bring | Required and preferred competencies | Scorecard competencies |
| Pay and schedule | Wage range, hours, benefits | Cost calculation |
| How to apply | Steps, timeline, what to submit | Hiring plan |
| Equal opportunity statement | Lawful inclusive language | EEOC guidance |

Posting a pay range saves time for everyone and is legally required in a growing number of states and cities. Check your jurisdiction.

## Language That Creates Legal Risk

The EEOC states that it is illegal for an employer to publish a job advertisement that shows a preference for or discourages someone from applying because of race, color, religion, sex (including transgender status, sexual orientation, and pregnancy), national origin, age (40 or older), disability, or genetic information. Its examples: an ad for "females" or "recent college graduates" may discourage men and people over 40 from applying ([EEOC, Prohibited Employment Policies/Practices](https://www.eeoc.gov/prohibited-employment-policiespractices)).

| Risky phrasing | Why | Better phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| "Young, energetic team" | Signals age preference | "Fast-paced team that values initiative" |
| "Recent graduates" | Discourages workers 40+ | "Entry-level; training provided" |
| "Native English speaker" | National origin | "Fluent written and spoken English required for customer communication" |
| "Salesman" / "Waitress" | Sex preference | "Salesperson" / "Server" |
| "Must be able-bodied" | Disability | "Must be able to lift 40 pounds with or without reasonable accommodation" (if essential) |
| "Digital native" | Age | "Comfortable learning new software tools" |

The EEOC suggests that an employer can lawfully encourage diverse applicants with language such as "Men and women, and members of all racial and ethnic groups, are encouraged to apply" ([EEOC](https://www.eeoc.gov/es/node/130418)).

## Recruiting Channels

Word-of-mouth referrals are popular because they are cheap and come with a trusted recommendation. The EEOC cautions, however, that relying on word-of-mouth recruitment among a mostly homogeneous workforce may be unlawful if it results in screening out people of a particular race, national origin, or other protected group ([EEOC](https://www.eeoc.gov/prohibited-employment-policiespractices)). Use referrals as one channel among several: online job boards, local workforce development centers, community colleges and trade schools, industry associations, and community organizations.

## The Application Process

Keep applications short but purposeful. A common small-business approach asks for a resume plus answers to two or three short questions tied to the role's outcomes. For example: "Describe a time you organized a messy schedule for someone else. What did you do first?" Responses reveal written communication and attention to instructions. Some owners add a simple instruction in the post, such as putting a specific phrase in the subject line, to check attention to detail. Score every application with the same short rubric.

:::example Illustrative: Job Post for an Office Coordinator
Office Coordinator (Part-Time, 25 hours/week) - Riverside Plumbing, Springfield
About us: We are a six-person plumbing company serving homeowners in Springfield since 2014.
Why this role exists: To make sure every customer call is answered, every job is scheduled correctly, and every invoice goes out on time.
What you will own: (1) Answer and return customer calls within two business hours. (2) Schedule technicians with fewer than 2 percent double bookings. (3) Send invoices within one business day of job completion and follow up on unpaid invoices weekly.
What you bring: Clear phone manner, comfort with scheduling software, careful attention to detail, one year of office or customer service experience preferred; training provided.
Pay and schedule: $19 to $22 per hour, Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to 2 p.m., paid holidays.
How to apply: Send your resume and a three-sentence answer to "Describe a time you fixed a scheduling mix-up" to the email listed. We review applications weekly and reply to everyone.
We are an equal opportunity employer. Men and women, and members of all racial and ethnic groups, are encouraged to apply.
:::

:::checklist Job Post Review
- Plain role title, location, and schedule in the headline.
- Outcomes come directly from the scorecard.
- Pay range included (and required if my jurisdiction mandates it).
- No language suggesting age, sex, national origin, disability, or other protected preference.
- Physical requirements included only if essential, with accommodation language.
- Equal opportunity statement included.
- At least three recruiting channels beyond referrals.
- Application questions tied to outcomes; a scoring rubric ready.
:::

:::summary
An effective post answers the candidate's questions and draws directly from the role scorecard. The EEOC prohibits ads that show preference based on protected characteristics, so avoid phrasing like "recent graduates" or "young team." Use multiple recruiting channels to avoid the risks of word-of-mouth-only hiring, and score applications consistently.
:::

# Chapter 5: Structured Interviews That Predict Performance

:::objectives
- Explain why structured interviews outperform unstructured conversations.
- Write behavioral and situational questions tied to the scorecard.
- Build a rating scale with behavioral anchors.
- Avoid questions that create legal risk.
:::

## What the Evidence Says

OPM's guidance contrasts the two interview types directly: unstructured interviews have low reliability, low to moderate validity, and legal vulnerability, while structured interviews have high reliability, high validity, and high legal defensibility ([OPM, Structured Interview Guide](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/structured-interviews/guide/)). OPM adds that higher levels of structure are associated with higher validity and less adverse impact on protected groups ([OPM, Structured Interviews](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/structured-interviews/)).

OPM's Assessment Decision Guide summarizes validity coefficients (correlations with job performance) for common methods ([OPM Assessment Decision Guide](https://piv.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/reference-materials/assessmentdecisionguide.pdf)):

| Method | Approximate validity |
|---|---|
| Work samples | .54 |
| Structured interviews | .51 |
| Job knowledge tests | .48 |
| Unstructured interviews | .38 |
| Reference checks | .26 |
| Years of job experience | .18 |

These figures come from research summarized for federal hiring and should be read as relative guidance: structured methods and work samples predict performance better than unstructured conversation or years of experience alone.

## What Makes an Interview Structured

A structured interview has four features:

1. **Questions derived from the job.** Each question maps to a scorecard outcome or competency.
2. **The same questions for every candidate,** asked in the same order.
3. **A rating scale with defined anchors** describing poor, acceptable, and excellent answers.
4. **Independent scoring** before discussion, ideally by more than one interviewer.

## Behavioral and Situational Questions

OPM distinguishes **behavioral** questions, which ask candidates to describe past experiences ("Tell me about a time when..."), from **situational** questions, which present a hypothetical scenario ("What would you do if...") ([OPM](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/structured-interviews/)). Behavioral questions suit experienced candidates; situational questions work well for entry-level candidates without directly relevant history.

| Scorecard competency | Behavioral question | Situational question |
|---|---|---|
| Customer communication | Tell me about a time a customer was upset. What did you say and do? | A customer calls angry that the technician is late. Walk me through your response. |
| Organization | Describe a time you managed several deadlines at once. How did you prioritize? | You have three urgent tasks and one hour. How do you decide? |
| Attention to detail | Tell me about an error you caught before it caused a problem. | You notice an invoice amount differs from the job ticket. What do you do? |
| Learning | Describe a new tool you learned quickly. How did you approach it? | On day one you are given unfamiliar software. How do you get up to speed? |

## Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scales

For each question, write what a 1, 3, and 5 answer looks like before interviewing anyone.

:::example Illustrative: Anchors for the Upset Customer Question
1 (Poor): Blames the customer or the company; no specific actions; no resolution.
3 (Acceptable): Listens, apologizes, takes a reasonable action, but follow-up is vague.
5 (Excellent): Describes a specific situation; acknowledges feelings; clarifies facts; offers concrete options; follows through and confirms resolution; reflects on what they learned.
Two interviewers score independently, then compare. Large differences prompt discussion of evidence, not impressions.
:::

## Questions to Avoid

The EEOC explains that before a job offer, employers generally may not ask about the existence, nature, or severity of a disability, but may ask whether an applicant can perform the job's functions, with or without reasonable accommodation ([EEOC, Questions and Answers](https://www.eeoc.gov/fact-sheet/federal-laws-prohibiting-job-discrimination-questions-and-answers)). As a practical rule, avoid questions about age, marital status, children or plans for children, pregnancy, religion, national origin, health conditions, and arrest records. Many states and cities also restrict questions about salary history and criminal history.

| Avoid | Ask instead (if job-related) |
|---|---|
| "Do you have kids?" | "This role requires availability 9 to 2, Monday through Friday. Can you meet that schedule?" |
| "Where are you from originally?" | "Are you legally authorized to work in the United States?" |
| "Do you have any health problems?" | "Can you perform the essential functions described, with or without accommodation?" |
| "How old are you?" | (If legally required) "Are you at least 18?" |

## Running the Interview

- Open with a brief overview of the role and the process.
- Ask each question in order; take notes on what the candidate said and did.
- Use neutral follow-ups ("What happened next?" "What was your specific role?").
- Leave time for the candidate's questions; these reveal preparation and priorities.
- Score immediately after the interview, before talking with other interviewers.

:::worksheet My Interview Guide
Competency 1: ___ Question: ___ Anchors 1/3/5: ___
Competency 2: ___ Question: ___ Anchors 1/3/5: ___
Competency 3: ___ Question: ___ Anchors 1/3/5: ___
Competency 4: ___ Question: ___ Anchors 1/3/5: ___
Interviewers: ___
Minimum average score to advance: ___
:::

:::summary
OPM's guidance shows structured interviews are more reliable, valid, and legally defensible than unstructured conversations. Structure means job-derived questions, consistent ordering, anchored rating scales, and independent scoring. Use behavioral and situational questions, and avoid pre-offer questions about disability, age, family, religion, or national origin.
:::

# Chapter 6: Work Samples and Paid Trials

:::objectives
- Explain why work samples predict performance.
- Design a short, fair work sample tied to the scorecard.
- Apply Martell's Test-First Hiring concept.
- Pay candidates fairly and score trials consistently.
:::

## Why Work Samples Work

A work sample asks a candidate to do a realistic slice of the job. In OPM's summary, work samples had the highest validity of the methods listed, about .54 ([OPM Assessment Decision Guide](https://piv.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/reference-materials/assessmentdecisionguide.pdf)). They also give candidates a realistic preview of the work, which helps them decide whether they want it.

## Test-First Hiring

Martell describes **Test-First Hiring**: before making a hire, give the candidate a paid trial project and score the result against a rubric written in advance ([Eckfeldt](https://www.eckfeldt.com/book-reviews-dan-martell-buy-back-your-time)). The principle is that how someone actually does the work matters more than how well they describe it.

## Designing a Work Sample

1. **Choose a representative task** tied to a top scorecard outcome.
2. **Keep it short:** one to four hours for most roles.
3. **Provide realistic materials:** a sample schedule, a dataset, a customer email, but never real customer data.
4. **Write the rubric first,** with criteria and point values.
5. **Pay for the time** at the role's hourly rate. Paying respects candidates and reduces the appearance of extracting free work. Consult a professional about how to pay (for example, as a short-term contractor or on payroll), since classification rules apply.
6. **Give everyone the same instructions and time.**
7. **Score blind where possible,** removing names before scoring.

| Role | Work sample | Rubric criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Office coordinator | Schedule 10 jobs across 3 technicians using constraints; draft two customer emails | Accuracy, constraint compliance, tone, completeness |
| Bookkeeper | Reconcile a sample bank statement with 30 transactions | Accuracy, flagged discrepancies, documentation |
| Marketing assistant | Draft three social posts and one email from a brief | Brand fit, clarity, accuracy, call to action |
| Technician | Supervised diagnosis on a training unit | Safety, method, diagnosis accuracy, communication |
| Virtual assistant | Organize an inbox of 40 sample emails and draft five replies | Prioritization, tone, follow-up flags |

:::example Illustrative: Office Coordinator Rubric (20 points)
Scheduling exercise (12 points): no double bookings (4); travel time respected (3); priority jobs placed first (3); notes clear (2).
Customer emails (8 points): accurate information (3); courteous tone (2); clear next step (2); proofread (1).
Threshold to advance: 15 points. Paid: two hours at $20 per hour.
:::

## Accommodations and Fairness

Offer reasonable accommodations if a candidate requests them because of a disability, and ensure the sample measures job-relevant skills only. Avoid tasks that depend on knowledge only insiders could have.

:::checklist Work Sample Design
- Task tied to a top scorecard outcome.
- Duration of four hours or less.
- Realistic materials with no real customer data.
- Rubric written before any candidate starts.
- Candidates paid for their time; payment method reviewed.
- Identical instructions and time limits.
- Accommodation process communicated.
- Scoring done blind where possible.
:::

:::summary
Work samples have the highest validity among the methods in OPM's summary. Martell's Test-First Hiring applies the idea through a paid trial project scored against a pre-written rubric. Keep samples short, realistic, and paid, and use identical instructions and blind scoring for fairness.
:::

# Chapter 7: References, Offers, and Required Paperwork

:::objectives
- Conduct useful, lawful reference checks.
- Make a clear written offer.
- Complete Form I-9 and Form W-4 on time.
- Report new hires to the state and keep required records.
:::

## Reference Checks

Reference checks had lower validity in OPM's summary (about .26) than structured interviews or work samples ([OPM Assessment Decision Guide](https://piv.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/reference-materials/assessmentdecisionguide.pdf)), so treat them as a check for red flags rather than a primary selection tool. Ask the candidate's permission, speak with former supervisors when possible, and use the same questions for each reference:

- In what capacity did you work with the candidate, and for how long?
- What were their main responsibilities?
- What did they do especially well?
- Where did they need the most support?
- Would you rehire them? Why or why not?

Many employers will confirm only dates and title. Background checks, if used, must comply with the Fair Credit Reporting Act and state laws, including written disclosure and authorization; consult a professional before using them.

## The Written Offer

A written offer prevents misunderstandings. It should state the role title, start date, pay rate and schedule, classification (exempt or non-exempt; full- or part-time), supervisor, benefits, any probationary or introductory period, contingencies such as work authorization, and at-will language if applicable in your state. Avoid promises of permanent employment or guaranteed raises. Have an attorney review your template once.

## Form I-9

Every U.S. employer must verify the identity and employment authorization of each new hire on Form I-9. USCIS explains that the employee must complete Section 1 no later than the first day of employment, and the employer must complete Section 2 within three business days of the employee's first day; for example, if the employee starts on Monday, Section 2 is due by Thursday ([USCIS, Section 2](https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central/completing-form-i-9/completing-section-2-employer-review-and-attestation)). The employee chooses which acceptable documents to present from the Lists of Acceptable Documents; employers may not specify which documents an employee must provide ([USCIS Form I-9](https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/forms/i-9.pdf)).

## Form W-4 and State Forms

The employee completes Form W-4 so you can withhold federal income tax. Most states have their own withholding form. Collect these before the first payroll.

## New-Hire Reporting

Federal law requires employers to report new hires to the state new-hire registry. The Administration for Children and Families explains that employers must report within 20 days of hire, that some states require shorter timeframes, and that states may impose penalties of up to $25 per unreported hire, or up to $500 if the failure results from a conspiracy ([ACF, New Hire Reporting](https://acf.gov/css/employers/employer-responsibilities/new-hire-reporting)). Most payroll services handle this automatically; confirm that yours does.

## Recordkeeping

The IRS advises keeping employment tax records for at least four years after the tax becomes due or is paid, whichever is later ([IRS, How Long Should I Keep Records](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-long-should-i-keep-records)). Keep I-9 forms separately from personnel files according to USCIS retention rules, and keep medical information in a separate confidential file.

|---|---|---|
| Written offer accepted | Before start | Owner, candidate |
| I-9 Section 1 | By first day of work | Employee |
| I-9 Section 2 | Within 3 business days of first day | Employer |
| W-4 and state withholding | Before first payroll | Employee |
| New-hire report | Within 20 days (or state deadline) | Employer or payroll service |
| Workers' compensation coverage | Before first day | Employer |
| Required posters displayed | Ongoing | Employer |

# Chapter 8: The First Week and Onboarding

:::objectives
- Explain why the first week shapes retention and performance.
- Prepare before the new hire arrives.
- Design a day-by-day first-week plan.
- Apply lessons from Zappos's "Offer" to your own onboarding.
:::

## Why the First Week Matters

New employees form impressions quickly. A disorganized first day, with no workspace, no login, and no plan, signals that the role is an afterthought. A prepared first week signals respect, clarifies expectations, and accelerates the time to useful contribution. For a small business, it also protects the owner's time, because questions are anticipated rather than fielded one at a time.

## Before Day One

- Send a welcome message with start time, location or login instructions, parking, dress expectations, and who to ask for.
- Set up accounts, email, software seats, and equipment.
- Prepare the I-9 and withholding forms.
- Print or share the role scorecard and the first-week schedule.
- Tell the existing team who is starting and what they will do.
- Assign a "buddy" who will answer everyday questions.

## A First-Week Framework

| Day | Focus | Activities |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Welcome and foundations | Paperwork; tour; meet the team; review scorecard; set up tools; owner explains the business, customers, and values |
| 2 | Observe | Shadow the owner or buddy doing the core tasks; take notes; ask questions |
| 3 | Practice with support | Perform tasks with the playbook while the trainer watches; immediate feedback |
| 4 | Perform with review | Perform tasks independently; trainer reviews output at set checkpoints |
| 5 | Reflect and plan | 30-minute check-in: what is clear, what is confusing, what support is needed; set goals for weeks 2 through 4 |

## The 30-60-90 Plan

Extend the first week into a simple plan: by day 30, the new hire performs core tasks with the playbook and review; by day 60, performs core tasks independently and meets most scorecard targets; by day 90, meets scorecard targets consistently and suggests one improvement. Write the plan down and review it at the end of each period.

:::case Zappos and "The Offer"
Harvard Business Review reported in 2008 that Zappos put new call-center hires through a four-week paid training program and, about a week in, offered them payment for the time they had worked plus a $1,000 bonus to quit. The amount had grown from $100 to $500 to $1,000, and roughly 10 percent of new call-center employees accepted ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2008/05/why-zappos-pays-new-employees)). Business Insider later reported the offer rose to $2,000 ([Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/zappos-tony-hsieh-paid-new-workers-to-quit-the-offer-2020-11)), and The Conference Board notes Amazon adopted a similar "Pay to Quit" program of up to $5,000 ([The Conference Board](https://www.conference-board.org/research/human-capital-briefs/blg-00-1-3025)). The lesson for small businesses is not to pay people to quit, which few can afford, but that a structured early period with an honest exit point helps both sides confirm fit before more is invested. Zappos's results are its own, not TTI's.
:::

## A Small-Business Adaptation

You can adapt the idea without a bonus: schedule an explicit "fit conversation" at the end of week two. Ask: "Is this the job you expected? What surprised you? Do you want to continue?" Share your own honest observations. A candid conversation early is far less costly than a slow failure months later.

:::example Illustrative: First Week for a Landscaping Crew Member
Day 1: Paperwork, safety orientation, equipment tour, meet crew lead (buddy), review scorecard (safe operation, quality standards, on-time arrival).
Day 2: Ride along; observe mowing, edging, and cleanup; review the quality checklist at each property.
Day 3: Operate mower under supervision at two properties; crew lead gives feedback using the checklist.
Day 4: Complete three properties with crew lead checking finished work.
Day 5: Owner meets with new hire for 30 minutes: what is working, what is unclear, goals for week two. Fit conversation scheduled for day 10.
:::

Extend the plan to 30, 60, and 90 days. Zappos's "Offer" illustrates the value of an honest early fit point.

# Chapter 9: Teaching the Work

:::objectives
- Capture know-how using the Camcorder Method.
- Write playbooks and checklists that new hires can follow.
- Apply Martell's 4Cs framework.
- Evaluate training through observation and output.
:::

## The Knowledge Transfer Problem

Owners carry years of tacit knowledge: how to handle a difficult customer, which supplier to call, how to price a nonstandard job. When this knowledge is never written down, every new hire depends on the owner for answers, and the owner never escapes. Training is the mechanism for transferring this knowledge into the business itself.

## The Camcorder Method

Martell's **Camcorder Method** is simple: record yourself doing the task once, narrating what you are doing and why, then hand the recording to the new person, who turns it into a written playbook ([Eckfeldt](https://www.eckfeldt.com/book-reviews-dan-martell-buy-back-your-time); [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/buy-back-your-time/summary/)). Recording costs the owner little extra time because the task was going to be done anyway, and asking the new hire to write the playbook tests their understanding.

:::case Stuart's Recorded Tasks
In a summary of Buy Back Your Time, Martell describes a client, Stuart, whose time was worth about $100 an hour but who spent his days on $10 tasks. Stuart filmed himself doing those tasks, hired two people, and used the recordings to train them. According to the summary, he freed more than 30 hours a week and cut his working day from 11 hours to 6 ([Deven Davis](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). This is a single anecdote from a practitioner book; Stuart's results are his own, not TTI's, and are not a prediction for any other business.
:::

## The 4Cs

Martell organizes training materials into the **4Cs** ([Eckfeldt](https://www.eckfeldt.com/book-reviews-dan-martell-buy-back-your-time)):

| C | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Camcorder | Video of the task done well | Screen recording of entering a job in scheduling software |
| Course | Step-by-step written process | Playbook: "How to schedule a new job" |
| Cadence | How often and when the task is done | Daily at 8 a.m. and 1 p.m. |
| Checklist | Short list to confirm the task is done correctly | Five-item scheduling checklist |

## Writing a Playbook

A good playbook has:

1. **Title and purpose:** what the task achieves.
2. **Trigger:** when the task starts.
3. **Tools and access needed.**
4. **Steps:** numbered, one action per step, with screenshots where helpful.
5. **Decision points:** "If X, do Y; if unsure, ask Z."
6. **Quality standard:** what "done" looks like.
7. **Owner and last-updated date.**

Write for a competent new person who has never seen your business. Test it by having someone follow it without help; every question they ask reveals a gap.

## Training Sequence: Tell, Show, Do, Review

- **Tell:** explain the purpose and standard.
- **Show:** demonstrate or play the recording.
- **Do:** the learner performs the task with the playbook.
- **Review:** compare output to the standard; give specific feedback.

Repeat "do" and "review" until the learner meets the standard consistently, then reduce checking frequency.

:::example Illustrative: Playbook Excerpt
Title: Sending Invoices After Job Completion.
Purpose: Get paid promptly and accurately.
Trigger: Technician marks job "complete" in the field app.
Tools: Field app, accounting software, customer email.
Steps: (1) Open completed job. (2) Confirm parts and labor match the technician's notes. (3) If any line is missing, message the technician before invoicing. (4) Create invoice from job. (5) Attach photos if included. (6) Send by email with standard message. (7) Mark job "invoiced."
Quality standard: Invoice sent within one business day; no missing lines; customer name and address correct.
Owner: Office coordinator. Last updated: March 2026.
:::

:::checklist Training Materials
- Top five tasks for the role identified.
- Each task recorded once with narration.
- New hire drafts playbooks from recordings; owner reviews.
- Each playbook has trigger, steps, decision points, and standard.
- Cadence and checklist written for each task.
- Playbooks stored in one shared, searchable location.
- Each playbook has an owner and a review date.
:::

:::summary
Owners hold tacit knowledge that must be transferred into the business. Martell's Camcorder Method records tasks once and has the new hire write the playbook. The 4Cs (Camcorder, Course, Cadence, Checklist) organize training materials. Teach with tell, show, do, review, and test playbooks by having someone follow them unaided.
:::

# Chapter 10: Delegation and the Management Rhythm

:::objectives
- Apply the 10-80-10 delegation approach.
- Define "done" for delegated work.
- Establish a weekly management rhythm.
- Give feedback that is specific, timely, and fair.
:::

## Why Delegation Fails

Delegation fails in predictable ways: the task is handed off without a clear outcome, the owner hovers and redoes the work, or the owner disappears entirely and discovers problems too late. Martell's view, quoted in summaries, is that "80% done by somebody else is 100% freaking awesome" ([Deven Davis](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)): accepting good-enough work by others is the price of buying back time.

## The 10-80-10 Rule

Martell's **10-80-10 rule** divides a delegated project into three parts: the owner provides the first 10 percent (vision, direction, and standards), the team member does the middle 80 percent, and the owner returns for the final 10 percent (review, polish, and approval) ([Eckfeldt](https://www.eckfeldt.com/book-reviews-dan-martell-buy-back-your-time)). This keeps the owner involved where judgment matters most, without doing the bulk of the work.

## Definition of Done

Every delegated task needs a **Definition of Done**: a short statement of what the finished result looks like, how it will be checked, and when it is due. A useful formula:

| Element | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | What result do we want? | A list of 20 qualified local suppliers |
| Standard | What does good look like? | Each with contact, pricing, delivery time, minimum order |
| Deadline | When is it due? | Friday at noon |
| Check-in | When will we review progress? | Wednesday, 10 minutes |
| Authority | What decisions can you make alone? | Can contact suppliers; cannot commit to purchases |

## Decision Rights

Clarify which decisions the team member can make independently, which require a recommendation first, and which the owner keeps. A common approach is three levels:

1. **Act and inform later:** routine decisions within guidelines (for example, refunds under $50).
2. **Recommend, then act:** propose a solution and wait for approval (for example, new supplier).
3. **Owner decides:** strategic, legal, or high-cost decisions.

Some practitioners use a "1-3-1" format for level-two decisions: one clearly stated problem, three options, and one recommendation. It trains team members to think through problems instead of bringing them unprocessed.

## The Weekly Rhythm

| Meeting | Frequency | Length | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily huddle | Daily | 10 minutes | Priorities, blockers |
| One-on-one | Weekly | 30 minutes | Progress, feedback, support, development |
| Team meeting | Weekly | 45 minutes | Metrics, problems, improvements |
| Scorecard review | Monthly | 30 minutes | Review outcome measures against targets |

Keep the one-on-one sacred. The employee owns the agenda for the first half; the owner owns the second half.

## Giving Feedback

Effective feedback is specific, timely, and focused on behavior and impact, not personality. A simple structure is situation, behavior, impact, and next step:

- **Situation:** "In this morning's call with the Smith family..."
- **Behavior:** "...you confirmed the appointment time and repeated the address back."
- **Impact:** "That prevented the mix-up we had last month."
- **Next step:** "Please do that on every call."

Use the same structure for corrective feedback, and deliver it privately and promptly. Document significant corrective conversations with the date, the issue, and the agreed next step.

:::example Illustrative: Applying 10-80-10
The owner of a bakery delegates creating a holiday catering menu.
First 10 percent: owner explains goals (12 items, three price tiers, 40 percent food-cost ceiling), shares last year's menu, and sets a Definition of Done due in two weeks with a check-in after one week.
Middle 80 percent: the manager researches, tests recipes, and drafts the menu and costing.
Final 10 percent: owner reviews, asks for two substitutions, approves pricing.
The owner spends about 3 hours instead of an estimated 20.
:::

:::worksheet Delegation Plan
Task to delegate: ___
Delegate to: ___
Outcome: ___
Standard: ___
Deadline: ___ Check-in: ___
Authority level (1, 2, or 3): ___
My first 10 percent: ___
My final 10 percent: ___
:::

:::summary
Delegation fails without clear outcomes, appropriate oversight, and acceptance of good-enough work. Martell's 10-80-10 rule keeps owners involved at the start and end. A Definition of Done, clear decision rights, a weekly management rhythm, and specific feedback make delegation reliable.
:::

# Chapter 11: Reviews, Retention, and When It Is Not Working

:::objectives
- Run fair, evidence-based performance reviews.
- Identify practical retention levers for small businesses.
- Manage performance problems with documentation and support.
- Handle separations lawfully and respectfully.
:::

## Performance Reviews

If feedback happens weekly, the formal review contains no surprises. Hold reviews at least twice a year, using the role scorecard as the frame. A simple format:

1. **Outcomes:** results against each scorecard measure, with data.
2. **Competencies:** examples of strengths and growth areas.
3. **Employee self-assessment:** completed before the meeting.
4. **Development:** one or two skills to build in the next period and how the business will support it.
5. **Compensation:** discussed separately or at a predictable time, based on clear criteria.

Apply the same criteria to every person in the same role. Inconsistent standards are unfair and create legal risk.

## Retention Levers

Small businesses rarely win on pay alone, but they have levers large companies struggle to match:

| Lever | Practical actions |
|---|---|
| Clarity | Scorecards, playbooks, Definition of Done |
| Growth | Cross-training, paid courses, new responsibilities |
| Recognition | Specific, timely praise; sharing customer compliments |
| Flexibility | Predictable schedules; reasonable time-off requests honored |
| Fair pay | Market-based ranges; periodic reviews |
| Voice | Ask for improvement ideas and act on some |
| Respect | Owner keeps commitments; problems addressed promptly |

## When It Is Not Working

Not every hire works out. A fair process protects the business and treats the employee with dignity:

1. **Name the gap early.** Use the feedback structure and refer to the scorecard.
2. **Diagnose.** Is the issue clarity, training, tools, capacity, or willingness? Many problems trace back to unclear expectations or missing training.
3. **Agree on an improvement plan.** Specific measures, support provided, and a timeline (often 30 to 60 days).
4. **Document.** Date, facts, expectations, support offered, and progress.
5. **Review on schedule.** Recognize improvement or move to a decision.

## Avoiding Retaliation and Discrimination

The EEOC enforces laws prohibiting discrimination and retaliation. Never take adverse action because someone complained about discrimination, requested an accommodation, took protected leave, or participated in an investigation ([EEOC](https://www.eeoc.gov/prohibited-employment-policiespractices)). Make decisions based on documented, job-related performance, apply standards consistently, and consult an employment attorney before terminating someone who has recently engaged in protected activity.

## Separation

If you must end employment, prepare: confirm documentation, check state rules on final paychecks (some states require payment on the last day), review benefits continuation obligations, retrieve equipment and revoke system access, and conduct the conversation privately, briefly, and respectfully. Afterward, review what you would change in the role design, selection, or training.

:::example Illustrative: An Improvement Plan
Situation: A customer service hire is missing the two-hour callback target (achieving 70 percent versus 95 percent target).
Diagnosis: In conversation, the employee explains that callbacks are deprioritized when walk-in customers arrive; the playbook does not address this conflict.
Plan (30 days): Owner clarifies priority rules; adds a callback block at 11 a.m. and 3 p.m.; tracks callbacks daily; weekly check-ins.
Outcome review: At day 30, callbacks within two hours reach 93 percent. The playbook is updated for all staff. The issue was a process gap, not a person problem.
:::

:::checklist Fair Performance Management
- Reviews use the role scorecard and the same criteria for everyone in the role.
- Feedback is given weekly so reviews contain no surprises.
- Gaps are diagnosed before conclusions are drawn.
- Improvement plans have measures, support, and timelines.
- Documentation is factual and dated.
- No adverse action follows protected activity without legal review.
- Separation steps follow state final-pay rules.
:::

:::summary
Reviews should be scorecard-based, evidence-driven, and consistent. Small businesses retain people through clarity, growth, recognition, flexibility, fair pay, voice, and respect. When performance falls short, diagnose, plan, document, and review on schedule, avoid retaliation, and handle separations lawfully and respectfully.
:::

# Chapter 12: From First Hire to a Team

:::objectives
- Recognize how management changes as headcount grows.
- Decide when to promote or hire a manager.
- Write simple team norms and values that guide behavior.
- Build a one-page organization chart and accountability map.
:::

## How Management Changes With Size

The jump from zero to one employee changes the owner's job from doing to directing. The jump from three or four to eight or ten changes it again, from directing individuals to building a team that coordinates without the owner in every conversation. Martell's "pain line," at roughly 12 direct reports, describes the point where an owner who still manages everyone personally becomes the bottleneck ([Deven Davis](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)).

| Stage | Typical headcount | Owner's main job | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solo | 0 | Doing the work | Burnout, no capacity |
| First hires | 1 to 3 | Teaching and delegating | Owner redoes work |
| Small team | 4 to 9 | Running the rhythm, coaching | Owner is the only decision-maker |
| Managed team | 10 or more | Developing managers, setting direction | Managers lack authority or training |

## When to Add a Manager

Signals that you need a team lead or manager include: you spend more than a third of your week answering routine questions; problems wait for you because no one else can decide; quality varies when you are absent; and one-on-ones are being skipped because there are too many. The final rung of Martell's Replacement Ladder is leadership, which he places after administrative, delivery, marketing, and sales roles ([Eckfeldt](https://www.eckfeldt.com/book-reviews-dan-martell-buy-back-your-time)).

## Promote or Hire?

Promoting a strong performer rewards loyalty and preserves know-how, but excellent individual contributors do not automatically become good managers. Hiring an experienced manager brings skills but risks a culture mismatch. Whichever path you choose, apply the same disciplines from earlier chapters: write a scorecard for the manager role (for example, team meets service targets, turnover below a set level, playbooks kept current), use structured interview questions about past management situations, and run a work sample such as leading a mock one-on-one or reviewing a problem scenario.

| Option | Advantages | Risks | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Promote from within | Knows the work, trusted, motivating to others | Lacks management skills; former peers | Training, a mentor, a 90-day plan, clear authority |
| Hire externally | Brings experience and new methods | Culture fit; slower to learn the work | Structured selection; thorough onboarding |

## Team Norms and Values

Values posters rarely change behavior. Specific norms do. A **team norm** is a concrete, observable behavior the team agrees to, such as "We return customer calls within two business hours," "We flag problems the same day we see them," or "We do not criticize a teammate in front of customers." Write five to eight norms with the team, connect each to a reason, and refer to them in feedback and reviews. Norms must apply equally to everyone, including the owner.

## The Accountability Map

As the team grows, ambiguity about who owns what causes dropped balls. A simple one-page accountability map lists each major function (sales, scheduling, delivery, billing, hiring, marketing), the single person accountable, the key measure, and the playbooks that support it. When a function has two owners, it effectively has none; when it has no owner, it defaults back to the founder.

:::example Illustrative: Accountability Map for an Eight-Person HVAC Company
Sales and estimates: Owner (close rate, average ticket).
Scheduling and dispatch: Office coordinator (on-time arrival rate).
Service delivery: Lead technician (callback rate, safety incidents).
Billing and collections: Office coordinator (days sales outstanding).
Marketing: Part-time marketing contractor, reviewed by owner (leads per month).
Hiring and onboarding: Owner, with lead technician running work samples (90-day retention).
Playbooks: Each owner maintains the playbooks for their function, reviewed quarterly.
The map shows the owner still holds sales and hiring, which suggests the next hire or promotion.
:::

## Communication as the Team Grows

With more people, information that once traveled by conversation gets lost. Establish a few reliable channels: a shared task or job board, a single place for playbooks, the weekly team meeting, and a written weekly update from each function lead covering results, problems, and next priorities. Keep messages about performance private and messages about priorities public.

# Glossary

**Behavioral question:** An interview question asking about past experience.

**Buyback Loop:** Martell's cycle of auditing time, transferring tasks, and filling freed time with higher-value work.

**Buyback Rate:** Annual pay divided by 2,000 hours and then by four; a heuristic for affordable delegation.

**Camcorder Method:** Recording a task once with narration so a new hire can turn it into a playbook.

**Definition of Done:** A statement of outcome, standard, deadline, check-in, and authority for delegated work.

**DRIP Matrix:** Martell's four quadrants of Delegation, Replacement, Investment, and Production.

**FICA:** Social Security and Medicare taxes; the employer pays 7.65 percent of wages up to applicable limits.

**Form I-9:** USCIS form verifying identity and employment authorization.

**FUTA:** Federal unemployment tax; 6.0 percent on the first $7,000, often reduced to 0.6 percent by state credit.

**Playbook:** A written, step-by-step process for a recurring task.

**Replacement Ladder:** Martell's suggested hiring sequence from assistant to leadership.

**Role scorecard:** A role definition based on mission, outcomes, competencies, and conditions.

**Situational question:** An interview question presenting a hypothetical scenario.

**Structured interview:** An interview with job-derived questions, consistent order, anchored ratings, and independent scoring.

**Test-First Hiring:** Paid trial projects scored against a pre-written rubric.

**Work sample:** A realistic slice of the job performed by a candidate.
