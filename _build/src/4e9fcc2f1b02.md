# How to Use This Book

A virtual assistant (VA) business sells reliable, remote help with defined work: managing inboxes and calendars, coordinating projects, preparing documents, handling customer messages under approval, maintaining records, and supporting the many small tasks that consume a business owner's time. Demand for this kind of help is real. Upwork's Freelance Forward 2023 study reported that 64 million Americans performed freelance work in the past year, representing 38 percent of the U.S. workforce [1]. Many business owners have learned that delegating routine work can free them to focus on higher-value activities.

Yet many new virtual assistant businesses struggle. The typical pattern is a vague offer ("I can help with anything"), underpricing, unclear scope, unsafe access to client accounts, and an exhausting mix of small tasks with no system behind them. This book is designed to prevent those problems. It treats a VA business as a professional service firm: one that sells a clear deliverable, protects the client's time and information, and runs on documented processes.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Explain the virtual assistant market and the main business models.
- Choose a niche and define a small, useful first offer.
- Turn vague client requests into a written scope card.
- Price services using hourly, retainer, and package models, and calculate your real effective rate.
- Find first clients through warm networks, referrals, and marketplaces, while avoiding scams.
- Prepare agreements and understand independent contractor basics.
- Onboard clients securely using least-privilege access and multifactor authentication.
- Build handoff queues, standard operating procedures, and communication routines.
- Check quality, handle errors, and report progress.
- Manage taxes, records, and business finances.
- Renew, expand, and eventually bring on subcontractors responsibly.

## How This Book Is Built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, explains its concepts, and gives a step-by-step method. Each includes a worked example with numbers labelled "illustrative," a real, publicly documented case with numbered citations to the References, a table, a worksheet or checklist, a summary, and review questions. Cases include BELAY, Chris Ducker's Virtual Staff Finder, and Dan Martell's writing on delegation. These are discussed because they are well documented, not because their outcomes are typical.

# Chapter 1: The Virtual Assistant Business Model

:::objectives
- Describe the market for remote administrative and operational support.
- Compare the main VA business models.
- Identify the economics that determine whether a VA business is sustainable.
- Recognize the difference between selling hours and selling outcomes.
:::

The term "virtual assistant" covers a wide range of work, from basic data entry to sophisticated executive support and project coordination. What unites it is that the work is performed remotely, for clients who are usually small businesses or busy professionals, under arrangements that are typically contractual rather than employment.

## The Market

Two forces support demand. First, there are many very small businesses. The U.S. Census Bureau reported 29.8 million nonemployer businesses in 2022, businesses with no paid employees, accounting for about $1.7 trillion in receipts [22]. Many of these owners do all their own administration. Second, remote work has become normal for many kinds of professional tasks, and freelancing is widespread, as the Upwork study indicates [1].

It is also useful to understand the employment benchmark. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported a 2025 median pay of $48,310 per year, or $23.23 per hour, for secretaries and administrative assistants [2]. A business owner comparing a VA to an employee will consider not only wages but also payroll taxes, benefits, equipment, and management time, as well as the flexibility of part-time help.

## Business Models

- **Hourly.** The client pays for time worked. Simple, but income depends on hours, and clients may scrutinize every minute.
- **Retainer.** The client pays a fixed monthly fee for a block of hours or availability. Predictable for both sides.
- **Package or deliverable.** The client pays a fixed price for a defined result (for example, a weekly inbox cleanup and summary, or monthly invoicing). Rewards efficiency.
- **Agency or team.** The owner sells services delivered by a team of assistants. Requires management, systems, and careful attention to worker classification.

## Hours Versus Outcomes

Clients do not want hours; they want relief from work, fewer missed opportunities, and confidence that tasks are handled well. The more clearly a VA business describes outcomes, the less it competes on hourly price alone.

:::case BELAY: A Fractional Staffing Model
BELAY was founded in 2010 by Bryan and Shannon Miles, who brought on Tricia Sciortino as the company's first hire and first assistant, according to the company's published history [3]. The company states that leaders needed part-time, remote support they could count on and that BELAY built its model to deliver it [3]. It reports that a 2011 client referral brought on Michael Hyatt, whose visibility helped prove the fractional staffing model, that it was first named to the Inc. 5000 in 2014, and that it has served more than 25,000 leaders [3]. In 2020, the founders stepped back and named Sciortino CEO [3]. The case shows that part-time remote support can be a durable business when it is professionally organized. BELAY's results are its own and reflect years of building teams and systems.
:::

:::example Comparing Models (illustrative)
Monique estimates she can sell 25 billable hours per week once established, out of about 40 working hours (the rest go to admin, marketing, and learning). At $45 per hour, 25 hours yields $1,125 per week, or about $58,500 per year if she works 52 weeks, but she plans for 4 weeks off, so 48 weeks gives $54,000. Under a retainer model, she might sell four clients a $1,400 monthly package (up to 25 hours each, though most clients use about 20): $5,600 per month, or $67,200 per year. The retainer produces more revenue for similar hours because it prices availability and outcomes rather than minutes. These figures are before expenses and taxes and are not predictions.
:::

| Model | Income predictability | Client perception | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Low | Easy to understand | Income tied to hours; scrutiny of time |
| Retainer | High | Reliable support | Scope creep if limits are unclear |
| Package | Medium to high | Clear result | Underestimating effort |
| Agency or team | Variable | Scalable support | Management load; classification issues |

# Chapter 2: Choosing a Niche and Offer

:::objectives
- Explain why specialization helps a new VA business.
- Select a niche based on experience, demand, and access.
- Define a small, useful first offer.
- Write a clear offer statement.
:::

"I can help with anything" sounds flexible but is difficult to buy. Buyers want to know that you understand their world, their tools, and their problems. A niche (a defined type of client) and a focused offer make marketing easier, referrals more likely, and work more efficient because tasks repeat.

## Step 1: Inventory Your Experience

List industries you have worked in, tools you know well, and tasks you perform reliably. Prior experience is a credibility asset. A former legal secretary understands deadlines and confidentiality; a former real estate coordinator understands transaction timelines.

## Step 2: Evaluate Demand and Access

For each candidate niche, ask: Do these businesses commonly lack administrative help? Can they afford it? Can I reach them through my network, associations, or online communities? Do I understand their tools?

## Step 3: Watch for Regulated Work

Some niches involve regulated information or decisions: health information, financial accounts, legal matters, or tax preparation. These are not off-limits, but they require additional knowledge, agreements, and safeguards. A new VA may prefer to start with less sensitive work and add regulated niches later with proper preparation.

## Step 4: Define the First Offer

A good first offer is small, useful, recurring, and easy to explain. Examples: daily inbox triage with an end-of-day summary; calendar and appointment management; weekly invoicing and payment follow-up; or customer inquiry responses using approved templates.

## Step 5: Write the Offer Statement

Template: "I help [niche] [achieve outcome] by [service], so they can [benefit]."

:::example Monique's Niche Decision (illustrative)
Monique scores three niches from 1 to 5 on experience, demand, ability to pay, and access. Small trade contractors: 5, 5, 4, 5 = 19. Real estate agents: 2, 4, 4, 3 = 13. Online coaches: 1, 3, 3, 2 = 9. She chooses trade contractors. Her offer: "I help owners of small electrical, plumbing, and HVAC companies get out of their inbox and back on the job by managing email, scheduling, and quote follow-up, so fewer leads slip through the cracks." In conversations with five contractors she knows, four say quote follow-up is the task they most often neglect.
:::

:::case Dan Martell: Why Owners Delegate
Software entrepreneur Dan Martell, author of Buy Back Your Time, said in a 2023 podcast interview that working with an executive assistant is "literally the highest form of leverage" and that, starting from scratch, he would hire one first [4]. He argued that founders often try to hire senior operators before freeing their own calendars [4]. For a VA, this perspective is useful in two ways: it explains why some owners value assistance highly, and it suggests a message, freeing the owner's time for higher-value work, that resonates with business owners. Martell's views and results are his own.
:::

# Chapter 3: Turning Requests into a Scope Card

:::objectives
- Recognize scope creep and its causes.
- Convert a vague request into a written scope card.
- Define inputs, outputs, decision rights, and finish conditions.
- Handle change requests professionally.
:::

Clients often describe needs vaguely: "Help me get organized," "Take some things off my plate," "Handle my email." Vague requests produce misunderstandings, unpaid work, and frustration on both sides. A scope card is a one-page description of the work that both parties approve before it begins.

## Elements of a Scope Card

1. **Task name and purpose.** What the task is and why it matters to the client.
2. **Inputs.** What the client provides: access, templates, information, decisions.
3. **Steps.** What the assistant does.
4. **Outputs.** What the client receives and in what form.
5. **Decision rights.** What the assistant may decide alone, what requires approval, and what is always the client's decision.
6. **Frequency and timing.** How often and by when.
7. **Finish condition.** How both parties know the task is done.
8. **Out of scope.** What is not included.

## Step 1: Ask Clarifying Questions

What does "done" look like? What happens now? What goes wrong? Who else is involved? What must never happen?

## Step 2: Draft and Confirm

Write the scope card and send it to the client for approval. Adjust after the first week.

## Step 3: Handle Change Requests

When a client asks for something outside scope, respond helpfully: confirm the request, explain that it is outside the current scope, and offer options (add it to the package, replace another task, or quote it separately).

:::example Inbox Scope Card (illustrative)
Monique's first client, a plumbing contractor, says, "Just handle my email." She drafts a scope card. Purpose: ensure no customer inquiry waits more than four business hours. Inputs: delegated inbox access, a list of VIP contacts, five approved reply templates. Steps: check the inbox at 9 a.m., noon, and 3 p.m.; label and file messages; reply to routine inquiries using templates; flag quotes and complaints. Outputs: an end-of-day summary listing flagged items. Decision rights: Monique may send template replies and schedule estimates on open calendar slots; she may not agree to prices, discounts, or refunds. Finish condition: inbox at zero unprocessed messages at 3:30 p.m. Out of scope: bookkeeping and vendor negotiations. The client approves it with one change: add supplier invoices to the flagged list.
:::

:::case Martell's Delegation Framework
In discussing Buy Back Your Time, Dan Martell described a "Buyback Loop" of auditing tasks, transferring them, and filling the freed time with higher-value work [4]. The transfer step depends on clear documentation; tasks that are not defined cannot be delegated reliably. A scope card is a practical form of that documentation, written from the assistant's side. Martell's frameworks are his own and are presented here for their practical logic, not as proof of any result.
:::

| Scope element | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs | What does the client provide? | Templates, access, VIP list |
| Steps | What does the assistant do? | Check, label, reply, flag |
| Outputs | What does the client receive? | End-of-day summary |
| Decision rights | Who decides what? | No pricing or refunds |
| Finish condition | When is it done? | Zero unprocessed at 3:30 p.m. |
| Out of scope | What is excluded? | Bookkeeping |

# Chapter 4: Pricing and Packages

:::objectives
- Calculate a target rate from income goals, costs, and billable hours.
- Design retainer packages with clear limits.
- Account for platform fees and self-employment taxes.
- Raise prices professionally over time.
:::

Pricing is where many VA businesses fail. Rates set by comparing to the cheapest marketplace listings often cannot cover taxes, software, unbilled time, and time off. A sustainable price begins with the business's real costs.

## Step 1: Start from the Income Goal

Decide the annual income you need before tax. Add business expenses (software, equipment, insurance, professional fees, marketing) and an allowance for self-employment tax. The IRS explains that self-employment tax consists of Social Security and Medicare taxes for individuals who work for themselves, at a combined rate of 15.3 percent on net earnings, subject to limits [16].

## Step 2: Estimate Billable Hours

Not every working hour is billable. Marketing, admin, learning, and gaps between clients consume time. Many solo service providers bill 50 to 70 percent of their working hours. Account for holidays, sick days, and vacation.

## Step 3: Calculate the Target Rate

Target hourly rate = (income goal + expenses + tax allowance) / annual billable hours.

## Step 4: Build Packages

Convert the rate into packages with clear limits: hours or tasks included, response times, communication channels, and what happens if the limit is exceeded. Typical structures include a starter package, a standard package, and a priority package.

## Step 5: Account for Platform Fees

If you find clients through a marketplace, include its fees. Upwork, for example, states that its freelancer service fee ranges from 0 to 15 percent of earnings, set per contract [6].

## Step 6: Review Prices Annually

Raise prices for new clients first. For existing clients, give advance written notice and explain the value delivered. Some clients will leave; that is often acceptable if the business replaces them at the new rate.

:::example Monique's Rate Calculation (illustrative)
Income goal before income tax: $55,000. Expenses: software $1,800, equipment $1,200, insurance $700, accountant $900, marketing $1,400 = $6,000. Self-employment tax allowance: roughly 15.3 percent of net earnings (she rounds to $8,400; her accountant will refine it). Total to cover: $69,400. Working weeks: 47. Working hours per week: 40. Billable share: 60 percent. Billable hours: 47 x 40 x 0.6 = 1,128. Target rate: $69,400 / 1,128 = $61.52 per hour. She sets packages accordingly: Starter, up to 10 hours per month, $650 ($65 per hour); Standard, up to 20 hours, $1,200 ($60 per hour); Priority, up to 35 hours with same-day response, $2,000 ($57 per hour). Additional hours: $70. These numbers are illustrative; tax outcomes vary by individual circumstances.
:::

:::case Martell's Buyback Rate
In a 2025 video, Dan Martell described calculating what an owner's time is worth by dividing annual income from the business by 2,000 hours, then setting a "buyback rate" at one quarter of that figure; tasks that can be outsourced below that rate are candidates for delegation [5]. For a business owner taking home $100,000, that implies about $50 per hour and a buyback rate of $12.50 [5]. A VA can use this framework to understand buyers: an owner earning $200,000 might see value in delegating tasks costing up to about $25 per hour by this rule, while specialized or higher-impact work can justify more. Martell's framework is a heuristic, not an industry standard, and VAs should price from their own costs first.
:::

# Chapter 5: Finding First Clients

:::objectives
- Use warm networks and referrals to find first clients.
- Evaluate freelance marketplaces realistically.
- Conduct discovery calls that qualify clients.
- Recognize and avoid scams targeting remote workers.
:::

The first clients are the hardest to find, because the business has no track record. The fastest route is usually through people who already know your work.

## Step 1: Warm Network

Tell former colleagues, managers, vendors, and friends what you now offer and for whom. Be specific: "I am helping small trade contractors with email, scheduling, and quote follow-up. Do you know an owner who is drowning in admin?"

## Step 2: Industry Communities

Join associations, local business groups, and online communities where your niche gathers. Contribute useful answers before promoting your services.

## Step 3: Marketplaces

Freelance marketplaces can provide first clients and reviews, but they involve fees and competition. Upwork's fees range from 0 to 15 percent [6]. Use marketplaces as one channel, with a specific profile that reflects your niche, rather than competing on price for generic tasks.

## Step 4: Discovery Calls

A discovery call qualifies the client and gathers information for a proposal. Ask about current pain points, tools, volume of work, expectations for response time, sensitive information involved, and budget. Listen more than you talk.

## Step 5: Proposals

Send a short proposal: the client's situation in their words, the recommended package, scope card summary, price, start date, and next step.

## Avoiding Scams

Remote workers are frequent targets of fraud. The FTC warns about work-from-home job scams and advises that legitimate employers do not ask you to pay for a job, buy equipment with your own money for reimbursement, or deposit checks and send money back [7]. Be cautious of clients who pay with overpayments, ask you to buy gift cards, or request your financial account access.

:::example First Ninety Days of Outreach (illustrative)
Monique sends personal messages to 40 people in her network, posts once a week in two contractor groups, and creates a niche-specific marketplace profile. Results after 90 days: 14 conversations, 8 discovery calls, 5 proposals, 3 clients (two Standard packages, one Starter). Monthly recurring revenue: 2 x $1,200 + $650 = $3,050. Two clients came from former colleagues; one came from a contractor group. The marketplace produced one inquiry, which turned out to be a check-overpayment scam she recognized and declined.
:::

:::case Chris Ducker: Building a VA Recruiting Business
Chris Ducker, a British entrepreneur living in the Philippines, writes that he founded Virtual Staff Finder in mid-2010 as a recruitment hub matching entrepreneurs with experienced Filipino virtual assistants, that it helped more than 8,000 VAs get hired, and that he exited the company fully in late 2024 [21]. He describes earlier building a call center business that grew to more than 400 staff [21]. The case illustrates both the scale of demand from small business owners for remote assistance and the global competition that VAs face. U.S.-based VAs often compete on niche expertise, time-zone alignment, and communication rather than price alone. Ducker's results are his own.

# Chapter 6: Agreements and the Business Relationship

:::objectives
- Set up the basic legal and administrative foundations of a VA business.
- Understand the distinction between independent contractors and employees.
- Identify the key elements of a client services agreement.
- Protect confidentiality and define liability.
:::

A VA business is a business, not a job. Treating it professionally from the start protects both the assistant and the client.

## Business Structure and Registration

Most VAs begin as sole proprietors or form a single-member LLC. The SBA explains the tradeoffs among structures, including liability protection and tax treatment [9]. The IRS provides free Employer Identification Numbers (EINs), which many sole proprietors use instead of their Social Security numbers on client forms [10]. Check state and local registration and licensing requirements.

## Insurance

The SBA describes business insurance types, including general liability and professional liability [11]. Professional liability (errors and omissions) insurance may be relevant for VAs who handle important client tasks. Cyber insurance may be worth considering if you access client systems.

## Independent Contractor Status

The IRS states that it is critical to determine correctly whether workers are employees or independent contractors and that, generally, a worker is an employee if the business can control what will be done and how it will be done [8]. Independent VAs typically control how they perform the work, use their own tools, serve multiple clients, and invoice for services. A client who sets your hours, supervises your methods closely, and treats you as staff may be creating an employment relationship. This matters for taxes and legal protections on both sides.

## The Services Agreement

A written agreement should cover:

- Services and scope (referencing scope cards).
- Fees, billing schedule, and payment terms.
- Term, renewal, and termination notice.
- Confidentiality and data handling.
- Ownership of work product.
- Access and security responsibilities.
- Limitation of liability.
- Independent contractor relationship.
- Dispute resolution and governing law.

Have an attorney review your template once; then use it consistently.

:::example Agreement Terms (illustrative)
Monique's template, reviewed by an attorney for $750, specifies: monthly packages billed in advance on the first of the month; payment due within 7 days; unused hours do not roll over; 30 days' written notice to terminate; confidentiality obligations that survive termination; client owns all work product upon payment; Monique uses her own equipment and sets her own working methods; liability is limited to fees paid in the prior three months. She also includes a clause stating that she will not make payments, sign documents, or agree to terms on the client's behalf without written approval.
:::

:::case Classification in Practice: BELAY's Model
BELAY's published history describes a business built on remote, part-time professionals serving many clients [3]. Any business that grows from a solo practice into a team must make classification decisions about the people who deliver the work. The IRS emphasizes that classification depends on the actual relationship, including control over the work [8]. For a VA considering growth, the lesson is to make these decisions deliberately, with professional advice, rather than by default.
:::

| Agreement element | Purpose | Example clause topic |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Defines work | Services per approved scope cards |
| Fees and payment | Ensures cash flow | Billed monthly in advance |
| Term and termination | Sets exit rules | 30 days' notice |
| Confidentiality | Protects client information | Survives termination |
| Work product | Clarifies ownership | Client owns upon payment |
| Liability | Caps exposure | Limited to recent fees |
| Contractor status | Clarifies relationship | Own tools and methods |

# Chapter 7: Secure Onboarding with Least Access

:::objectives
- Apply the principle of least privilege to client access.
- Use delegated access, password managers, and multifactor authentication.
- Protect client information on your own devices.
- Offboard cleanly when an engagement ends.
:::

Clients trust a VA with their inbox, calendar, documents, and sometimes customer and financial information. A security failure can harm the client and end the business relationship. Good security habits are also a selling point: clients notice when an assistant handles access carefully.

## Principle of Least Privilege

Grant only the access needed for the task, for only as long as needed. The FTC's Start with Security guide advises businesses to control access to data sensibly, restricting access to those who need it [12]. For a VA, this means using delegated or role-based access rather than the client's own login wherever possible.

## Delegated Access

Many email, calendar, and document systems allow a user to grant another person access to specific resources without sharing a password. Use these features. If a system does not support delegation, use a shared password manager vault controlled by the client.

## Multifactor Authentication

CISA, the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, advises businesses to require multifactor authentication (MFA), explaining that strong passwords alone are no longer enough and that MFA adds a layer of protection by requiring two or more ways to verify identity [13]. Use MFA on your own accounts and support clients in enabling it on theirs.

## Your Own Devices

The FTC's cybersecurity guidance for small businesses covers basics such as keeping software updated, securing devices and networks, and training on phishing [14]. For a VA: keep devices updated, use full-disk encryption and screen locks, separate work and personal accounts, avoid public Wi-Fi without protection, and never store client data on unapproved services.

## Onboarding Steps

1. Agree on the systems and access needed for each scope card.
2. Client grants delegated access or shares credentials through a password manager.
3. Confirm MFA on all accounts.
4. Record the access in an access log.
5. Test access with a low-risk task.

## Offboarding

When an engagement ends, return or delete client data as agreed, remove your access, ask the client to revoke delegations and rotate any shared credentials, and record the offboarding in the access log.

:::example Access Log (illustrative)
For her plumbing client, Monique's access log lists: email (delegated access, granted March 3, MFA confirmed); calendar (delegated, March 3); scheduling software (individual user account with dispatcher role, not owner role, March 5); shared drive (one folder, edit rights, March 5); accounting software (none; she sends invoice drafts for the client to approve). When the client asks her to pay a supplier invoice, she declines to accept payment access and instead prepares a payment summary for him to execute, consistent with her agreement.
:::

:::case Security as a Professional Standard
The FTC's Start with Security guide draws lessons from its enforcement actions, emphasizing practices such as limiting access to data, requiring secure passwords and authentication, and securing remote access [12]. CISA's guidance on MFA reflects the same direction [13]. These sources are written for businesses generally, but they apply directly to VAs, who often have access to multiple clients' systems. A VA who follows them can explain their practices confidently to prospective clients.

# Chapter 8: Handoffs, SOPs, and Communication

:::objectives
- Build a handoff queue that captures every client request.
- Write standard operating procedures for recurring tasks.
- Choose communication rhythms that suit remote work.
- Use asynchronous communication effectively.
:::

A VA's reliability depends less on effort than on systems. Requests arrive by email, text, voice messages, and chat. Without a single place to capture and track them, tasks get lost. Without documented procedures, quality varies. Without agreed communication rhythms, clients feel either neglected or interrupted.

## The Handoff Queue

A handoff queue is a single list, board, or project tool where every request is recorded with: date received, requester, description, due date, priority, status, and any approvals needed. The client should know how to add requests, and the VA should move any request received elsewhere into the queue.

## Standard Operating Procedures

An SOP describes how a recurring task is done: purpose, trigger, steps, tools, quality checks, and escalation rules. Write an SOP the second time you perform a task. SOPs make work consistent, allow you to take time off with a backup, and are the foundation for later delegation to subcontractors.

## Communication Rhythms

Agree with each client on:

- **Channels.** Which channel for which purpose (for example, the queue for requests, chat for quick questions, phone for urgent issues).
- **Response times.** Matched to the package.
- **Regular check-ins.** A weekly 15-minute call or written update.
- **Escalation.** What counts as urgent and how to reach the client.

## Asynchronous Communication

GitLab, which operates as an all-remote company, describes asynchronous communication as a way to work without requiring people to be available at the same time, and its handbook encourages documentation and written updates over meetings where possible [15]. For VAs, asynchronous habits include writing complete messages (context, question, options, recommendation), batching non-urgent questions, and documenting decisions in the queue.

:::example A Weekly Rhythm (illustrative)
Monique's Standard clients receive: a shared task board where they add requests; a daily end-of-day summary at 4 p.m. listing completed, pending, and flagged items; a Friday written update covering the week's metrics (emails processed, estimates scheduled, quotes followed up) and next week's priorities; and a 15-minute Monday call. Urgent issues (an angry customer or a same-day emergency job) go by phone. In the first month, her plumbing client sends 61 requests by text; by month three, 85 percent arrive through the board, and Monique spends about two fewer hours per week reconstructing requests.
:::

:::case GitLab: Documentation as Infrastructure
GitLab's handbook explains how the company runs as an all-remote organization, emphasizing written communication, a single source of truth, and asynchronous work over meetings [15]. Few VAs operate at that scale, but the principles transfer directly. A handoff queue is a single source of truth for client requests; SOPs are documentation that lets work continue without the original person; and written updates reduce the need for meetings. GitLab's practices reflect its specific context and are cited here for their principles.

# Chapter 9: Quality, Errors, and Reporting

:::objectives
- Define quality standards for each service.
- Use checklists and review steps to prevent errors.
- Handle mistakes transparently and learn from them.
- Report results in terms clients value.
:::

Clients judge a VA by reliability: work done correctly, on time, without reminders. Quality is designed through standards and checks, not left to memory.

## Quality Standards

For each scope card, define what good looks like: accuracy, timeliness, tone, and format. For example, "All customer inquiries receive a reply within four business hours using approved templates, with correct names and job details."

## Prevention

- **Checklists** for recurring tasks.
- **Review steps** before anything leaves your hands to a third party (customers, vendors).
- **Approval gates** for high-impact actions: pricing, payments, contracts, sensitive communications.
- **Templates** for routine messages, approved by the client.

## Handling Errors

Errors happen. Handle them in four steps: (1) notify the client promptly and plainly; (2) fix the immediate problem; (3) find the cause; (4) change the process so it does not recur, and update the SOP. Clients usually forgive errors that are reported honestly and fixed systematically; they rarely forgive concealment.

## Reporting

Report outcomes the client values: inquiries answered, appointments booked, quotes followed up, invoices sent, overdue payments collected, and hours saved. Avoid reporting only activity.

:::example An Error Review (illustrative)
Monique schedules a customer estimate on a day her client had blocked for a family event, because the block was on his personal calendar, which she cannot see. She notifies him within ten minutes of noticing, reschedules the customer with an apology, and proposes a fix: he will mark personal blocks on the work calendar as "busy" without details. She adds a step to her scheduling SOP: confirm any weekend or evening appointment by text before booking. No similar error occurs in the following three months.
:::

:::case Delegation and Trust
Dan Martell has described hiring an executive assistant as a major source of leverage for founders [4]. Leverage depends on trust: an owner will delegate more as reliability is demonstrated. A VA who reports errors transparently, fixes processes, and reports outcomes earns that trust and, often, a larger scope of work. Martell's perspective is offered as context on how owners think about delegation, not as evidence of any VA's results.

# Chapter 10: Using AI Tools Responsibly

:::objectives
- Identify VA tasks where AI tools can help.
- Protect client confidentiality when using AI.
- Review AI output before it reaches clients or third parties.
- Disclose AI use appropriately.
:::

AI tools can draft emails, summarize documents, transcribe calls, and organize information. Used carefully, they can make a VA faster and more consistent. Used carelessly, they can expose confidential information or produce errors under a client's name.

## Where AI Helps

Drafting routine replies from approved templates; summarizing long email threads; transcribing and summarizing meetings (with participants' consent); formatting documents; creating first drafts of SOPs; and organizing research.

## Confidentiality

Before entering client information into any AI tool, check the tool's data policies and your client agreement. Some tools may retain or use inputs. Use business-grade tools with appropriate data protections, avoid entering sensitive personal, financial, or health information, and get the client's agreement for any use of AI with their data.

## Review Everything

AI output can be wrong, incomplete, or inappropriate in tone. Review every AI-assisted output before it goes to the client or any third party. You remain responsible for the work.

## Disclosure

Tell clients how you use AI in your work and agree on boundaries. Some clients welcome it; others prohibit it for certain information.

:::example AI-Assisted Inbox Summary (illustrative)
With her client's written agreement, Monique uses a business-grade AI tool to summarize long vendor email threads. She removes customer phone numbers and addresses before summarizing, reviews each summary against the original thread, and corrects one summary that misstated a delivery date. Her daily summary time drops from about 40 minutes to 25 minutes. She records the AI use policy in the client's onboarding file.
:::

:::case Security Guidance Applies to AI Too
The FTC's Start with Security guide emphasizes not collecting or keeping personal information you do not need and controlling who can access data [12]. Those principles apply when using AI tools: share only what is necessary, use tools whose data handling you understand, and limit access. This is an application of general guidance to a new kind of tool, not a specific FTC rule about AI assistants.
:::

| AI use | Benefit | Safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Drafting replies | Speed | Client-approved templates; human review |
| Summarizing threads | Time saving | Remove sensitive data; verify facts |
| Meeting transcription | Accurate notes | Participants' consent |
| SOP drafting | Faster documentation | Review against actual practice |

:::checklist AI Use Policy
- Client agreement on AI use recorded.
- Tools' data policies reviewed.
- Sensitive information excluded or protected.
- Every output reviewed by a human before use.
- Consent obtained for recording or transcription.
- AI use described in onboarding documents.
:::

:::summary
AI tools can speed drafting, summarizing, and documentation, but VAs must protect confidentiality, review all output, and agree with clients on how AI is used. General security guidance, such as limiting data shared and controlling access, applies directly to AI tools.
:::

# Chapter 11: Taxes, Records, and Money

:::objectives
- Understand self-employment and estimated tax obligations.
- Keep records that support income and deductions.
- Separate business and personal finances.
- Plan cash flow for a service business.
:::

Self-employed VAs are responsible for their own taxes and records. Getting these basics right avoids penalties and makes it easier to see whether the business is profitable.

## Self-Employment Tax

The IRS explains that self-employed individuals generally pay self-employment tax, consisting of Social Security and Medicare taxes, at 15.3 percent on net earnings, in addition to income tax [16].

## Estimated Taxes

Because no employer withholds taxes, self-employed people generally must make quarterly estimated tax payments if they expect to owe a certain amount; the IRS explains the requirements and payment methods [17].

## Records

The IRS advises keeping records that support income, deductions, and credits, including receipts, invoices, and bank statements [18]. Use accounting software or a spreadsheet, record every invoice and payment, and save receipts for business expenses.

## Home Office

If you use part of your home regularly and exclusively for business, you may qualify for a home office deduction. The IRS simplified option allows $5 per square foot for up to 300 square feet [19].

## Forms from Clients

Clients who pay you as a contractor may issue Form 1099-NEC. IRS instructions indicate that the reporting threshold rises to $2,000 for payments made after 2025 [20]. Report all business income whether or not you receive a form.

## Cash Flow

Bill retainers in advance, set clear payment terms, and keep a reserve of at least one to three months of expenses. Set aside a percentage of each payment for taxes in a separate account.

:::example Monthly Tax Set-Aside (illustrative)
Monique's monthly revenue is $4,250 and her expenses are $500, for net earnings of $3,750. Her accountant suggests setting aside 25 percent of net earnings for federal self-employment and income tax, plus her state's rate. She transfers $940 for federal taxes, plus a separate amount for state tax, to a tax savings account on the first of each month and makes quarterly estimated payments from it. At year end, her accountant reviews actual liability; her set-aside covers it with $600 to spare. The percentage is illustrative; the right figure depends on individual circumstances.
:::

:::case The Scale of Self-Employment
The Census Bureau's count of 29.8 million nonemployer businesses in 2022 [22] and Upwork's estimate of 64 million Americans freelancing in 2023 [1] show that self-employment is common. The IRS maintains a Self-Employed Individuals Tax Center and specific guidance on self-employment tax, estimated taxes, and recordkeeping because these obligations apply to so many people [16][17][18]. A VA who learns these basics early is joining a large group of independent professionals with well-documented obligations.
:::

# Chapter 12: Renew, Expand, and Grow

:::objectives
- Conduct renewal reviews with clients.
- Expand scope responsibly.
- Decide whether to stay solo or build a team.
- Bring on subcontractors with appropriate systems and classification.
:::

Growth in a VA business can mean better clients, higher prices, broader scope, or a team. Each path requires different skills. This chapter covers the decisions.

## Renewal Reviews

Every quarter, review each client relationship: outcomes delivered, hours used, satisfaction, and fit. Propose adjustments: more hours, different tasks, or a price change.

## Expanding Scope

Expand into tasks that build on proven reliability and your skills. Each new task needs a scope card, SOP, and access review. Decline tasks that require licenses or expertise you lack.

## Solo or Team

Staying solo offers simplicity and control; income is limited by your hours and rates. Building a team offers capacity but requires recruiting, training, quality control, and management. BELAY's history shows the team path at scale: from one first assistant in 2010 to a company that reports serving more than 25,000 leaders [3]. Most VAs who build teams stay much smaller.

## Bringing on Subcontractors

If you bring on help:

- Start with documented SOPs.
- Classify workers correctly; the IRS emphasizes that control over the work determines status [8].
- Use written agreements with confidentiality terms.
- Give subcontractors least-privilege access and review their work.
- Tell clients who will work on their accounts, as your agreement requires.
- If you pay a contractor $2,000 or more for payments after 2025, you may need to issue Form 1099-NEC [20].

:::example Adding a Subcontractor (illustrative)
After a year, Monique has six clients and is at capacity. She hires a subcontractor at $30 per hour for 15 hours per week to handle inbox processing for two Standard clients, using her SOPs. Revenue from those clients: $2,400 per month. Subcontractor cost: about 65 hours x $30 = $1,950, leaving Monique about $450 per month from those accounts instead of $2,400. The roughly 40 hours a month she previously spent on those accounts (the subcontractor, still learning, takes longer) let her take on two new Standard clients at $1,200 each. Her total from the four accounts becomes $450 + $2,400 = $2,850 per month, about $450 more than before, minus her added management and review time. The gain is modest; the larger benefit is capacity to keep growing without working more hours. She informs both clients in writing and adds the subcontractor to her access log.
:::

Each stage required systems and leadership beyond doing the work itself. For a small VA practice, the lesson is that growth depends on documented processes, clear roles, and leadership capacity. BELAY's results are its own.

# Appendix K: Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need certification to be a VA?** No general license is required for administrative VA work in most places, but regulated tasks (such as tax preparation or certain financial services) may require credentials. Check requirements for any specialized work.

**Should I charge hourly or by package?** Many VAs start hourly to learn how long tasks take, then move to packages. Packages with clear limits often produce more predictable income.

**How many clients can one VA serve?** It depends on package sizes and task complexity. Four to eight retainer clients is a common range for a full-time solo VA, but there is no standard.

**What if a client asks me to do something unethical or unsafe?** Decline, explain why, and document the request. Your agreement should limit your authority to act on the client's behalf.

**Can I work for clients in other countries?** Yes, but consider time zones, payment methods, currency, contracts, and data protection rules that may apply.

# Glossary

**Access log.** A record of the systems a VA can access, when access was granted, and when it was revoked.

**Approval gate.** A step requiring client approval before a high-impact action.

**Asynchronous communication.** Communication that does not require both parties to be available at the same time.

**Billable share.** The percentage of working hours that can be billed to clients.

**Buyback rate.** In Dan Martell's framework, the hourly cost below which an owner should consider delegating work.

**Delegated access.** A feature letting one user act on another's account resources without sharing a password.

**Discovery call.** A conversation to understand a prospect's needs and assess fit.

**EIN.** Employer Identification Number issued by the IRS.

**Estimated taxes.** Quarterly tax payments made by people without withholding.

**Handoff queue.** A single list or board where all client requests are tracked.

**Independent contractor.** A self-employed person who controls how they perform work for clients.

**Least privilege.** Granting only the minimum access needed for a task.

**Multifactor authentication (MFA).** Verifying identity with two or more methods.

**Retainer.** A recurring fee for a defined amount of service or availability.

**Scope card.** A one-page description of a task's purpose, inputs, steps, outputs, decision rights, and finish condition.

**Scope creep.** The gradual expansion of work beyond what was agreed.

**Self-employment tax.** Social Security and Medicare taxes paid by self-employed individuals.
