# How to Use This Book

A podcast is one of the few marketing channels in which a prospective customer will voluntarily spend twenty or thirty minutes listening to you explain how you think. That is a remarkable form of attention, and it is the reason so many owners consider starting one. It is also the reason so many podcasts fail: the format rewards consistency, clarity, and genuine usefulness, and it punishes shows that are vague, irregular, or thinly disguised advertisements.

This book treats a podcast launch as a project with a beginning, a middle, and a measurable outcome. It draws on public technical requirements from Apple Podcasts, legal guidance from the Federal Trade Commission and the U.S. Copyright Office, audience research from Edison Research, and documented launches from real organizations, including the grocery chain Trader Joe's and the entrepreneur Pat Flynn. The audience data matters because it frames the opportunity realistically: Edison Research's Infinite Dial 2025 survey estimated that 55 percent of Americans age 12 and older consumed a podcast in the prior month ([Edison Research, The Infinite Dial 2025](https://www.edisonresearch.com/the-infinite-dial-2025/)). A large audience exists, but it is spread across an enormous number of shows, so a new podcast must earn attention one listener at a time.

## Who This Book Is For

- Small business owners who want a podcast to build trust with prospective customers.
- Consultants, coaches, and professionals who want to demonstrate expertise.
- Marketing staff asked to launch a company podcast with a modest budget.
- Anyone who has bought equipment but has not yet published an episode.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Decide whether a podcast fits your business goals and capacity.
- Define a target listener, a topic, and a one-sentence show promise.
- Choose a format, episode length, and publishing cadence you can sustain.
- Create a name, description, and artwork that meet directory requirements.
- Set up a simple recording space and equipment kit.
- Plan, record, and edit episodes to recognized loudness standards.
- Publish through a host and RSS feed to the major directories.
- Run a launch, grow an audience, and measure results honestly.
- Handle sponsorships, guest releases, and music in line with public guidance.

## How the Book Is Organized

The book has twelve chapters that follow the order of a real launch, from the strategic decision to measurement after the first season. Each chapter includes learning objectives, key definitions, a step-by-step framework, a reference table, a worked illustrative example, a worksheet or checklist, a summary, and review questions. Several chapters include case studies of real, publicly documented podcasts. The illustrative examples follow a hypothetical owner, Ken, who runs a small home remodeling company and is launching a show called The Remodel Room; Ken and his company are invented to demonstrate the method. The appendices contain a launch timeline, episode templates, guest outreach scripts, a show notes template, and a pre-publication checklist.

# Chapter 1: Should Your Business Start a Podcast?

:::objectives
- Describe the size and behavior of the U.S. podcast audience.
- Identify business goals a podcast can and cannot serve well.
- Estimate the time a weekly show requires.
- Make a go, wait, or no-go decision.
:::

## The Audience in Context

Podcast listening has grown steadily for two decades. Edison Research's Infinite Dial 2025 found that 73 percent of Americans 12 and older had consumed a podcast in audio or video form, an estimated 210 million people, and that 55 percent were monthly consumers ([Edison Research, The Infinite Dial 2025](https://www.edisonresearch.com/the-infinite-dial-2025/)). The same study reported that YouTube was the service used most often for podcasts among weekly listeners, at 33 percent. Edison's companion report estimated that weekly podcast listening time rose from 170 million hours in 2015 to 773 million hours in 2025 ([Edison Research, The Podcast Consumer 2025](https://www.edisonresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/The-Podcast-Consumer-2025-revised-FINAL.pdf)).

Two implications follow. First, many of your customers are likely already podcast listeners, so the habit does not need to be created. Second, video now matters: a show that publishes only audio may miss listeners who discover podcasts on YouTube.

## What a Podcast Does Well

A **podcast** is a series of episodic audio or video programs distributed through an RSS feed so listeners can subscribe and receive new episodes automatically. For a business, the format is strong at a few specific jobs:

| Goal | Fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Building trust before a sale | Strong | Long-form listening reveals expertise and personality |
| Educating existing customers | Strong | Answers recurring questions at scale |
| Building relationships with referral partners | Strong | Interviewing partners creates goodwill and shared content |
| Recruiting employees | Moderate | Shows culture and values |
| Generating immediate leads | Weak | Audiences grow slowly; results lag |
| Replacing paid advertising | Weak | Reach is usually small at first |

## What a Podcast Costs

The main cost is time, not equipment. A typical weekly 25-minute interview show requires time for planning and guest coordination, recording, editing (or reviewing an editor's work), writing show notes, and promotion. Owners commonly underestimate the total by half. Estimate honestly before committing.

| Task | Hours per episode (typical range) |
|---|---|
| Topic planning and research | 0.5 to 1.5 |
| Guest outreach and scheduling | 0.5 to 1 |
| Recording, including setup | 1 to 1.5 |
| Editing and mastering | 1 to 4 (less if outsourced) |
| Show notes, titles, artwork | 0.5 to 1 |
| Promotion and clips | 1 to 2 |
| Total | 4.5 to 11 |

These ranges are planning estimates for this book's method, not measured industry averages.

## The Decision

Use three questions. Do you have a defined listener who would benefit from regular audio? Can you commit the hours for at least 12 episodes? Will you judge success over 6 to 12 months rather than weeks? Three yes answers suggest go. One or two suggest wait and fix the gap. None suggests choosing a different channel.

:::case Trader Joe's: A Five-Episode Experiment That Became a Long-Running Show
In May 2018, the grocery chain Trader Joe's launched Inside Trader Joe's, which it announced as a five-part series featuring crew members and executives, including the company's CEO, discussing how the retailer does business and answering customers' frequent questions ([Trader Joe's press release via PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/trader-joes-launches-first-ever-podcast-series-300639639.html)). A company spokesperson described the show as an extension of the company's communication with customers, and a co-host promised it would not be a "long ad" ([LA Magazine](https://lamag.com/pop-culture/trader-joes-podcast/); [iDrop News](https://www.idropnews.com/news/trader-joes-podcast/70724/)). In a 2026 episode, the company said it began the podcast "thinking it would be a five-episode opportunity" and was now "eight years and more than 100 episodes later" ([Apple Podcasts, Inside Trader Joe's](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/inside-trader-joes/id1375630453)). The lesson for smaller businesses is the structure, not the scale: a limited, clearly defined first season lowered the commitment and let the company learn before continuing. Trader Joe's results are its own, not TTI's.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Decision
Ken owns a remodeling company with 11 employees. Prospective clients often ask the same questions about budgets, permits, and timelines, and many hesitate for months before signing. Ken's goal is to build trust before the first consultation. He estimates 6 hours per episode, which he can sustain every other week if his office manager handles scheduling and an editor handles post-production. He commits to a first season of 10 episodes and will judge results after 9 months. Decision: go.
:::

# Chapter 2: Listener, Topic, and Promise

:::objectives
- Define a specific target listener.
- Choose a topic area with enough depth for many episodes.
- Write a one-sentence show promise.
- Test the promise against real customer questions.
:::

## The Target Listener

A **target listener** is a specific description of the person you most want to serve. "Homeowners" is too broad. "Homeowners in our county, ages 35 to 65, planning a kitchen or bathroom remodel in the next 12 months" is useful. The narrower the description, the easier it is to choose topics, guests, and language that feel personally relevant.

Describe the listener in five dimensions:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Situation | What are they trying to do or decide? |
| Problem | What frustrates or worries them? |
| Knowledge | What do they already know, and what confuses them? |
| Context | When and where will they listen (commute, workout, job site)? |
| Next step | What would you like them to do after listening? |

## Choosing a Topic Area

A good topic area sits at the intersection of three circles: what your listener needs, what you know deeply, and what connects to your business. It also needs depth. A quick test is to list 30 possible episode titles in one sitting. If you cannot reach 30, the topic may be too narrow; if every title feels generic, it may be too broad.

## The Show Promise

A **show promise** is a single sentence that tells a prospective listener who the show is for, what they will get, and why it is worth their time. Use this template:

"[Show name] helps [target listener] [achieve outcome or solve problem] through [format], so they can [benefit]."

Example: "The Remodel Room helps homeowners planning a remodel understand budgets, permits, and timelines through short conversations with builders and designers, so they can make confident decisions and avoid expensive surprises."

The promise guides every later decision. If a proposed episode does not serve the promise, it does not belong in the show.

## Testing the Promise

Collect 20 real questions from customers, prospects, or staff. Sort them into groups. If most questions fit your promise, the promise matches real demand. If many fall outside it, adjust the promise or the listener definition.

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Listener and Promise
Ken's office manager pulls 40 questions from emails and consultation notes. The largest clusters are budgets (11), permits and inspections (8), timelines and delays (7), choosing materials (6), and living through a remodel (5). Ken's target listener is "a homeowner in our region planning a kitchen, bath, or addition within the next year." His promise, quoted above, covers 37 of the 40 questions.
:::

:::worksheet Listener and Promise

Show promise ("___ helps ___ ___ through ___, so they can ___"): ___

:::

:::summary
A specific target listener, a deep topic area, and a one-sentence show promise form the foundation of a podcast. Test the topic by listing 30 episode ideas and test the promise against 20 real customer questions. Use the promise to accept or reject every future episode idea.

# Chapter 3: Format, Length, and Cadence

:::objectives
- Compare common podcast formats.
- Choose an episode length suited to your listener.
- Set a publishing cadence you can sustain.
- Decide between seasons and an ongoing schedule.
:::

## Common Formats

| Format | Description | Strengths | Challenges |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solo | One host teaches or comments | Full control; easy scheduling | Requires strong preparation; can feel flat |
| Co-hosted | Two regular hosts converse | Natural energy; shared workload | Requires chemistry and coordination |
| Interview | Host interviews a different guest each episode | Built-in variety; guest networks | Scheduling; quality depends on guests |
| Panel | Several guests discuss a topic | Multiple viewpoints | Harder to edit; crosstalk |
| Narrative or documentary | Scripted, produced storytelling | High engagement | Time-intensive |
| Q&A | Host answers listener questions | Directly serves audience needs | Needs a steady question supply |

Many business shows combine formats, such as interviews most weeks with a short solo Q&A episode monthly.

## Episode Length

There is no universally correct length. Match length to your listener's context and the content's needs. A homeowner listening during a 20-minute commute may prefer episodes that fit the drive. Apple's documentation does not set a required episode length; the right length is the one your listener finishes. Plan a target range, such as 20 to 30 minutes, and edit to remove anything that does not serve the promise.

## Cadence

**Cadence** is the publishing schedule: weekly, every other week, or monthly. Consistency matters more than frequency. Pat Flynn, whose show is discussed in Chapter 10, described starting at once every other week before moving to weekly after he saw how strongly listeners responded ([Smart Passive Income, Episode 716](https://www.smartpassiveincome.com/podcasts/spi-716-moment-podcasting-was-going-to-change-my-life/)). Start with a schedule you are confident you can keep, and increase it only when you have a buffer of finished episodes.

## Seasons Versus Ongoing

A **season** is a defined set of episodes released over a period, followed by a planned break. Seasons reduce burnout, allow a themed arc, and create natural review points. Apple's RSS specification includes dedicated tags for episode and season numbers ([Apple, A Podcaster's Guide to RSS](https://help.apple.com/itc/podcasts_connect/en.lproj/itcb54353390.html)), and an episodic or serial show type, so directories can display seasons properly.

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Format
Ken chooses an interview format with a solo Q&A episode every fourth release. Target length: 22 to 28 minutes. Cadence: every other Tuesday. Season one: 10 episodes, with 3 recorded before launch so he has a buffer.
:::

:::checklist Format Decisions
- Format chosen and matched to my strengths and time.
- Target length range set based on listener context.
- Cadence chosen that I can sustain for a full season.
- Season length defined, with a planned break.
- Buffer of at least 2 to 3 finished episodes planned before launch.
:::

:::summary
Choose a format that fits your strengths and time; interview and solo formats are the most common for businesses. Set length by listener context and edit tightly. Pick a cadence you can keep, build a buffer, and consider seasons to manage workload and create review points.
:::

# Chapter 4: Name, Description, and Artwork

:::objectives
- Choose a clear, searchable show name.
- Write a show description that serves both listeners and search.
- Create artwork that meets Apple Podcasts specifications.
- Avoid common rejection causes.
:::

## The Show Name

Apple's RSS guide advises a clear, concise, specific title and notes that a show called "Our Community Bulletin" is too vague to attract many subscribers. It also warns that Apple uses the title field for search and that stuffing it with keywords "in an attempt to game podcast search" can lead to removal from the directory ([Apple, A Podcaster's Guide to RSS](https://help.apple.com/itc/podcasts_connect/en.lproj/itcb54353390.html)).

Criteria for a strong name:

| Criterion | Test |
|---|---|
| Clear | A stranger can guess the topic |
| Short | Fits on artwork at thumbnail size |
| Distinct | Not confusingly similar to an existing show |
| Speakable | Easy to say and spell aloud |
| Honest | Not keyword-stuffed |

Search the major directories for your candidate name before committing.

## The Description

The show description tells a prospective listener what to expect. Apple allows up to 4,000 bytes of text in the description tag ([Apple, A Podcaster's Guide to RSS](https://help.apple.com/itc/podcasts_connect/en.lproj/itcb54353390.html)). Structure it as follows: the show promise in the first sentence; who hosts and why they are credible; what a typical episode includes; the schedule; and an invitation to subscribe.

## Artwork Specifications

Apple's current specification for show cover art is 3000 by 3000 pixels preferred, with RSS-delivered artwork accepted from 1400 by 1400 up to 3000 by 3000 pixels, in PNG or JPG format, with no transparency ([Apple Podcasts for Creators, Show Cover](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/5514-show-cover-template)). The RSS guide adds that artwork should be 72 dpi, in the RGB color space, without an alpha channel, and effective at both full and thumbnail size ([Apple, A Podcaster's Guide to RSS](https://help.apple.com/itc/podcasts_connect/en.lproj/itcb54353390.html)).

## Design Principles

- Test the design at the size of a postage stamp; most people first see it tiny.
- Use few words: the show name, perhaps a short subtitle.
- Use high contrast between text and background.
- Avoid fine detail that disappears when small.
- Make it consistent with your business brand without copying your logo alone.
- Use only images you have rights to, and avoid platform logos.

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Branding
Name: The Remodel Room. Ken searches directories and finds no show with the same name. Description (opening): "The Remodel Room helps homeowners planning a remodel understand budgets, permits, and timelines through short conversations with builders and designers. Hosted by Ken, owner of a regional remodeling company with 20 years in the trade, each episode answers one question homeowners ask before they hire. New episodes every other Tuesday." Artwork: a 3000 by 3000 pixel PNG with the show name in bold white letters on a deep blue background and a simple line drawing of a doorway, checked at thumbnail size on a phone.
:::

:::checklist Branding and Artwork
- Name is clear, short, distinct, speakable, and not keyword-stuffed.
- Name searched in major directories.
- Description opens with the show promise and stays under 4,000 bytes.
- Artwork is square, 3000 by 3000 pixels (minimum 1400 by 1400).
- Artwork is PNG or JPG, RGB, 72 dpi, no transparency.
- Artwork tested at thumbnail size.
- All images and fonts are licensed or original.
:::

:::summary
Choose a clear, specific, honest name and avoid keyword stuffing, which Apple warns can lead to removal. Write a description that opens with your show promise. Create square artwork at 3000 by 3000 pixels in RGB PNG or JPG without transparency, designed to read clearly at thumbnail size.
:::

# Chapter 5: Equipment and Recording Space

:::objectives
- Assemble a basic, reliable recording kit.
- Choose between USB and XLR microphones.
- Prepare a room that sounds good.
- Plan for remote guests and video.
:::

## Start Simple

Sound quality depends more on the room and microphone technique than on expensive equipment. A modest microphone in a quiet, furnished room usually sounds better than a premium microphone in an empty kitchen with hard surfaces. Buy the minimum reliable kit, record several test episodes, and upgrade only to fix a specific problem you can hear.

## The Basic Kit

| Item | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Microphone | Captures your voice | Dynamic microphones reject more room noise than condenser microphones in untreated rooms |
| Headphones | Monitor your sound | Closed-back models reduce bleed into the microphone |
| Microphone arm or stand | Holds the microphone at a steady distance | Reduces handling noise |
| Pop filter or foam cover | Reduces bursts from "p" and "b" sounds | Inexpensive and effective |
| Audio interface (XLR only) | Connects XLR microphones to the computer | Not needed for USB microphones |
| Recording software | Captures and edits audio | Many free and paid options exist |
| Webcam or camera (optional) | Records video for YouTube | Consider given YouTube's role in podcast listening |

## USB Versus XLR

A **USB microphone** plugs directly into a computer and is simple to set up. An **XLR microphone** connects through an audio interface or mixer, which adds cost and complexity but allows more flexibility and easier upgrades. For a solo owner starting out, USB is usually sufficient. For a co-hosted in-person show, XLR microphones through an interface allow each host to be recorded on a separate track.

## Preparing the Room

Hard surfaces reflect sound and create echo. Soft surfaces absorb it. Choose a small room with carpet, curtains, upholstered furniture, or full bookshelves. A walk-in closet full of clothes is a well-known improvised booth. Turn off fans, heating and air conditioning noise if possible, refrigerators nearby, and notifications. Record a 10-second sample of silence and listen for hum or hiss.

## Microphone Technique

- Keep a consistent distance, often about a fist's width from a dynamic microphone; follow your microphone's guidance.
- Speak across the microphone slightly rather than directly into it to reduce plosives.
- Avoid touching the desk or stand while recording.
- Keep water nearby and avoid dairy before recording if it affects your voice.

## Remote Guests

Many interviews are recorded remotely. Remote recording platforms can record each participant locally and upload separate high-quality tracks, which avoids the quality loss of recording a video call. Ask guests to use headphones, sit in a quiet room, and use a wired internet connection if possible. Send these instructions in advance (see Appendix C).

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Setup
Ken converts a small office with carpet and a full bookcase into his recording room. He buys a dynamic USB microphone, closed-back headphones, a desk arm, and a foam cover. For video, he uses a webcam mounted above his monitor. He hangs a moving blanket on the bare wall behind his desk to reduce echo. His test recording reveals a faint hum from a nearby server closet, so he records on Tuesday mornings when he can power down the noisy equipment.
:::

:::checklist Equipment and Room
- Microphone suited to my room (dynamic for untreated rooms).
- Closed-back headphones for monitoring.
- Arm or stand and pop filter installed.
- Room chosen with soft surfaces; noise sources identified and controlled.
- Ten-second silence test recorded and reviewed.
- Remote guest instructions written.
- Video setup decided (yes or no).
:::

:::summary
Room and technique matter more than expensive gear. Start with a reliable microphone, closed-back headphones, a stand, and a pop filter, and choose a small room with soft surfaces. USB microphones suit most solo starters; XLR setups offer flexibility for multiple hosts. Plan remote guest recording and consider video.
:::

# Chapter 6: Planning Episodes and Booking Guests

:::objectives
- Build a season plan from your show promise.
- Outline a solo episode and an interview episode.
- Find, invite, and prepare guests.
- Obtain recording consent and a guest release.
:::

## The Season Plan

A **season plan** lists every episode in the season with its working title, format, guest (if any), and the listener question it answers. Start from the question clusters gathered in Chapter 2. Sequence episodes so that early ones address the most common and urgent questions; first impressions determine whether new listeners subscribe.

## Episode Outlines

A solo episode outline:

1. Hook (30 to 60 seconds): the question and why it matters.
2. Introduction (30 seconds): show name, host, promise.
3. Main content (three to five points), each with an example.
4. Recap (one minute).
5. Call to action: one specific next step.

An interview episode outline:

1. Hook: a compelling quote or question from the interview.
2. Introduction of the guest and why they are qualified.
3. Five to seven prepared questions, ordered from context to specifics to advice.
4. A closing question, such as "What is the one thing a listener should do this week?"
5. Where to find the guest; call to action.

## Finding Guests

Good guests for a business show include customers (with permission), referral partners, suppliers, industry experts, local officials, and authors. Prioritize guests who can serve your listener, not just guests with large followings. Each guest should answer one clear listener question.

## Preparing Guests

Send a confirmation that includes the date, time, length, recording platform, technical instructions, the topic and sample questions, and how and when the episode will be published. A short pre-interview call of 10 to 15 minutes improves conversation quality.

## Consent and Releases

Recording laws differ by state. The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press explains that federal law requires the consent of at least one party, while about 11 states primarily require the consent of all parties to a conversation, with some states applying different rules to phone calls and in-person conversations ([Reporters Committee, Introduction to the Reporter's Recording Guide](https://www.rcfp.org/introduction-to-reporters-recording-guide/)). Because guests may be in other states, the safe practice for a podcast is to obtain every participant's consent and to record that consent at the start of the session. A written **guest release** should also confirm that the guest permits you to publish, edit, and promote the recording. Have an attorney review your release template.

# Chapter 7: Recording Sessions That Go Smoothly

:::objectives
- Follow a pre-recording checklist.
- Conduct a natural, focused interview.
- Capture backup recordings.
- Handle mistakes without stopping the flow.
:::

## Before You Press Record

A consistent routine prevents most problems. Ten minutes before the session: close unnecessary programs, silence phones, check input levels, confirm the recording platform is set to record separate tracks, and put water nearby. Check that your headphones are plugged in and that the microphone selected in software is the correct one; recording with a laptop's built-in microphone by mistake is a common error.

## The Interview

- **Open warmly.** Spend two minutes chatting before recording to put the guest at ease.
- **Record consent.** "Before we begin, can you confirm you agree to be recorded for the podcast?"
- **Listen more than you talk.** Follow up on interesting answers rather than racing to the next prepared question.
- **Ask for specifics.** "Can you give an example?" turns general advice into memorable stories.
- **Watch the time.** Leave room for the closing question.
- **Close gracefully.** Thank the guest, confirm the publication date, and explain how you will share the episode.

## Backups

Equipment and software fail. Use at least one backup: a second recording program, a local recording on the guest's side, or a separate recorder. Losing a guest interview damages a relationship and your schedule.

## Handling Mistakes

If you stumble, pause for two seconds and repeat the sentence. The pause creates a visible gap in the waveform that makes editing easy. Some hosts clap once to mark a spot. Do not stop the conversation for minor errors; you can edit them later.

:::case Pat Flynn: Starting Before Feeling Ready
Pat Flynn, founder of Smart Passive Income, has said publicly that he bought recording equipment in December 2008 but did not publish his first episode until July 2010 because he was "way too scared" ([Smart Passive Income, Episode 716](https://www.smartpassiveincome.com/podcasts/spi-716-moment-podcasting-was-going-to-change-my-life/)). In the first episode's transcript, he noted he had been talking about the podcast since late 2008 ([SPI Episode 1 transcript](https://www.smartpassiveincome.com/download/transcript-SPI001.pdf)). He describes his early episodes as nervous and timid. The lesson for new hosts is that recording skill improves with repetition, not with more preparation time. Flynn's experiences and results are his own, not TTI's.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Interview with the Building Inspector
Ken records with a retired county inspector over a remote recording platform. Before recording, he asks her to put on headphones and close her door. After recording her consent, he asks his prepared questions but follows up when she mentions that most failed inspections involve the same three issues. That tangent becomes the most useful part of the episode. When Ken mispronounces a code section, he pauses and repeats it. His backup recorder captures the session when the platform briefly disconnects.
:::

:::checklist Recording Session
- Phone silenced; programs closed; notifications off.
- Correct microphone selected in software; levels checked.
- Separate tracks enabled; backup recording running.
- Guest wearing headphones in a quiet room.
- Consent recorded at the start.
- Prepared questions and closing question at hand.
- Mistakes marked with a pause; session not stopped for minor errors.
- Guest thanked; publication date confirmed.
:::

:::summary
A consistent pre-recording routine prevents most technical problems. Open warmly, record consent, listen closely, ask for specifics, and close gracefully. Always run a backup recording, and mark mistakes with a pause rather than stopping. Pat Flynn's delayed start illustrates that skill comes from repetition.

# Chapter 8: Editing, Loudness, and Show Notes

:::objectives
- Follow an efficient editing workflow.
- Master audio to Apple's recommended loudness.
- Export files in accepted formats.
- Write show notes and episode titles.
- Use music legally.
:::

## The Editing Workflow

1. **Rough cut.** Remove false starts, long silences, off-topic tangents, and anything the guest asked to remove.
2. **Content edit.** Tighten the structure; move the strongest moment to the hook if useful.
3. **Clean up.** Reduce background noise, remove distracting sounds, and balance levels between speakers.
4. **Add elements.** Intro, outro, music beds, and any announcements.
5. **Master.** Set final loudness and peak levels.
6. **Export and review.** Listen to the full episode once before publishing.

Do not over-edit. Removing every "um" can make speech sound unnatural. Remove filler only when it distracts.

## Loudness Standards

**Loudness** is the perceived volume of audio, measured in LUFS or LKFS (the two units are equivalent). Apple recommends that podcast audio be preconditioned so overall loudness remains around -16 dB LKFS, with a tolerance of plus or minus 1 dB, and that the true-peak value not exceed -1 dB FS, measured according to ITU-R BS.1770-5. Apple notes that this preconditioning should happen before encoding, because compression typically does not change loudness and might clip the signal ([Apple Podcasts for Creators, Audio requirements](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/893-audio-requirements)). Most editing software includes a loudness meter or normalization tool.

## File Formats

| Use | Apple guidance |
|---|---|
| RSS feed delivery | MP3 or AAC |
| Mono MP3, recommended | 44.1 or 48 kHz, 96 to 128 kbps |
| Stereo MP3, recommended | 44.1 or 48 kHz, 128 to 256 kbps |
| Archive master | Keep an uncompressed WAV for future re-exports |

Source: [Apple Podcasts for Creators, Audio requirements](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/893-audio-requirements).

## Episode Titles

Apple's RSS guide advises against putting episode or season numbers in episode titles because dedicated tags exist for that information, and it also advises against repeating the show's name in each episode title ([Apple, A Podcaster's Guide to RSS](https://help.apple.com/itc/podcasts_connect/en.lproj/itcb54353390.html)). Write titles that describe the value: "What a Kitchen Remodel Really Costs" tells a listener more than "Episode 1."

## Show Notes

**Show notes** are the written description of an episode. They help listeners decide whether to listen and help search engines find your content. Include a two- to three-sentence summary, three to five key takeaways, guest name and links, resources mentioned, timestamps for major sections, and a call to action. A template appears in Appendix D. Transcripts improve accessibility and can be repurposed into articles.

## Music and Copyright

The U.S. Copyright Office explains that there are no legal rules permitting the use of a specific number of words or musical notes without permission, that fair use depends on all the circumstances, and that "in cases of doubt, the Copyright Office recommends that permission be obtained" ([U.S. Copyright Office, Fair Use FAQ](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-fairuse.html)). A recorded song can involve two separate copyrights, the musical work and the sound recording, which are often owned and licensed separately ([U.S. Copyright Office, What Musicians Should Know](https://www.copyright.gov/engage/musicians/)). Use music you created, commissioned, or licensed for podcast use, and keep the license terms on file.

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Editing Process
Ken's freelance editor receives separate tracks for Ken and the building inspector. She removes a five-minute tangent about the inspector's vacation, moves the "three most common failures" segment into a 40-second hook, reduces fan noise on the guest's track, and adds a licensed intro theme Ken purchased from a music library with a podcast-use license. She masters to -16 LUFS integrated with a -1 dB true-peak ceiling and exports a 128 kbps stereo MP3 and a WAV archive. Ken listens once at 1.5x speed and approves.
:::

:::checklist Editing and Publishing Quality
- Rough cut, content edit, cleanup, elements, master completed.
- Loudness about -16 LUFS (plus or minus 1); true peak at or below -1 dBFS.
- Exported as MP3 or AAC at recommended settings; WAV archived.
- Title describes value; no episode number in title.
- Show notes with summary, takeaways, guest links, timestamps, call to action.
- All music created, commissioned, or licensed; license on file.
- Full episode reviewed once before publishing.
:::

:::summary
Follow a six-step editing workflow and avoid over-editing. Master to Apple's recommended loudness of about -16 LKFS with true peaks no higher than -1 dB FS, and export MP3 or AAC at recommended settings. Write value-based titles without episode numbers and useful show notes. Use only music you own or have licensed; the Copyright Office recommends obtaining permission when in doubt.
:::

# Chapter 9: Hosting, RSS, and Directories

:::objectives
- Explain how podcast hosting and RSS distribution work.
- Choose a hosting provider.
- Complete the required show metadata.
- Submit your show to major directories.
:::

## How Distribution Works

A **podcast host** is a service that stores your audio files and generates an **RSS feed**, a structured file listing your show's information and episodes. Directories and apps, such as Apple Podcasts and Spotify, read your RSS feed and display your show. When you publish a new episode on your host, the feed updates and subscribers' apps download it. You upload once; the feed distributes everywhere. Apple describes the RSS feed as containing "all the metadata of a podcast," which governs what listeners see, from show art to search results ([Apple, A Podcaster's Guide to RSS](https://help.apple.com/itc/podcasts_connect/en.lproj/itcb54353390.html)).

## Choosing a Host

| Factor | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Reliability | What is the provider's track record for uptime and support? |
| Analytics | Does it provide download data by episode, app, and geography? |
| Ease of use | Can a non-technical team member publish episodes? |
| Distribution | Does it help submit to major directories? |
| Ownership and portability | Can you redirect your feed if you move to another host? |
| Video | Does it support video or integrate with YouTube? |
| Cost | What is included at each plan level? |

Portability is critical. Confirm that the host supports a feed redirect so your subscribers follow you if you change providers.

## Required Show Metadata

Apple lists these as required show-level tags in the RSS feed: title, description, artwork (itunes:image), language, category (itunes:category), and explicit content flag (itunes:explicit). Apple also recommends an author and a website link. Apple recognizes only the first category and subcategory listed, so choose the most accurate one first, and language codes must come from the ISO 639 list or the feed will fail validation ([Apple, A Podcaster's Guide to RSS](https://help.apple.com/itc/podcasts_connect/en.lproj/itcb54353390.html)). Your host's settings page typically fills in these tags for you.

| Metadata | Ken's entry |
|---|---|
| Title | The Remodel Room |
| Description | Opens with the show promise (Chapter 4) |
| Artwork | 3000 by 3000 PNG |
| Language | English (en) |
| Category | Leisure, subcategory Home and Garden (or Business, depending on emphasis) |
| Explicit | false |
| Author | Ken's company name |
| Link | The podcast page on the company website |

## Submitting to Directories

Submit your feed to Apple Podcasts through Apple Podcasts Connect, to Spotify through its creator platform, and to other directories your listeners use. Most hosts provide guided submission. Publish at least one episode (or a trailer) before submitting, because directories need content to review. Allow time for review; plan to submit one to two weeks before your public launch date.

## The Podcast Website Page

Create a page on your business website for the podcast. List every episode with show notes, embed a player, and include subscribe links. This page helps search engines find your content and gives you a single address to share.

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Distribution Setup
Ken chooses a host that offers analytics, feed redirects, and a simple publishing screen his office manager can use. He fills in the required metadata, uploads a two-minute trailer, and submits to Apple Podcasts and Spotify 12 days before launch. He also adds the show to YouTube as a playlist of the video versions. A podcast page on his website lists the trailer, subscribe buttons, and an email signup.
:::

:::checklist Distribution
- Host chosen with analytics, feed redirect, and easy publishing.
- Required tags complete: title, description, artwork, language, category, explicit.
- Recommended tags complete: author, link.
- Trailer or first episode published to the feed.
- Feed submitted to Apple Podcasts, Spotify, and other relevant directories one to two weeks before launch.
- Video version planned or uploaded to YouTube.
- Podcast page live on business website.
:::

:::summary
A podcast host stores your files and publishes an RSS feed that directories read. Choose a host with reliable service, analytics, and feed portability. Complete Apple's required tags, choose the most accurate first category, and submit your feed with a trailer one to two weeks before launch. Create a podcast page on your website.
:::

## How Many Episodes at Launch

Many shows release several episodes on launch day so new listeners can sample more than one episode and judge whether the show fits them. Releasing three is a common approach. Whatever you choose, keep the subsequent schedule consistent.

## Mobilizing Your Network

Your first listeners are your existing relationships. Announce the show to:

- Your email list and past customers, with a personal note.
- Employees, who can share with their networks.
- Guests, who have a natural reason to share their episode.
- Referral partners and suppliers.
- Social media followers, using short audio or video clips.

Ask clearly for one action: subscribe or follow, and if they find it useful, leave a rating or review.

## Realistic Expectations

Most new podcasts begin with small audiences. For a local or specialized business, a small audience of the right listeners can still be valuable; 200 homeowners in your service area who are planning a remodel may be worth more than 20,000 random downloads. Judge the launch by whether the right people are listening and responding, not by comparison to national shows.

:::case Pat Flynn: Listener Response Shapes the Plan
When Pat Flynn launched the Smart Passive Income Podcast in July 2010, it was an extension of a blog that, according to a 2012 New York Times report, then had about 7,500 subscribers ([The New York Times, You're the Boss blog](https://archive.nytimes.com/boss.blogs.nytimes.com/2012/11/30/how-pat-flynn-uses-podcasting-to-build-his-business/)). Flynn recalls publishing every other week at first; after meeting listeners at an event in September 2010 who "could not stop talking about the darn podcast," he decided to double down and moved to a weekly schedule ([Smart Passive Income, Episode 716](https://www.smartpassiveincome.com/podcasts/spi-716-moment-podcasting-was-going-to-change-my-life/)). The New York Times reported in 2012 that the show had been downloaded more than two million times. Two lessons apply: launch to an audience you already have, and let real listener feedback, not assumptions, guide changes in cadence. Flynn's audience and results are his own, not TTI's, and few shows grow at that rate.
:::

## Repurposing

**Repurposing** means turning one episode into several pieces of content for other channels. One 25-minute episode can produce:

| Asset | Channel |
|---|---|
| Full video version | YouTube |
| Three 30- to 60-second clips | Social media |
| Quote graphics | Social media, email |
| Blog article from the transcript | Website |
| Email summary | Newsletter |
| Frequently asked question answer | Website FAQ, sales follow-up |

Given that YouTube was the service weekly podcast listeners used most often in Edison's 2025 data ([Edison Research](https://www.edisonresearch.com/the-infinite-dial-2025/)), the video version deserves particular attention.

## Guesting and Collaborations

Appearing as a guest on other shows introduces you to established audiences. Look for shows that serve listeners similar to yours but are not direct competitors. A remodeling company owner might appear on real estate, interior design, or local business shows. Episode swaps, where two hosts each feature the other, are another option.

## Search and Discoverability

Clear show and episode titles, accurate categories, and detailed show notes help both directory and web search. Write titles in the words listeners use: "How Much Does a Bathroom Remodel Cost" is more searchable than "Dollars and Sense." Remember Apple's caution against keyword stuffing in the show title.

## The Listener Feedback Loop

Invite questions in every episode, respond personally to messages, and feature listener questions in Q&A episodes. Feedback reveals which topics matter most and builds loyalty. Track which episodes generate the most responses and plan more like them.

## Paid Promotion

Some directories and apps offer paid promotion for podcasts. Treat paid promotion as an experiment with a defined budget and a measurable goal, and only after your show has enough episodes to retain new listeners.

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Growth Routine
For every episode, Ken's editor produces a YouTube video, three vertical clips, and a transcript. His office manager turns the transcript into a blog post and adds a summary to the monthly newsletter. Ken appears as a guest on a local real estate podcast and a regional home show's podcast. He reads one listener question in every episode, which increases replies to his newsletter.
:::

- Transcript turned into a blog article.
- Email summary sent to list.
- Listener question invited and featured.
- One guest appearance or collaboration pursued per month.
:::

:::summary
Repurpose each episode into video, clips, articles, and emails, with special attention to YouTube. Appear on related shows, write searchable titles, and build a feedback loop by inviting and featuring listener questions. Treat paid promotion as a measured experiment.

# Chapter 12: Measuring Results and Monetizing Responsibly

:::objectives
- Choose metrics that reflect business goals.
- Interpret download and consumption data cautiously.
- Connect the podcast to business outcomes.
- Handle sponsorships and endorsements in line with FTC guidance.
:::

## Choosing Metrics

Podcast metrics fall into three levels:

| Level | Examples | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | Downloads, unique listeners, followers | How many people the show reaches |
| Engagement | Consumption or completion rates, reviews, replies, questions submitted | How much listeners value the show |
| Business outcome | Consultations booked, referrals, inquiries mentioning the podcast, recruiting applicants | Whether the show advances business goals |

Reach metrics are easiest to collect but least connected to business value. Prioritize outcomes and engagement.

## Interpreting Data Cautiously

Download counts vary by host and app, and different platforms count differently. Compare your show against its own history rather than against others. Look at trends over months, not days. Note which episodes perform best and why.

## Connecting to Business Outcomes

- Add "How did you hear about us?" to intake forms, with the podcast as an option.
- Mention a dedicated web page or offer in episodes.
- Ask new customers directly whether they listened.
- Track how often sales staff send episodes to prospects and whether those prospects convert.

## Monetization Options

| Option | Description | Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect (your business) | The podcast builds trust that leads to customers | The most common model for business shows |
| Sponsorship | Companies pay to be mentioned | Requires audience size; disclosure obligations |
| Affiliate links | Commission on referred sales | Disclosure obligations |
| Premium content | Paid subscriber episodes | Requires a loyal audience |

## Endorsements and Disclosure

The FTC's Endorsement Guides apply to podcasts. Endorsements must reflect the endorser's honest opinions, and a material connection between an endorser and a seller that the audience would not reasonably expect must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously ([16 CFR Part 255](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255)). The Guides include a podcast example: when a host reads what is obviously a commercial near the start of an episode, a payment disclosure is not needed because listeners would expect compensation, but the host must hold any views expressed as their own; and a separate social media mention of the product may still require a disclosure ([16 CFR 255.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255)). FTC staff suggest simple disclosures such as "This is an ad for BRAND" or "BRAND paid me to tell you about it" ([FTC, Endorsement Guides: What People Are Asking](https://consumer.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking)). For audio content, the disclosure should be made in the audio itself.

Practical rules:

- Disclose paid, gifted, affiliate, family, and employment relationships in the episode, near the mention.
- Only endorse products you have used and believe in.
- Do not make claims you cannot support.
- Disclose separately on social media posts about sponsored products.

:::example Illustrative: Ken's Season One Review
After nine months, Ken's show averages a modest number of downloads per episode, which he compares only with his own early episodes. More important to him: his intake form shows that a growing share of new consultation requests mention the podcast, and his project managers report that prospects who listened arrive with better questions. A local cabinet supplier offers to sponsor season two. Ken agrees on the condition that the supplier's segment is clearly identified as a paid message and that he discloses the relationship whenever he mentions the supplier elsewhere. These figures and outcomes are hypothetical.
:::

How I will capture it (intake question, page, offer): ___
Engagement metrics I will watch: ___
Reach metrics I will watch: ___
Review date: ___

:::

:::summary
Measure reach, engagement, and especially business outcomes, comparing the show with its own history. Connect listening to results with intake questions, dedicated pages, and direct conversations. If you accept sponsors or affiliate income, follow the FTC Endorsement Guides: hold the views you express, disclose material connections clearly in the audio, and disclose separately on social media.

# Glossary

**AAC.** An audio compression format accepted by Apple Podcasts for RSS delivery.

**Cadence.** The publishing schedule of a podcast, such as weekly or every other week.

**Dynamic microphone.** A microphone type that tends to reject room noise, suited to untreated rooms.

**Endorsement.** An advertising message that consumers are likely to believe reflects the opinions or experiences of someone other than the advertiser.

**Feed redirect.** A setting that points directories to a new RSS feed address when changing hosts.

**Guest release.** A written agreement in which a guest permits recording, editing, and publication.

**Loudness (LUFS or LKFS).** A measure of perceived audio volume; Apple recommends about -16 for podcasts.

**Material connection.** A relationship between an endorser and a seller, such as payment or free products, that could affect how an audience weighs an endorsement.

**Podcast host.** A service that stores audio files and generates the RSS feed.

**Repurposing.** Turning one episode into multiple pieces of content for other channels.

**RSS feed.** A structured file containing a podcast's metadata and episodes, read by directories and apps.

**Season.** A defined set of episodes released over a period, followed by a planned break.

**Show notes.** The written description accompanying an episode.

**Show promise.** A one-sentence statement of who the show is for and what it offers.

**Target listener.** A specific description of the person the show most wants to serve.

**True peak.** The maximum level of an audio signal, accounting for peaks between samples.

**USB microphone.** A microphone that connects directly to a computer.

**XLR microphone.** A professional microphone that connects through an audio interface or mixer.
