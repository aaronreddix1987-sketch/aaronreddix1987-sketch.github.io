# How to Use This Book

Digital products such as templates, checklists, guides, workbooks, spreadsheets, short courses, and toolkits are among the most accessible products a small business or independent professional can create. They require no inventory, can be delivered instantly, and can be sold repeatedly without additional production cost. These characteristics have produced a large market and an equally large volume of exaggerated promises about "passive income."

This workbook takes a disciplined view. A digital product is a business offering like any other. It succeeds when it solves a specific problem for a specific buyer better than the alternatives, when it is priced so that each sale is profitable after fees and taxes, when it is delivered reliably, and when its marketing is honest. It fails, often quietly, when any of those elements is missing. Most first digital products sell modestly. Some sell nothing. That outcome is common and should be planned for.

The book is written for consultants, coaches, tradespeople, educators, freelancers, and small-business owners who want to package what they know into a product that others can buy and use. It assumes no technical background beyond comfort with ordinary office software.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Explain the economics of digital products, including platform fees, payment costs, refunds, and taxes.
- Identify a problem worth solving through structured conversations with real prospective buyers.
- Write a one-sentence offer and choose an appropriate product format.
- Plan, build, and quality-check a product, including accessibility basics.
- Confirm that you have the rights to everything in the product and that your claims are honest.
- Price the product and calculate per-sale margin.
- Set up checkout, delivery, and tax handling, and test them end to end.
- Build a small audience before launch and comply with email marketing law.
- Run a seven-day small-audience launch test and measure results.
- Maintain, update, and extend the product over time.

## How This Workbook Is Built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, explains its concepts in plain language, and provides a step-by-step method. Each includes at least one worked example with numbers, using invented creators labelled "illustrative," and at least one worksheet or checklist. Real, publicly documented cases appear in case-study boxes with numbered citations to the References, including Pat Flynn's first study-guide ebook, Nathan Barry's self-published design books, and Justin Welsh's solo digital-product business. Every chapter ends with a table, a summary, and review questions.

The workbook is meant to be written in. Complete each worksheet before moving to the next chapter; later chapters depend on earlier decisions. The appendices collect blank versions of the key worksheets.

# Chapter 1: The Economics of Digital Products

:::objectives
- Define a digital product and distinguish common formats.
- Explain why digital products have high gross margins and what costs remain.
- Calculate net revenue per sale after platform, payment, and refund costs.
- Set realistic expectations about sales volume and time.
:::

A digital product is a good delivered electronically that the buyer can use without ongoing personal service from the seller. Common formats include PDF guides and workbooks, spreadsheet tools and calculators, document and design templates, checklists and standard operating procedure packs, recorded courses, and bundles that combine several of these.

Digital products differ from services in one fundamental way: the cost of delivering one more copy is close to zero. Once the product exists, the hundredth sale costs almost nothing more to fulfill than the first. This is why digital products can have very high gross margins. It is also why they attract unrealistic expectations. Low marginal cost does not mean low total cost, and it does not mean demand will appear.

## The Costs That Remain

Even with near-zero delivery cost, several costs apply to every sale or to the product as a whole:

- **Platform fees.** Hosted storefronts charge a percentage and sometimes a fixed fee per sale. For example, Gumroad lists a fee of 10 percent plus $0.50 per transaction for sales through a creator's profile or direct links, and 30 percent for sales through its Discover marketplace [1].
- **Payment processing.** Card processors charge per transaction; Stripe's standard U.S. pricing is 2.9 percent plus 30 cents per successful domestic card transaction [2]. Some platforms include processing in their fee; others charge it separately. Gumroad's help center notes that its direct-sale fee does not include card processing [3].
- **Refunds and chargebacks.** Some buyers will request refunds; a few will dispute charges.
- **Taxes.** Sales taxes and value-added taxes may apply depending on where buyers live (Chapter 7), and income tax applies to profit.
- **Creation time.** The hours spent researching, writing, designing, and testing are a real investment, even if not paid in cash.
- **Marketing.** Time or money spent reaching buyers.
- **Maintenance.** Updates as information changes.

:::example Net Revenue per Sale (illustrative)
Luis plans to sell his estimating toolkit for $49 through a hosted platform that charges 10 percent plus $0.50 per sale, with card processing of 2.9 percent plus $0.30 charged separately. Platform fee: $4.90 + $0.50 = $5.40. Processing: $1.42 + $0.30 = $1.72. Net before refunds: $49.00 - $5.40 - $1.72 = $41.88. If he expects 4 percent of buyers to request refunds, the expected refund cost per sale is about $49 x 0.04 = $1.96, leaving about $39.92 per sale, or 81 percent of the price. Income tax on profit is additional. These rates are examples; confirm current fees with each provider.
:::

## The Volume Question

The central uncertainty is how many people will buy. That depends on the size and engagement of the audience you can reach, the urgency of the problem, the quality of the product, and price. A useful way to frame expectations is to work backward from a goal. If Luis wants $10,000 in net revenue over a year and nets about $40 per sale, he needs about 250 sales. If roughly 2 percent of people who visit his sales page buy, which is a planning assumption rather than a benchmark, he needs about 12,500 sales-page visits. That arithmetic immediately shows whether the plan is realistic given his current reach.

:::case Pat Flynn's First Study Guide
Pat Flynn, an architect laid off in 2008, built a website to help himself prepare for the LEED green-building exam and posted his notes and study aids there. In an interview with Forbes, he explained that the site began attracting thousands of visitors a day, that readers told him they would pay for his content, and that in October 2008 he released an ebook study guide for $19.99, delivered as a PDF by email immediately after purchase [4]. He reported earning $7,008.55 in the first month [4]. He went on to found Smart Passive Income, where he documented his earnings and lessons. The case illustrates a sequence that recurs in successful digital products: an audience formed around useful free content, buyers asked for more, and the product answered a specific, urgent need (passing a professional exam). Flynn's results are his own, occurred under particular market conditions, and are not typical.
:::

Common failure patterns include building a product before confirming that anyone wants it; addressing a vague problem ("productivity") rather than a specific one ("estimating residential panel upgrades"); pricing without accounting for fees and refunds; launching to no audience; broken checkout or delivery links; and making claims the product cannot support. Each is addressed in later chapters.

# Chapter 2: Finding a Problem Worth Solving

:::objectives
- Distinguish problems people will pay to solve from problems they merely acknowledge.
- Identify a specific buyer and the situation that creates urgency.
- Conduct five structured buyer conversations.
- Evaluate candidate problems using consistent criteria.
:::

The most common mistake in digital product creation is building first and looking for buyers later. The remedy is research conducted before any product work begins. Research does not need to be elaborate. Five careful conversations with people who fit your intended buyer will often reveal more than months of solitary planning.

## Paid Problems and Acknowledged Problems

People acknowledge many problems they will never pay to solve. A paid problem has several characteristics: it is frequent or costly, the buyer is aware of it, the buyer has already tried to solve it (with time, money, or workarounds), and the buyer can access the money to pay. "I wish I were more organized" is an acknowledged problem. "I lost two jobs last quarter because my estimates were wrong and I don't have a reliable method" is a paid problem.

## Step 1: Name the Buyer

Describe the buyer specifically enough that you could find and talk to five of them this week. Include role, situation, experience level, and the trigger that makes the problem urgent. Luis's buyer: "Owners of one- to five-person residential electrical contracting businesses in their first three years who are losing money or jobs because their estimates are inconsistent."

## Step 2: Gather Evidence of the Problem

Before conversations, collect public evidence:

- Questions asked repeatedly in forums, groups, and comment sections in the buyer's industry.
- Complaints in reviews of existing products or books on the topic.
- Search suggestions and "people also ask" questions on search engines.
- Existing paid products, which indicate a market exists.
- Your own records: questions clients and colleagues ask you repeatedly.

## Step 3: Conduct Five Conversations

Ask open questions about past behavior rather than hypothetical intentions. People are poor predictors of what they will buy, but reliable reporters of what they have done.

1. "Tell me about the last time you dealt with [problem]."
2. "What did you do about it?"
3. "What did that cost you, in time or money?"
4. "What have you tried that did not work?"
5. "If you could change one thing about how you handle this, what would it be?"
6. "Have you ever paid for something to help with this? What was it?"

Avoid pitching during these conversations. Record the exact words people use; they will become your sales language.

:::example Five Conversations, Summarized (illustrative)
Luis speaks with five contractor owners. Four describe losing money on at least one job in the past six months because of underestimated labor. Three use a spreadsheet they built themselves and describe it as "a mess." Two have bought a general construction-estimating software subscription at $79 to $150 per month and stopped using it because it was "built for bigger outfits." All five say the hardest part is estimating labor hours for older homes. Luis records phrases such as "I just guess the hours" and "old houses kill my margins." He concludes that a focused toolkit for labor estimating on residential jobs, especially older homes, addresses a paid problem.
:::

## Step 4: Score Candidate Problems

If you have several candidate problems, score each from 1 to 5 on urgency, frequency, willingness to pay, your expertise, and your ability to reach buyers. Choose the highest total, and break ties using your expertise, which most affects quality.

| Criterion | Question | Evidence source |
|---|---|---|
| Urgency | Does the problem cause immediate pain or loss? | Conversations; complaints |
| Frequency | How often does it occur? | Conversations; forums |
| Willingness to pay | Have buyers paid for solutions before? | Existing products; conversations |
| Expertise | Can you solve it better than most? | Your experience and results |
| Reach | Can you reach buyers affordably? | Your network, list, platforms |

# Chapter 3: Defining the Offer and Format

:::objectives
- Write a one-sentence offer that names the buyer, problem, and result.
- Choose a format that fits the problem and the buyer's context.
- Strengthen the offer using a value framework.
- Test the offer's clarity with strangers.
:::

An offer is the promise a product makes, expressed in the buyer's terms. It is distinct from the product itself. A clear offer lets the right buyer recognize immediately that the product is for them, and lets the wrong buyer move on without confusion.

## The One-Sentence Offer

Use this pattern: "A [format] that helps [buyer] [achieve result] without [common obstacle]." Luis's offer: "A spreadsheet toolkit that helps small residential electrical contractors estimate labor hours accurately, including in older homes, without expensive software built for large firms."

The sentence should contain no jargon the buyer would not use, no superlatives, and no promises of financial results. "Estimate labor hours accurately" describes what the product helps the buyer do; "double your profits" would be an unsupported claim.

## Choosing the Format

Choose the format that best fits how the buyer will use the solution:

- If the buyer needs to repeat a calculation, a spreadsheet tool fits better than a PDF.
- If the buyer needs to follow a process, a checklist or workbook fits.
- If the buyer needs to see a skill performed, short videos may be necessary.
- If the buyer needs ready-made documents, templates fit.

The simplest format that fully solves the problem is usually best. It is faster to build, easier to update, and less intimidating to use.

## Strengthening the Offer

Alex Hormozi describes four drivers of value: the dream outcome, the perceived likelihood of achieving it, the time delay before results, and the effort and sacrifice required [5]. Apply them to your offer:

| Value driver | Question | Luis's answer |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | What does the buyer ultimately want? | Bid jobs confidently and stop losing money on labor |
| Perceived likelihood | Why should they believe it will work? | Built by a licensed electrician from 15 years of job records; includes worked examples |
| Time delay | How soon will they benefit? | First estimate completed within an hour of download |
| Effort and sacrifice | How much work is required? | Enter job details into labeled fields; no software to learn |

## The Stranger Test

Show the one-sentence offer to someone who fits the buyer description but does not know you. Give them 15 seconds, then ask: "Who is this for, and what does it do?" If they cannot answer accurately, revise. Repeat with three people.

:::example Revising an Offer (illustrative)
Luis's first draft read: "The Ultimate Electrical Business Toolkit for success." In the stranger test, two of three contractors could not say what it did; one guessed it was a marketing course. His revised offer specified the buyer, the task (labor estimating), the special case (older homes), and the obstacle (expensive software). On retesting, all three described it accurately, and two asked about the price. Clarity improved from one in three to three in three.
:::

An offer is the product's promise expressed in the buyer's terms. Write it as one sentence naming the format, buyer, result, and obstacle, without jargon, superlatives, or financial promises. # Chapter 4: Building the Product

:::objectives
- Outline a product around the buyer's sequence of tasks.
- Produce a minimum complete version efficiently.
- Apply quality and accessibility checks before release.
- Use AI tools responsibly in product creation.
:::

A product should be built around what the buyer does, in the order they do it. Organizing content around the creator's knowledge ("everything I know about estimating") produces long, unfocused products. Organizing around the buyer's tasks ("estimate this job, step by step") produces products that are shorter and more useful.

## Step 1: Map the Buyer's Task Sequence

List the steps a buyer takes to go from the problem to the result. For Luis: gather job information; classify the job type; estimate labor hours by task; adjust for home age and conditions; add materials; calculate overhead and margin; produce a client-ready estimate; record actual hours afterward to improve future estimates.

## Step 2: Define the Minimum Complete Version

A minimum complete version solves the whole problem simply. It is not a partial product; it is a focused one. Every element should map to a step in the task sequence. Anything that does not can wait for a later version. Luis's minimum complete version: a spreadsheet with job-information inputs, a labor table for 40 common residential tasks, home-age adjustment factors, an overhead and margin calculator, an estimate output sheet, a job-actuals tracker, and a 12-page PDF guide with three worked examples.

## Step 3: Produce in Focused Sessions

Schedule production in blocks. Draft the core tool or content first, then the examples, then the instructions. Resist polishing early; content that is clear and correct matters more than design.

## Step 4: Quality Check

Before release, check:

- **Accuracy.** Every figure, formula, and statement is correct. Spreadsheet formulas are tested with known inputs.
- **Completeness.** A buyer can go from start to finish without needing to contact you.
- **Clarity.** Instructions use the buyer's vocabulary. A test user can follow them unaided.
- **Consistency.** Terminology, formatting, and numbering are uniform.
- **File hygiene.** Files are named clearly, open on common software, and contain no hidden personal data or leftover drafts.

## Step 5: Accessibility

Accessible products serve more buyers and reflect professional quality. The World Wide Web Consortium's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the internationally recognized standard for digital accessibility [6]. For documents and templates, practical steps include using real headings rather than enlarged text, sufficient color contrast, descriptive link text, alternative text for meaningful images, readable font sizes, and not relying on color alone to convey information.

## Using AI Tools Responsibly

AI writing and design tools can speed drafting, editing, and formatting. Three cautions apply. First, AI output can contain errors; every factual statement must be verified. Second, the U.S. Copyright Office concluded in January 2025 that outputs of generative AI can be protected by copyright only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements, that prompts alone are not enough, and that using AI to assist creation or including AI-generated material in a larger human-authored work does not bar copyright protection [7]. A product that is mostly unedited AI output may therefore have weak copyright protection. Third, a product built from generic AI text offers little that buyers cannot get for free. Your experience, examples, and judgment are what make the product worth buying.

:::example Test-User Session (illustrative)
Luis asks two contractors to use the toolkit on a real job while he watches over a video call without helping. The first completes an estimate in 52 minutes but cannot find where to enter permit fees. The second completes it in 38 minutes but misreads the home-age adjustment as a percentage rather than a multiplier. Luis adds a labeled permit-fee field, changes the adjustment column heading to "Multiplier (e.g., 1.15)," and adds a one-line example. Two small fixes prevent errors that might otherwise have generated refund requests.
:::

| Quality dimension | Check | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Formulas and facts are correct | Test with known inputs; verify sources |
| Completeness | Buyer can finish unaided | Test-user session |
| Clarity | Instructions are understood | Observe a test user without helping |
| Accessibility | Usable by people with disabilities | WCAG-informed checklist |
| File hygiene | Files open and are clean | Open on a second device; inspect metadata |

:::checklist Pre-Release Product Check
- Every element maps to a step in the buyer's task sequence.
- All formulas tested with at least three known inputs.
- Every factual statement verified against a reliable source.
- At least one test user completed the product without help.
- Headings, contrast, alt text, and font sizes reviewed for accessibility.
- AI-assisted content reviewed, edited, and verified by a human.
- Files open correctly on at least two devices or programs.
- Personal data and draft comments removed from files.
- A short "Start Here" page explains what is included and how to begin.
:::

:::summary
Organize the product around the buyer's task sequence and build a minimum complete version that solves the whole problem simply. Produce in focused sessions, prioritizing correctness over polish. Check accuracy, completeness, clarity, consistency, file hygiene, and accessibility, using WCAG as a reference. Use AI tools as assistants, verify their output, and remember that copyright protection depends on human authorship.
:::

# Chapter 5: Rights, Sources, and Honest Claims

:::objectives
- Confirm ownership of or permission for every element in a product.
- Understand basic copyright principles relevant to digital products.
- Maintain a rights and sources register.
- Write marketing claims that are truthful and substantiated.
:::

Selling a product means representing that you have the right to sell everything in it. Images, fonts, quotations, data, templates, and code all carry rights. A product that infringes someone else's rights can be removed from platforms, generate legal claims, and damage your reputation. Similarly, marketing claims must be truthful. This chapter covers both.

## Copyright Basics

The U.S. Copyright Office explains that copyright protects original works of authorship fixed in a tangible form, and that an original work is independently created by a human author with at least a minimal degree of creativity [8]. Copyright protects expression, not ideas, procedures, or facts [8]. In practice:

- You own the copyright in original text, designs, and spreadsheets you create, unless you created them as an employee within the scope of employment or assigned the rights by contract.
- Facts and ideas are not protected, but someone else's specific expression of them is.
- Copying another creator's template, text, or design without permission is likely infringement, even if you change some details.
- Fair use is a limited, fact-specific defense, not a general permission; the Copyright Office describes it as depending on factors such as the purpose of the use, the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on the market [9]. Do not rely on fair use for commercial products without legal advice.

## Third-Party Assets

Images, icons, fonts, and templates from other sources come with licenses. Read them. Many "free" assets permit personal use only, prohibit resale, or prohibit use in products that are themselves templates. Keep copies of licenses and download receipts.

## Employer and Client Materials

If you developed materials while employed or for a client, those materials may belong to the employer or client. Your general knowledge and skill are yours; specific documents, data, and proprietary methods may not be. Review employment agreements and client contracts before including any material derived from past work. Luis, for example, uses his own job records to build labor tables, but he removes customer names and addresses and does not use his former employer's estimating templates.

## The Rights and Sources Register

Keep a simple register listing every element of the product that you did not create from scratch, its source, the license or permission, and where the evidence is stored. This takes minutes and can save hours in a dispute.

## Honest Claims

The Federal Trade Commission requires that advertising be truthful and that objective claims be substantiated. It has given specific warnings to sellers of money-making opportunities: in 2021 it sent notices to more than 1,100 businesses warning that unsubstantiated earnings claims could result in civil penalties [10]. Its 2024 rule on consumer reviews and testimonials prohibits fake or false reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated reviews, and prohibits conditioning incentives on positive reviews [11].

For a digital product, honest marketing means:

- Describe what the product contains and what it helps the buyer do.
- Do not promise income, savings, or results you cannot substantiate.
- Use only genuine testimonials, with permission, and do not present exceptional results as typical.
- Disclose any incentive given for a review or testimonial.
- State refund terms clearly.

:::example Rewriting Claims (illustrative)
Luis's draft sales page included three claims. Claim 1: "Never lose money on a job again." He cannot substantiate this; he revises it to "Estimate labor hours more consistently using tables built from 15 years of residential jobs." Claim 2: "Contractors using this toolkit earn 30 percent more." He has no data; he deletes it. Claim 3: "Includes 40 common residential tasks with labor ranges and home-age multipliers." This is a factual description of the contents; he keeps it. The page becomes more specific and more credible.
:::

| Element | Ownership or permission question | Evidence to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Your original text and spreadsheets | Created by you, not as work for hire? | Draft files with dates |
| Stock images and icons | License permits commercial use in a product for sale? | License text and receipt |
| Fonts | License permits embedding in distributed files? | License text |
| Data and statistics | Facts are free, but the source should be cited | Source URLs |
| Quotations | Short, attributed, and appropriate? | Source citation |
| Former employer or client materials | Do you have the right to use them? | Agreements; written permission |
| Testimonials | Genuine and permitted? | Written permission |

# Chapter 6: Pricing and Per-Sale Math

:::objectives
- Identify reference points buyers use to judge price.
- Apply value-based reasoning to set an initial price.
- Design tiered packages where appropriate.
- Calculate margin and break-even for each tier.
:::

Price communicates value and determines how many sales are needed to reach a goal. New creators often underprice out of uncertainty. A low price does not necessarily produce more sales; it can signal low quality and leave too little margin to fund marketing and updates.

## Reference Points

Buyers judge price against alternatives: doing it themselves (time cost), hiring someone (service cost), buying software (subscription cost), or buying a competing product. Identify these reference points from your research. For Luis, the alternatives are building a spreadsheet themselves (many hours), paying for estimating software ($79 to $150 per month, according to his conversations), or continuing to guess and occasionally losing money on jobs.

## Value-Based Reasoning

Estimate the value of the result to the buyer. If a contractor loses $600 on one underestimated job each quarter, and the toolkit helps them reduce that loss, the value over a year is substantial relative to a price of $49 or even $99. Value-based reasoning justifies a price; it does not justify promising the value will be achieved.

## Tiered Packages

Offering more than one package lets buyers choose the level of help they need. Nathan Barry, who later founded the email marketing company ConvertKit (now Kit), discussed on the Smart Passive Income podcast his three-tier pricing structure for self-published ebooks and how multiple packages can substantially increase revenue compared with a single option [12].

:::case Nathan Barry's Self-Published Books
Nathan Barry launched his first ebook, The App Design Handbook, with an email list of under 1,000 people and reported making over $12,000 on the first day [12]. He sold it from his own website rather than through Amazon, using three packages at different prices [12]. When the book later became outdated, he wrote that sales had declined to about $1,000 per month; he partnered with designer Jeremy Olson on a 50/50 revenue split to update it for iOS 7, and the updated edition sold 548 copies for $36,297 in gross revenue in its first 24 hours [13]. The case illustrates three principles relevant to this workbook: a small, engaged audience can support a launch; tiered packages let different buyers choose different levels of value; and products decay without maintenance. Barry's results are his own and were achieved with an audience built through extensive prior writing and teaching; they are not typical.
:::

A common three-tier structure:

1. **Core.** The essential product.
2. **Complete.** The core plus additional tools, templates, or examples.
3. **Premium.** The complete package plus higher-touch elements such as video walkthroughs, a live session, or a license for a team.

Each tier should offer clearly more value, not just more files.

:::example Tier Math (illustrative)
Luis designs three tiers. Core at $49: the spreadsheet and guide. Complete at $99: adds a commercial-job labor table, 15 estimate templates, and a client-proposal template. Team at $199: adds a five-user license and a recorded 60-minute walkthrough. Using a 10 percent plus $0.50 platform fee and 2.9 percent plus $0.30 processing:

Core: fees $5.40 + $1.72 = $7.12; net $41.88.
Complete: fees $10.40 + $3.17 = $13.57; net $85.43.
Team: fees $20.40 + $6.07 = $26.47; net $172.53.

If 100 buyers split 60 / 30 / 10 across the tiers, net revenue is (60 x $41.88) + (30 x $85.43) + (10 x $172.53) = $2,512.80 + $2,562.90 + $1,725.30 = $6,801. With a single $49 product, 100 buyers would net $4,188. The split is hypothetical; actual tier choices vary.
:::

## Launch Pricing and Discounts

A modest, time-limited launch price for early buyers can reward an existing audience and generate initial feedback. Be truthful: if you say a price will rise on a certain date, it must rise. Avoid permanent "sales" that misrepresent a regular price as a discount.

| Pricing approach | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Single low price | Simple; low barrier | Thin margin; may signal low value |
| Single value-based price | Strong margin | Fewer buyers at higher price |
| Tiered packages | Buyers self-select; higher average order | More to build and explain |
| Launch discount | Rewards early buyers; creates a real deadline | Must be honest about the regular price |
| Pay-what-you-want | Low barrier; learns willingness to pay | Unpredictable revenue |

# Chapter 7: Checkout, Delivery, Tax, Refunds

:::objectives
- Choose a selling platform with an understanding of the merchant-of-record model.
- Recognize sales tax and VAT obligations for digital goods.
- Design a reliable delivery process.
- Write a clear, fair refund policy.
:::

The buyer's experience between clicking "buy" and opening the product shapes their first impression and determines many refund requests. Checkout and delivery also carry legal and tax obligations. This chapter covers the practical decisions.

## Merchant of Record

A merchant of record is the party legally responsible for the sale, including collecting and remitting sales tax or VAT. Some platforms act as merchant of record; others leave that responsibility with you. Gumroad states that it became the merchant of record for all sales on its platform from January 1, 2025, handling sales tax and VAT on sellers' behalf [1]. Paddle explains that as a merchant of record it calculates, collects, and remits VAT and sales tax on transactions it processes [14]. If you sell through a payment processor on your own website without a merchant of record, the tax obligations generally remain yours.

## Sales Tax on Digital Goods

Digital products are taxable in some U.S. states and not in others. The Washington State Department of Revenue, for example, explains that digital products, including digital goods such as downloaded books, music, and software, are generally subject to retail sales tax in Washington [15]. Many countries also apply VAT to digital sales to consumers. Rules change and depend on where buyers are located, so a merchant-of-record platform or professional tax advice is prudent for sellers without tax expertise. Income tax on profits is a separate obligation; the IRS Self-Employed Individuals Tax Center outlines obligations for sole proprietors [16].

## Delivery

Reliable delivery means the buyer receives the product immediately, can find it again later, and can open it. Practical steps:

- Deliver through the platform's built-in download or library system, not by manual email.
- Include a "Start Here" file that lists contents and first steps.
- Use common formats (PDF, XLSX, or Google Sheets copies) and test them.
- Send a confirmation email with access instructions and a support contact.
- Purchase the product yourself (or use a test mode) to see exactly what the buyer sees.

## Refund Policy

A refund policy should be clear, visible before purchase, and consistently applied. Many creators offer a refund window, such as 14 or 30 days. A generous policy can increase buyer confidence; a policy with conditions (for example, asking the buyer to describe what did not work) can produce useful feedback. Whatever the policy, state it on the sales page and honor it. Platform policies may also apply.

:::example Seven-Point Checkout Proof (illustrative)
Before launch, Luis completes a test purchase and records: (1) the sales page loads on a phone in under three seconds; (2) the price and tier names match the page; (3) tax is calculated at checkout for a Washington address; (4) the receipt email arrives within one minute; (5) the download link works and the files open on a laptop and a phone; (6) the Start Here file names the support email; (7) the refund policy appears on the sales page and receipt. He finds that point 5 fails on his phone because the spreadsheet uses a macro; he removes the macro and replaces it with a formula. A single test purchase prevents a defect that would have affected every mobile buyer.
:::

| Setup option | Who handles sales tax/VAT | Typical tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| Merchant-of-record platform | Platform, generally | Higher fees; less tax work |
| Own site with a payment processor | Seller | Lower fees; seller handles tax compliance |
| Marketplace with discovery | Platform, generally | Higher fees; access to marketplace buyers |

:::checklist Checkout and Delivery Check
- Merchant-of-record status of the platform confirmed.
- Sales tax and VAT responsibility understood and documented.
- Test purchase completed on desktop and phone.
- Receipt and access email reviewed.
- Files open on at least two devices.
- Start Here file included with support contact.
- Refund policy displayed before purchase and in the receipt.
- Records kept of sales, fees, and refunds for income tax purposes.
:::

:::summary
A merchant of record is legally responsible for sales and tax; platforms such as Gumroad and Paddle take on that role, while selling directly through a processor generally leaves tax obligations with the seller. Digital goods are taxable in some jurisdictions, including Washington State. Deliver through reliable systems, test the full purchase, and publish a clear refund policy that you apply consistently.
:::

# Chapter 8: Building an Audience First

:::objectives
- Explain why an audience matters more than a product launch platform.
- Build a small, permission-based email list.
- Apply CAN-SPAM requirements to commercial email.
- Use helpful content to earn attention in a niche.
:::

Most digital products fail not because they are poor but because no one sees them. Marketplaces bury new products among thousands of others, and paid advertising is expensive for a $49 item. The most reliable launch asset is a group of people who already know, trust, and want to hear from you. This chapter explains how to build one, even a small one.

## Why Small Audiences Work

A product needs relatively few buyers to be worthwhile. At a net of about $40 per sale, 100 buyers produce roughly $4,000. If 3 percent of an engaged email list buys, 100 sales require a list of about 3,300. If the list is highly targeted and the product is closely matched to their problem, conversion can be higher. Nathan Barry launched with a list of under 1,000 people [12]. These are illustrations, not predictions; conversion rates vary widely.

## Owned and Rented Channels

Social media followers are rented: the platform controls who sees your posts. An email list is owned: you can reach subscribers directly, and you can take the list with you if a platform changes. Use social platforms to meet people and email to keep in touch.

## Building the List

1. **Choose one channel where your buyers spend time.** For Luis, that is a regional electricians' Facebook group, an online trade forum, and LinkedIn.
2. **Publish helpful content consistently.** Answer real questions. Share worked examples. Explain what you have learned.
3. **Offer a useful free resource.** A one-page checklist or a small calculator that solves part of the problem, in exchange for an email address.
4. **Send a regular, useful email.** Weekly or every two weeks, with one practical idea per issue.
5. **Ask questions.** Replies tell you what subscribers need and build relationships.

## Email Law

The FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide explains that commercial email must not use false or misleading header information or deceptive subject lines, must identify the message as an advertisement where required, must include a valid physical postal address, must tell recipients how to opt out, and must honor opt-out requests within 10 business days [17]. Using a reputable email service and sending only to people who opted in makes compliance straightforward.

:::case Justin Welsh: Audience Before Products
Justin Welsh, a former sales executive, reported that his solo business passed $10 million in total revenue about five years and nine months after he started, reaching his first $1 million in 29 months [18]. He wrote that the revenue was 100 percent organic with no paid advertising, with margins of about 89 percent [18]. He began with consulting and services before shifting to digital products, and he grew his LinkedIn following from about 2,000 to more than 750,000 through consistent posting [18]. The relevant lesson for this chapter is sequence: he built an audience and learned what it needed before scaling products. His results are his own, reflect years of daily writing and prior professional experience, and are not typical.
:::

:::example Ninety Days of Audience Building (illustrative)
Luis commits to three posts a week in two trade groups and on LinkedIn, each answering a specific estimating question with a worked example. He offers a free "Service Upgrade Labor Checklist" in exchange for an email address and sends a weekly email. After 90 days, he has 410 subscribers, 38 of whom have replied to at least one email. Fourteen describe their estimating problems in detail, and seven ask when the full toolkit will be available. The list is small, but it is specific and engaged, which is what matters for a first launch.
:::

| Channel | Ownership | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| Social media posts | Rented | Meeting new people; showing expertise |
| Online communities and forums | Rented | Answering questions; learning problems |
| Email list | Owned | Ongoing relationship; launches |
| Personal website or blog | Owned | Reference content; search traffic |
| Podcasts or guest articles | Borrowed | Reaching established audiences |

:::checklist Audience-Building Plan
- One primary channel chosen where buyers already gather.
- Posting schedule set and realistic.
- Free resource that solves a small part of the problem.
- Reputable email service set up with opt-in forms.
- Postal address and unsubscribe link included in emails.

:::

:::summary
An engaged audience is the most reliable launch asset, and a small, targeted list can support a first product. Social followers are rented; an email list is owned. Build the list with consistent helpful content, a free resource, and regular email, and follow CAN-SPAM rules. Justin Welsh's experience illustrates building an audience and learning its needs before scaling products, though his results are not typical.
:::

# Chapter 9: The Seven-Day Launch Test

:::objectives
- Plan a short, structured launch to a small audience.
- Write a launch email sequence that is honest and specific.
- Set measurable targets before launching.
- Interpret results to decide next steps.
:::

A first launch is an experiment. Its purpose is to learn whether the offer, price, and message work with real buyers, and to generate initial sales and feedback. A seven-day test, sent to an existing list and community, is long enough to reach most interested people and short enough to keep focus.

## Before the Test

Set targets in advance so that you judge results against expectations rather than emotions. Useful targets include email open rate, sales page visits, conversion rate from visit to purchase, number of sales, and refund requests. Prepare all emails, the sales page, and the checkout before day one.

## The Seven-Day Plan

- **Day 1: Announcement.** Explain the problem, the product, who it is for, what it includes, the price, and any launch pricing deadline.
- **Day 2: Story and example.** Show how the product works using a worked example.
- **Day 3: Questions.** Answer the most common questions, including who it is not for.
- **Day 4: Inside look.** Show screenshots or a short walkthrough.
- **Day 5: Early feedback.** Share genuine, permitted comments from early buyers, if any. Do not invent or exaggerate.
- **Day 6: Reminder.** Remind subscribers of the launch price deadline.
- **Day 7: Final notice.** State that launch pricing ends today, then end it as promised.

Interested subscribers who do not want launch emails should be able to opt out of the launch sequence without unsubscribing entirely, if your email service supports it.

## Writing Honest Launch Emails

Each email should have one purpose, use the buyer's language, and describe the product factually. Avoid false urgency, invented scarcity, and income claims. A real deadline, such as the end of a launch price, is acceptable when it is true.

:::example Seven-Day Scoreboard (illustrative)
Luis sends the sequence to 410 subscribers and posts in two groups. Targets: 45 percent open rate, 120 sales page visits, 5 percent visit-to-purchase conversion, 6 sales. Results: average open rate 52 percent; 186 sales page visits; 14 sales (9 Core at $39 launch price, 4 Complete at $79, 1 Team at $159); 7.5 percent conversion; 0 refund requests in the first week. Gross revenue is $351 + $316 + $159 = $826. The results exceed targets, but the sample is small. Luis treats them as evidence that the offer resonates, not as a forecast.
:::

| Metric | Target | Result | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Email open rate | 45% | 52% | List is engaged |
| Sales page visits | 120 | 186 | Message attracts interest |
| Visit-to-purchase | 5% | 7.5% | Offer and price are acceptable |
| Sales | 6 | 14 | Exceeds target (illustrative) |
| Refund requests (week 1) | 1 or fewer | 0 | Early satisfaction signal |

## Interpreting Results

- **Low opens:** subject lines or list health need attention.
- **Opens but few visits:** the email does not connect the problem to the product.
- **Visits but few sales:** the sales page, price, or offer needs work.
- **Sales but refunds:** the product does not match the promise, or quality needs work.

Change one major variable at a time in the next test so you can tell what caused any difference.

Target open rate, visits, conversion, sales: ___
Day 1 to Day 7 email subjects: ___
Questions to answer on Day 3: ___

:::

:::summary
Treat a first launch as an experiment. Set targets in advance, prepare all materials, and run a seven-day sequence with one purpose per email. Use honest, specific messaging and real deadlines only. Compare results to targets, diagnose where buyers drop off, and change one variable at a time in subsequent tests.

# Chapter 10: Measuring, Iterating, Maintaining

:::objectives
- Track a small set of metrics after launch.
- Collect and act on buyer feedback.
- Plan product updates and versioning.
- Recognize product decay and respond to it.
:::

A launch is the beginning of a product's life, not the end. Sales usually spike during a launch and then settle to a lower level. Products also age as tools, regulations, and buyer needs change. Measurement and maintenance determine whether a product remains useful and saleable.

## Core Metrics

Track monthly: sales page visits; conversion rate; sales by tier; refunds and refund reasons; net revenue after fees; support requests and their topics; and email list growth. A simple spreadsheet is sufficient.

## Buyer Feedback

Ask buyers, a week or two after purchase, three questions: What did you hope the product would help with? Did it help? What was confusing or missing? Record answers in a feedback log and look for patterns. Support requests are also feedback: repeated questions indicate unclear instructions.

## Iteration

Improve the product in small, scheduled releases. Fix errors immediately. Batch improvements into versions (1.1, 1.2) and tell existing buyers what changed. Many creators offer free updates to existing buyers, which builds trust; state your update policy clearly.

## Product Decay

Products decay when their content becomes outdated. Software interfaces change, rules change, prices change, and examples age. Nathan Barry's experience illustrates this: he wrote that sales of The App Design Handbook declined to about $1,000 per month as the book became outdated, and that the updated edition, developed with Jeremy Olson, generated $36,297 in its first 24 hours [13]. Updating an existing product with a proven audience can be more productive than creating a new one.

:::example Post-Launch Review (illustrative)
Three months after launch, Luis has 61 total sales, 3 refunds (two citing "too detailed for my small jobs," one a duplicate purchase), and 22 support emails. Eleven support emails ask how to handle panel upgrades with service relocation. He adds a dedicated worksheet for that job type in version 1.1, adds a simplified "Quick Estimate" tab for small jobs, and emails all buyers about the update. Support emails fall to four the following month. The improvements came from patterns in feedback, not guesses.
:::

| Signal | Possible meaning | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Repeated support questions | Instructions unclear or content missing | Add instructions or content |
| Refunds citing mismatch | Sales page over- or under-describes | Revise the sales page |
| Declining conversion | Message or price no longer fits | Test a revised offer |
| Declining sales over time | Product or audience outreach has aged | Update the product; resume outreach |
| Buyers asking for more | Demand for related products | Plan a next product |

:::checklist Monthly Product Review
- Metrics updated in tracking sheet.
- Refunds and reasons reviewed.
- Support questions categorized.
- Feedback log reviewed for patterns.
- Errors fixed and documented.
- Version notes drafted for any changes.
- Buyers notified of meaningful updates.
- Content checked for outdated facts, prices, or screenshots.
:::

:::summary
After launch, track a small set of metrics, collect buyer feedback, and treat support questions as signals. Improve the product in scheduled versions and notify buyers. Products decay as their content ages; Nathan Barry's update of The App Design Handbook shows that maintaining a proven product can be valuable. Respond to signals with targeted changes.
:::

# Chapter 11: Delegation and a Product Ladder

:::objectives
- Identify tasks to delegate using a buyback-rate approach.
- Document repeatable processes before handing them off.
- Design a product ladder of related offers.
- Avoid overexpansion that dilutes quality.
:::

As a product business grows, the creator's time becomes the constraint. Support, updates, marketing, and new products compete for limited hours. Two strategies help: delegating tasks that others can do, and building a coherent ladder of products that serves buyers at different stages.

## The Buyback Principle

Dan Martell, a software entrepreneur and author of Buy Back Your Time, discussed with Joe Polish on the Genius Network podcast the idea of systematically buying back time by delegating tasks that drain energy or have low value, so the founder can focus on high-value work [19]. An Entrepreneur article on the concept describes calculating a "buyback rate" from what your time is worth and using it to decide which tasks to pay others to handle [20]. For a small product business, delegation candidates include customer support, formatting, video editing, bookkeeping, and community moderation.

## Documenting Before Delegating

Delegation works when the task is documented. Record yourself doing the task once, write a short checklist, define what "done" looks like, and list decisions the helper may make without asking. Start with a small trial project and review the result.

## The Product Ladder

A product ladder is a sequence of related offers at increasing levels of depth and price. For example: a free resource; a core product; a complete product; a higher-touch offer such as a workshop, course, or done-with-you service. Each step should serve buyers who have outgrown the previous one. A ladder should grow from what buyers ask for, as revealed in feedback.

:::example Luis's Ladder (illustrative)
Free: Service Upgrade Labor Checklist. Core: Estimating Toolkit ($49). Complete: Toolkit plus commercial tables and templates ($99). Workshop: a quarterly live, two-hour estimating workshop for up to 20 contractors ($149 per seat). Luis hires a part-time virtual assistant at $22 per hour for six hours a week to answer routine support emails using a documented guide and to format update releases. That costs about $528 per month (6 hours x 4 weeks x $22) and frees about 24 hours a month for content and the workshop. The figures are illustrative; whether delegation pays depends on how the freed time is used.
:::

## Avoiding Overexpansion

New products are tempting, but each requires maintenance and support. Before adding one, ask: Does it solve a real problem buyers have asked about? Can I maintain it? Does it fit the ladder? It is often better to improve one excellent product than to add three mediocre ones.

| Task | Delegate? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Routine customer support | Often | Use a documented response guide |
| Formatting and design | Often | Provide templates and examples |
| Bookkeeping | Often | Use a qualified professional |
| Product content and expertise | Rarely | Core value; keep close |
| Strategy and pricing | Rarely | Owner decision |
| Community moderation | Sometimes | Clear rules required |

# Appendix J: Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should my product be?** As long as needed to solve the problem completely and no longer. Buyers value results and clarity, not page counts alone.

**Should I sell on a marketplace or my own site?** Marketplaces can add discovery but usually charge higher fees. Your own site, or a direct link from a hosted platform, gives more control. Many creators start with a hosted platform and direct traffic from their own audience.

**Do I need a business entity?** Many creators begin as sole proprietors. The SBA explains the tradeoffs between business structures [21]. Consult a professional for your situation.

**Can I use AI to write my product?** You can use AI as an assistant, but verify all content, add your own expertise, and understand that copyright protection depends on human authorship [7].

**What if no one buys?** Treat it as data. Review where buyers dropped off, return to problem conversations, and revise the offer, audience, or message before building something new.

# Glossary

**Accessibility.** Designing products so that people with disabilities can use them, often guided by WCAG.

**Break-even.** The number of sales at which net revenue equals fixed costs.

**CAN-SPAM Act.** The U.S. law setting rules for commercial email, enforced by the FTC.

**Conversion rate.** The percentage of visitors who take a desired action, such as purchasing.

**Copyright.** Legal protection for original works of authorship fixed in a tangible form.

**Digital product.** A product delivered electronically, such as an ebook, template, spreadsheet, or course.

**Fair use.** A limited legal defense permitting certain uses of copyrighted material, determined case by case.

**Merchant of record.** The party legally responsible for a sale, including collecting and remitting sales tax or VAT.

**Minimum complete version.** A focused product that solves the whole problem simply, without extras.

**Net revenue.** Revenue after platform fees, processing fees, and refunds.

**Owned channel.** A communication channel you control, such as an email list or website.

**Product decay.** Decline in a product's usefulness and sales as its content becomes outdated.

**Product ladder.** A sequence of related offers at increasing levels of depth and price.

**Refund rate.** The percentage of sales that are refunded.

**Rented channel.** A channel controlled by a third party, such as a social platform.

**Substantiation.** Evidence supporting an objective marketing claim.

**Tiered pricing.** Offering multiple packages at different prices and levels of value.

**Value equation.** Alex Hormozi's framework of four drivers of perceived value.

**VAT.** Value-added tax, a consumption tax applied in many countries, including on digital sales.

**WCAG.** Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, published by the W3C.
