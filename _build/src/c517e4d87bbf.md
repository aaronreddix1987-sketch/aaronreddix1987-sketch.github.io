# How to Use This Book

This book is written for people who are considering real estate wholesaling as a business activity and who want to understand it the way a careful practitioner, a regulator, and a skeptical lender would understand it. It is also written for small investors, real estate licensees, and operations staff who already work around wholesale transactions and want a structured, defensible process.

Most popular material on wholesaling begins with marketing: how to find motivated sellers, how to write a postcard, how to "lock up" a property. This book deliberately begins somewhere else. It begins with the law that applies where the property sits, with the duties you owe the people on the other side of the table, and with the arithmetic that determines whether a deal is real. Marketing comes later, because marketing an activity you are not permitted to conduct is the fastest way to lose money, reputation, and, in some states, the contract itself.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this book you should be able to:

- Explain, in plain language, what a wholesale transaction is, who the parties are, and what interest the wholesaler actually holds.
- Identify whether the jurisdiction where a property is located regulates wholesaling through licensing, registration, disclosure, cancellation rights, or a combination of these.
- Build a lead-generation process that respects the federal Telemarketing Sales Rule, the National Do Not Call Registry, and fair housing principles.
- Analyze a property using after-repair value, repair estimates, holding and closing costs, and a documented maximum allowable offer.
- Work with a real estate attorney and a title or escrow company to choose between assignment and a double closing, and to track every deadline.
- Recruit and verify end buyers rather than relying on unverified "cash buyer" lists.
- Run the operation as a system: written procedures, weekly metrics, delegation, and a formal go/no-go decision after a defined pilot period.

## How each chapter is built

Every chapter follows the same architecture so that you can study it, apply it, and return to it as a reference:

1. **Learning objectives** state what the chapter is meant to teach.
2. **Explanation and definitions** introduce the concepts in plain language.
3. **A step-by-step framework** turns the concepts into a procedure.
4. **A worked example** applies the framework with numbers. All worked examples in this book are illustrative and hypothetical. The people and companies in them are invented, and their figures demonstrate arithmetic only.
5. **Case studies** describe real, publicly documented laws, agencies, or businesses, with sources listed in the References chapter. Where a case involves a real business or person, their results are their own, not TTI's.
6. **A table, checklist, or worksheet** gives you a tool you can use immediately.
7. **A summary and review questions** help you confirm understanding.

The appendices contain full-page worksheets, scripts, and trackers that you may copy for your own use.

# Chapter 1: What Wholesaling Is, and What It Is Not

:::objectives
- Define real estate wholesaling and distinguish it from brokerage, flipping, and buy-and-hold investing.
- Identify the parties to a wholesale transaction and the interest each holds.
- Explain the difference between an assignment of contract and a double (simultaneous) closing.
- Describe where value is created, and where it is merely transferred, in a wholesale deal.
:::

## A working definition

In its common form, real estate wholesaling is a transaction in which a person or company enters into a contract to purchase a property and then, before closing, transfers the right to buy that property to another buyer in exchange for a fee or a price spread. The wholesaler typically never takes title in the ordinary sense, never renovates the property, and never lives in or rents it.

The Oregon Real Estate Agency offers a useful plain-language description: wholesaling is when someone markets a property they do not fully own yet, holding only a contract to buy it or an option to purchase it, and then sells or assigns the rights to buy the property to someone else for a fee or profit [1]. Pennsylvania's statute, amended in 2024, uses similar language: a wholesale transaction is "undertaking to promote the sale, exchange or purchase of an equitable interest or other interest in residential property with the intent to assign, sell or otherwise transfer the interest for a fee, commission or other valuable monetary consideration without having taken title as the owner of record" [4].

Two ideas in those definitions deserve emphasis because they recur throughout this book.

**Equitable interest.** When a buyer signs an enforceable purchase contract, the buyer acquires a contractual right to complete the purchase on the agreed terms. Lawyers often call this an equitable interest. It is not ownership. The seller still holds legal title, still bears most ordinary risks of ownership until closing (subject to the contract), and still has the right to insist that the contract's terms be followed.

**Marketing.** Many statutes focus on the act of marketing. The concern is not that a contract changes hands, which happens in ordinary commerce, but that a person who does not own a property is advertising it, negotiating its sale, and earning compensation in a way that looks very much like brokerage.

## The parties

A typical wholesale transaction involves at least five parties, and each has distinct interests:

| Party | What they hold or provide | What they typically want |
|---|---|---|
| Seller (owner of record) | Legal title to the property | A fair price, certainty of closing, speed, or relief from a burden |
| Wholesaler | A contractual right to purchase (equitable interest) | A fee or spread for finding the deal and arranging the transfer |
| End buyer | Capital and the intent to renovate, rent, or resell | A property priced low enough to support their own plan |
| Title or escrow company / closing attorney | Title search, insurance, escrow, settlement | A clean, insurable closing with correct documents |
| Regulator | Licensing, registration, disclosure, and enforcement authority | Consumer protection and honest dealing |

The wholesaler sits between the seller and the end buyer. That position is the source of both the business opportunity and the regulatory scrutiny.

## Assignment versus double closing

There are two common mechanics for completing a wholesale deal.

**Assignment of contract.** The wholesaler signs a purchase agreement with the seller that permits assignment. The wholesaler then signs an assignment agreement with the end buyer, transferring the right to purchase in exchange for an assignment fee. At closing, the end buyer purchases directly from the seller. The assignment fee is usually visible on the settlement statement, which means the seller can see what the wholesaler earned.

**Double closing (also called a simultaneous or back-to-back closing).** The wholesaler actually buys the property from the seller and, in a second transaction usually on the same day, sells it to the end buyer. The wholesaler briefly takes title. This may require funds for the first closing (sometimes called transactional funding), two sets of closing costs, and careful coordination with the title company and any lender, some of which have rules about how quickly a property can be resold.

Neither mechanic is inherently honest or dishonest. Each has legal, tax, and disclosure consequences that differ by jurisdiction. Chapter 6 addresses how to choose between them with counsel.

## Wholesaling compared with related activities

| Activity | Takes title? | Renovates? | Typical capital need | Main regulatory touchpoints |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wholesaling (assignment) | No | No | Low (earnest money, marketing) | Licensing/registration, disclosure, telemarketing |
| Wholesaling (double close) | Briefly | No | Moderate (transactional funds, two closings) | Same as above plus lender and title rules |
| Fix-and-flip | Yes | Yes | High | Permits, contractor licensing, lending, disclosure on resale |
| Buy-and-hold rental | Yes | Often | High | Landlord-tenant law, fair housing, local rental codes |
| Brokerage (licensed agent) | No | No | Low to moderate | Full licensing, agency duties, trust accounts |

## Where value comes from

A wholesale fee is only defensible when the wholesaler provides something the seller and buyer genuinely value. Legitimate sources of value include:

- **Speed and certainty** for a seller who cannot or does not want to list the property on the open market, make repairs, or wait months for a financed buyer.
- **Search and matching** for a buyer who wants properties that meet specific criteria and does not have the time to find them.
- **Problem solving** in situations involving probate, deferred maintenance, code violations, or title issues, where the wholesaler coordinates the professionals who resolve them.

Value is not created when a seller simply receives far less than the property is worth because the seller did not understand the transaction. That is a transfer of wealth from an uninformed person to an informed one, and it is the precise behavior that recent state laws were written to curb. A 2026 Governing report quoted a Missouri legislator describing wholesalers who target people "in duress" through foreclosure, bankruptcy, or divorce and seek contracts "well under market value" [6]. An ethical business model must be built so that it would survive full transparency to the seller.

:::example Illustrative: who earns what in a simple assignment
Assume a hypothetical seller agrees to sell a house for $142,000. A wholesaler assigns the contract to an investor for an assignment fee of $9,000, so the investor's total acquisition price is $151,000 before closing costs. The investor estimates the property will be worth $215,000 after $38,000 of repairs.

- Seller receives: $142,000 minus the seller's closing costs.
- Wholesaler receives: $9,000 minus marketing, earnest-money risk, and overhead.
- Investor's projected margin before holding and selling costs: $215,000 − $151,000 − $38,000 = $26,000.

Notice that the investor's margin is thin once holding costs, financing, and resale commissions are subtracted. If the wholesaler had demanded $20,000 instead of $9,000, the deal would likely fail for the investor, and the contract would collapse. The fee is constrained by the end buyer's math, not by the wholesaler's preference. All figures are hypothetical.
:::

:::checklist Before you go further
- I can explain the difference between legal title and an equitable interest.
- I understand that an assignment fee is typically visible to the seller at closing.
- I know that a double closing means I briefly take title and may need funds.
- I can state, in one sentence, what value I would provide to a seller and to a buyer.
- I accept that the end buyer's numbers, not my preference, limit my fee.
:::

:::summary
Wholesaling is the transfer of a contractual right to purchase real estate, usually for a fee, without the wholesaler holding ordinary ownership. It involves a seller, a wholesaler, an end buyer, a closing professional, and, increasingly, a regulator. It can be completed by assignment or by double closing. A sustainable wholesale business creates value through speed, certainty, matching, and problem solving, and it is designed to withstand full disclosure to every party.
:::

# Chapter 2: The Regulatory Map

:::objectives
- Describe the main regulatory approaches states use for wholesaling: licensing, registration, disclosure, and cancellation rights.
- Summarize the published rules in Oregon, Illinois, Pennsylvania, Missouri, and Rhode Island as examples.
- Prepare a written description of your proposed activity for review by a regulator and an attorney.
- Maintain a regulatory log that documents what you asked, whom you asked, and what you were told.
:::

## Why the map comes first

For many years, wholesaling occupied a gray area. Real estate license laws generally require a license to sell, negotiate, or advertise real estate for another person for compensation. Wholesalers argued that they were acting for themselves, as principals who held a contract, and therefore were not acting "for another." Regulators and legislatures in a growing number of states have closed that gap. According to Governing, as of August 2026, 20 states had active rules or regulatory regimes for home wholesalers, and Delaware was phasing in a policy that had not yet taken full effect [6]. The same report noted that no state had banned wholesaling outright, although a ban had been proposed in West Virginia [6].

The practical lesson is simple: the first question for any deal is not "Is this a good property?" but "What does the law require of me where this property is located?"

## Four regulatory tools

States generally use one or more of four tools:

1. **Licensing.** The wholesaler must hold a real estate license, often under a sponsoring broker, and is subject to the full code of conduct that applies to licensees.
2. **Registration.** The wholesaler registers with the state as a wholesaler without obtaining a full license, and accepts specific conduct and disclosure duties.
3. **Disclosure.** The wholesaler must give sellers, buyers, or both a written notice explaining the nature of the transaction, sometimes on a state-prescribed form and sometimes within a set time before signing.
4. **Cancellation rights.** The seller (and sometimes the buyer) receives a statutory right to cancel the contract within a period, or at any time if required disclosures were not provided.

## Five published examples

The following summaries describe published rules as reported by official or reputable sources at the time of writing. They are examples for study, not a substitute for current legal advice, and they should never be transplanted to another state.

:::case Oregon: registration or license, plus written disclosure
The Oregon Real Estate Agency states that a person who engages in residential property wholesaling in Oregon must either be registered as a Residential Property Wholesaler or hold a real estate license as a broker, principal broker, or property manager [1]. Oregon defines residential property wholesaling as marketing residential property where the marketer has only an equitable interest or option, has held it for fewer than 90 days at the time of marketing, and has invested less than $10,000 in development or improvement costs [1]. The wholesaler must provide a state written disclosure to potential sellers and buyers before entering a contract, to brokers involved on either side, and in all advertising, with a limited social-media exception where the post links to a page containing the disclosure [1]. If the disclosure is not given to the seller before contracting, the agency states that the seller may cancel at any time without penalty and keep any earnest money paid; unregistered or unlicensed wholesaling may lead to civil penalties and a cease-and-desist order [1]. The program was created by Oregon Laws 2024, chapter 3 (HB 4058) [1].
:::

:::case Illinois: a broker license after two transactions in 12 months
Illinois amended its Real Estate License Act in 2019 (Public Act 101-0357). The definition of "broker" now includes anyone who, "whether for another or themselves," engages in a pattern of business of buying, selling, offering to buy or sell, marketing for sale, exchanging, or otherwise dealing in contracts, including assignable contracts, for real estate [3]. A pattern of business means engaging in one or more of these practices on two or more occasions in any 12-month period [3]. The Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation summarized the change by stating that a broker's license is required for this activity at least twice in a 12-month period [2]. Illinois REALTORS legal guidance added that licensees who wholesale must disclose their licensee status in writing and provide notices of no agency [7].
:::

:::case Pennsylvania: license, contract disclosures, and a 30-day cancellation right
Pennsylvania's Act 52 of 2024, enacted from SB 1173 on July 8, 2024, amended the Real Estate Licensing and Registration Act [5]. It defines "wholesale transaction" and adds it to the activities that require broker licensure [4]. A sales agreement for a wholesale transaction must prominently state that it is a wholesale transaction, that the consumer may obtain an appraisal or consult an unaffiliated licensee or lawyer, that the consumer may cancel until midnight of the 30th day after signing or until conveyance, whichever comes first, and that payments will be refunded within ten business days of a cancellation notice [4]. If the agreement lacks the required information, the consumer may cancel at any time before conveyance, and the right to cancel cannot be waived [4].
:::

:::case Missouri and Rhode Island: disclosure-led and hybrid approaches
Governing reported in November 2026 that Missouri's law, effective in late August 2026, requires wholesalers to give sellers written notice at least 14 days before entering a contract, stating that they are dealing with a wholesaler, that the wholesaler seeks its own profit and intends to sell its contract interest, and that the price may be below market value, and recommending legal advice [6]. Without the signed notice, the seller may cancel without penalty, and the seller or the attorney general may sue [6]. Rhode Island combines tools: a law taking effect in January 2027 requires broker licensing, disclosures to both sellers and buyers, and a three-business-day review period during which either may cancel without a fee [6].
:::

| Oregon | Registration or license [1] | Yes, before contract | Yes, before contract | Seller may cancel if disclosure missing |
| Illinois | Broker license if pattern of business [3] | Licensee status, no-agency notice [7] | Yes [7] | Not described in sources cited |

## The regulatory inquiry framework

Use this five-step process before marketing any property in a new jurisdiction.

**Step 1: Describe the activity precisely.** Write one page describing who you are, what entity you will use, what property type and location you are targeting, how you will find sellers, what contract you will sign, whether you intend to assign or double close, how you will advertise, who will pay you, and when.

**Step 2: Locate the regulator.** Identify the state real estate commission or agency for the property's location. Read its published pages on wholesaling, unlicensed activity, and advertising.

**Step 3: Ask specific questions in writing.** Ask whether your described activity requires a license, registration, or neither; whether a state disclosure form exists; whether advertising rules apply; and whether there are cancellation rights. Regulators often cannot give legal advice, but they can point you to the governing statute, rules, and forms.

**Step 4: Obtain an attorney's review.** Provide the same one-page description to a real estate attorney licensed in that state. Ask for a written opinion or at least written notes on licensing, contract terms, disclosures, assignment, earnest money, and closing.

**Step 5: Record everything.** Keep a regulatory log (Appendix A) with the date, the person contacted, the question, the answer, and any open issues. If an answer is unclear, do not treat silence as permission.

:::example Illustrative: the one-page activity description
"Northfield Property Partners LLC (a hypothetical company) intends to contract to purchase one-to-four-unit residential properties in [County], [State], from owners who respond to direct mail. We intend to sign a purchase agreement that permits assignment, deposit $1,000 in earnest money with a title company, and, within 21 days, either assign the contract to a verified investor for a disclosed assignment fee or terminate under the inspection contingency. We will advertise the contract on our website and by email to investors who have opted in. We will not hold title. We expect to complete fewer than six transactions in the next 12 months. Questions: Does this activity require a license or registration? Is there a required disclosure form for sellers, buyers, or advertising? Are there cancellation rights or waiting periods?"

This description gives a regulator and an attorney enough facts to identify the issues. It is hypothetical.
:::

# Chapter 3: Ethics and the Distressed Seller

:::objectives
- Explain why seller vulnerability is the central ethical issue in wholesaling.
- Apply a disclosure-first conversation structure with every seller.
- Recognize pressure tactics and replace them with informed-consent practices.
- Use a fairness test before presenting any offer.
:::

## Why ethics is an operating issue, not a slogan

Wholesalers commonly work with owners who need to sell quickly or who own properties in poor condition. Governing summarized the typical situations: inherited properties, divorce, inability to maintain the home, or inability to afford the payments [6]. These are exactly the circumstances in which a person is least able to compare options, read a contract carefully, or resist pressure. The legislative trend described in Chapter 2 is, at bottom, a response to conduct that took advantage of those circumstances.

For a practitioner, ethics is therefore not a matter of personal virtue alone. It is a risk-control system. Contracts obtained through pressure or misunderstanding are more likely to be cancelled, litigated, reported to a regulator, or refused by a title company. A business that depends on sellers not understanding the deal is fragile; a business that would still function if every seller fully understood the deal is durable.

## Core duties to observe even where the law is silent

Even in a jurisdiction with no wholesaling statute, general contract law, consumer protection statutes, and common decency suggest several duties:

- **Truthful identity.** State who you are, which company you represent, and that you are a buyer acting for your own account who may assign the contract. Never imply you are a family, a charity, a government program, or the seller's agent when you are not.
- **Truthful intent.** If you intend to assign the contract or resell the property quickly, say so. Several states now require exactly this disclosure [1][4][6].
- **No false urgency.** Do not invent deadlines, competing offers, or threats about foreclosure timelines you have not verified.
- **Encourage independent advice.** Invite the seller to consult a lawyer, a licensed agent, a housing counselor, or family before signing. Pennsylvania requires a statement that the consumer may obtain an appraisal or consult a lawyer or unaffiliated licensee [4]; good practice extends that invitation everywhere.
- **Clear exits.** Explain the inspection period, the closing date, and what happens if you cannot close, including what happens to earnest money.

## The disclosure-first conversation

A disclosure-first conversation places the uncomfortable facts at the beginning, not at the end. It has six movements:

1. **Introduce and disclose.** "My name is ___ with ___. We buy properties for our own account. Sometimes we close ourselves, and sometimes we assign our contract to another investor for a fee. I want you to know that up front."
2. **Listen to the situation.** Ask open questions: What is prompting the sale? What timeline matters to you? What has been tried already?
3. **Present options, including ones that do not involve you.** Listing with an agent, selling to a neighbor, loan modification or housing counseling for owners facing foreclosure, or simply waiting.
4. **Explain your offer and its basis.** Show the after-repair value estimate, the repair estimate, and the costs the buyer will bear. Sellers deserve to know why the number is lower than retail.
5. **Explain the process and exits.** Timeline, inspection, earnest money, the role of the title company, and any statutory cancellation right.
6. **Invite review.** Offer to send the contract in advance and encourage the seller to review it with a lawyer or trusted person.

## Pressure tactics versus informed consent

| Pressure tactic to avoid | Informed-consent alternative |
|---|---|
| "This offer expires tonight." | "Take the time you need. Here is the contract to review with anyone you trust." |
| Signing at the kitchen table on the first visit | Leave a copy; schedule signing after the seller has reviewed it |
| Vague description of "our investors" | "We may assign this contract to another investor and earn a fee, which will appear on the closing statement." |
| Implying you can stop a foreclosure | "I cannot give legal advice. A HUD-approved housing counselor or attorney can explain your options." |
| Discouraging calls to an agent | "You may want to ask a listing agent what the house might sell for on the open market." |
| Using a lowball number with no explanation | Show the arithmetic and the comparable sales used |

## The fairness test

Before presenting an offer, answer five questions in writing:

1. Would I be comfortable if the seller's adult child, attorney, or a regulator read every word I said and wrote?
2. Have I disclosed that I may assign the contract or resell, and does the seller understand that?
3. Does the seller understand roughly what the property might sell for if repaired and listed, and why my offer differs?
4. Has the seller had a genuine opportunity to seek independent advice?
5. If the seller cancels, will I honor that without retaliation or penalty beyond what the law and contract fairly allow?

If any answer is "no" or "not sure," do not present the offer yet.

:::example Illustrative: a fairness test in practice
A hypothetical wholesaler, Dana, speaks with an elderly owner of a house that needs a new roof and foundation repair. The owner says she "just wants it gone" and is willing to sign today at $85,000. Dana's analysis suggests a repaired value of about $190,000 and repairs of about $55,000.

Dana's fairness test flags question 3: the owner does not appear to understand the repaired value. Dana explains, in writing, that a renovated house in the neighborhood might sell for around $190,000, that repairs could cost around $55,000, and that a buyer would also pay holding, financing, and selling costs. She suggests the owner ask her son, who lives nearby, and a listing agent for their views. The owner's son calls a week later; the family decides to list with an agent as-is. Dana loses the deal but avoids a contract that could have been cancelled, challenged, or reported. All details are hypothetical.
:::

:::case Missouri's 14-day notice as an ethical design pattern
Governing reported that Missouri's 2026 wholesaling law requires written notice at least 14 days before contracting, including a statement that the wholesaler is seeking its own profit and may offer a below-market price, along with a recommendation to seek legal advice [6]. Even if you work elsewhere, the structure is instructive. It separates the first conversation from the signing, it names the conflict of interest plainly, and it points the seller toward independent advice. A practitioner can adopt a voluntary "cooling period" and plain-language conflict statement as standard operating procedure.
:::

:::checklist Seller protection standards
- I disclose my identity, my company, and my intent to assign or resell at the first contact.
- I never sign a contract at the first meeting.
- I provide the full contract in advance and invite independent review.
- I show the seller the basis for my offer, including repaired value and repairs.
- I refer owners facing foreclosure to a housing counselor or attorney.
- I document each conversation in my CRM the same day.
- I honor statutory and contractual cancellation rights promptly and politely.
:::

:::summary
Distressed sellers are the population most exposed to unfair wholesale practices, which is why regulators now focus on disclosure and cancellation rights. A disclosure-first conversation, a refusal to use pressure tactics, and a written fairness test protect sellers and, in turn, protect the durability of the business.
:::

# Chapter 4: Lawful Lead Generation

:::objectives
- Describe the core requirements of the FTC Telemarketing Sales Rule as they relate to seller outreach.
- Explain how the National Do Not Call Registry and entity-specific do-not-call lists work.
- Design outreach channels that rely on permission, inquiry, and mail rather than cold robocalls or unsolicited texts.
- Apply fair housing principles to marketing and seller selection.
:::

## The lead-generation problem

Wholesalers need sellers, and many marketing courses recommend high-volume cold calling, ringless voicemail, and mass text messaging to "skip-traced" lists. Federal law imposes substantial limits on these practices, and violations carry large per-call penalties. The FTC states that companies that illegally call numbers on the National Do Not Call Registry or place illegal robocalls can be fined up to $50,120 per call [9], and its business guidance for the Telemarketing Sales Rule cites civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation [8]. Penalty amounts are adjusted periodically, so check the current figure.

This chapter is not a full legal treatment of telemarketing law. The Federal Communications Commission also regulates calls and texts under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, and many states have their own telemarketing and texting rules. Ask counsel to review any calling or texting program before launch.

## What the Telemarketing Sales Rule requires

The FTC's guidance defines telemarketing as a plan, program, or campaign to induce the purchase of goods or services involving more than one interstate telephone call [8]. Whether a particular wholesaler's calls to sellers are covered is a question for counsel, but the safest course is to design your process as if the rule's core protections apply. The FTC's consumer guidance summarizes several of them [9]:

- Calls may not be made before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time.
- Callers must promptly state who they represent and that the call is a sales call, and must not be deceptive or abusive.
- Callers must transmit their phone number, and name if possible, to caller ID.
- A prerecorded sales message (robocall) is illegal unless the company has written permission directly from the consumer.
- If a consumer asks not to be called, the company must stop, even if there is an existing business relationship [10].

## The National Do Not Call Registry

The FTC explains that sellers and telemarketers covered by the rule must access the Registry, pay the applicable fee for the area codes they call, and remove registered numbers from their lists at least every 31 days [8]. A company may call a consumer with whom it has an established business relationship for up to 18 months after the last purchase, delivery, or payment, and for up to three months after the consumer makes an inquiry or application [10]. A company may also call a consumer who has given express written agreement, which must include the number to be called and the consumer's signature [8].

The FTC also describes a safe harbor for errors: a seller that has written do-not-call procedures, trains staff, maintains an entity-specific do-not-call list, uses a Registry version no more than 31 days old, monitors compliance, and can show the call was an error may avoid penalties for that call [8].

## A permission-first channel strategy

Because cold outreach is legally complex, a permission-first strategy is both safer and, often, more efficient. The idea is to invite sellers to raise their hands, then respond quickly and respectfully.

| Channel | How it works | Compliance notes |
|---|---|---|
| Direct mail (letters, postcards) | Mailed offer or information to owners | Not covered by telemarketing rules, but must not be deceptive; include required state disclosures |
| Website and search | Seller submits an inquiry form | Inquiry may create a 3-month window for calls under the TSR [10]; obtain express written consent for texts or automated contact |
| Referral partners | Attorneys, probate professionals, contractors refer clients | Check whether referral fees are lawful in your state; disclose relationships |
| Driving for dollars with mail follow-up | Note distressed properties, mail the owner | Respect privacy and trespass laws; do not leave notes that imply official authority |
| Inbound calls from signs or ads | Owner calls you | Follow call disclosures; comply with local sign ordinances |
| Cold calling | Outbound calls to skip-traced numbers | Highest risk; requires DNC scrubbing, entity-specific list, time limits, and counsel review |

## Fair housing in marketing and selection

The federal Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination in housing-related transactions based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability [11]. Wholesalers should apply this in at least three ways: do not target or exclude owners based on protected characteristics or on proxies for them; do not use advertising language or imagery that signals a preference; and apply the same buyer qualification standards to every prospective end buyer. Many states and cities add further protected classes.

## The outreach framework

**Step 1: Choose channels.** Start with direct mail and inbound inquiry channels. Add outbound calling only after counsel has approved a written compliance program.

**Step 2: Build disclosures into every piece.** Include your company name, a statement that you buy for your own account and may assign, and any state-required disclosure language.

**Step 3: Capture consent correctly.** On web forms, use a clear, separate consent statement for calls and texts that names your company and the number, and do not make consent a condition of service.

**Step 4: Maintain your internal do-not-contact list.** Record every opt-out the same day and suppress it across all channels.

**Step 5: Measure.** Track cost per inquiry, inquiry-to-appointment rate, and appointment-to-contract rate for each channel.

:::example Illustrative: comparing two channels
A hypothetical wholesaler tests two channels over 90 days in one county.

- Direct mail: 3,000 letters at $0.75 each = $2,250. Inquiries: 36 (1.2%). Cost per inquiry: $2,250 ÷ 36 = $62.50. Appointments: 12. Contracts: 2. Cost per contract: $1,125.
- Search ads to a seller-inquiry page: $1,800 spend. Inquiries: 24. Cost per inquiry: $75. Appointments: 10. Contracts: 1. Cost per contract: $1,800.

On these hypothetical numbers, mail produced contracts more cheaply in this period. The sample is small, so the wholesaler continues both for another 90 days before deciding. None of these response rates should be assumed for a real campaign.
:::

:::checklist Outreach compliance
- Counsel has reviewed every calling and texting program before launch.
- If I make covered calls, I have a Registry subscription for each area code and scrub at least every 31 days.
- I maintain and enforce an entity-specific do-not-call list.
- I call only between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. recipient local time.
- I never send prerecorded sales messages without written permission.
- All mail and ads include required disclosures and avoid any official-looking design.
- My targeting and language comply with fair housing law.
:::

:::summary
Lead generation is where many wholesalers incur legal risk. The Telemarketing Sales Rule, the National Do Not Call Registry, the TCPA, state laws, and fair housing law all shape what outreach is permitted. A permission-first strategy built on mail, inquiries, and referrals, with documented consent and suppression lists, reduces risk and produces measurable results.
:::

# Chapter 5: Property Analysis and the Maximum Allowable Offer

:::objectives
- Estimate after-repair value (ARV) using comparable sales.
- Build a structured repair estimate with contingency.
- Calculate the end buyer's total costs and a defensible maximum allowable offer.
- Run sensitivity analysis to see how errors change the outcome.
:::

## Why analysis protects everyone

A wholesale deal works only if three numbers fit together: what the seller will accept, what the end buyer can pay, and what the wholesaler needs to cover cost and risk. Poor analysis harms everyone. An inflated ARV or understated repair estimate attracts a buyer who later loses money, damages the wholesaler's reputation, and may lead to a failed closing that leaves the seller worse off than before.

## After-repair value

After-repair value is an estimate of what the property would likely sell for after the planned renovation, in the current market. It is an opinion, not a fact. Build it from recent sales of comparable properties:

- **Similarity.** Same neighborhood or a closely matched one, similar size, bedrooms, bathrooms, lot, age, and style.
- **Recency.** Sales within the last three to six months where volume allows.
- **Condition.** Comparables should be renovated to the standard the buyer will achieve, not luxury flips if the plan is a basic rental-grade rehab.
- **Adjustments.** Adjust for differences such as an extra bathroom or a garage, and document each adjustment.

Use at least three comparables, record the source of each, and report ARV as a range rather than a single number. When in doubt, ask a licensed agent or appraiser; in some states the seller has an explicit right to obtain an appraisal [4].

## Repair estimates

Divide the property into systems and estimate each separately. A walk-through checklist (Appendix B) prevents omissions.

| System | Questions to ask | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Roof | Age, leaks, number of layers | Photos, attic inspection |
| Foundation and structure | Cracks, settling, moisture | Structural engineer if signs exist |
| Electrical | Panel type, amperage, wiring | Licensed electrician quote |
| Plumbing | Supply lines, drains, water heater | Plumber inspection, sewer scope |
| HVAC | Age, function, ductwork | Contractor assessment |
| Interior finishes | Floors, paint, kitchen, baths | Square footage and finish level |
| Exterior | Siding, windows, drainage | Photos, measurements |
| Permits and code | Open permits, violations | Municipal records |

Add a contingency, commonly 10 to 20 percent, to reflect unknowns. Older properties and properties with visible water damage deserve the higher end.

## The end buyer's cost stack

The buyer does not merely pay the purchase price and repairs. A realistic cost stack includes:

- Acquisition price (seller price plus any assignment fee)
- Buyer's closing costs on purchase
- Repairs plus contingency
- Holding costs: interest or cost of capital, taxes, insurance, utilities, security
- Selling costs: commissions, seller closing costs, concessions
- Required profit margin for the buyer's risk and effort

## The maximum allowable offer

The maximum allowable offer (MAO) to the seller is what remains after subtracting all buyer costs, the buyer's required profit, and the wholesaler's fee from the ARV:

MAO = ARV − repairs − contingency − buying costs − holding costs − selling costs − buyer profit − wholesale fee

Some investors use a shortcut, such as a fixed percentage of ARV minus repairs. Shortcuts can be useful for screening, but they ignore local costs and should never replace a full cost stack for an actual offer.

:::example Illustrative: building an MAO
A hypothetical three-bedroom house has three renovated comparables that sold for $228,000, $236,000, and $241,000. After adjustments, the analyst sets ARV at $230,000 to $238,000 and uses the conservative $230,000 for the offer.

| Line item | Amount |
|---|---|
| ARV (conservative) | $230,000 |
| Repairs (roof, kitchen, baths, flooring, paint) | −$42,000 |
| Contingency (15% of repairs) | −$6,300 |
| Buyer purchase closing costs | −$3,000 |
| Holding costs (6 months) | −$9,600 |
| Selling costs (about 7% of ARV) | −$16,100 |
| Buyer profit target (about 12% of ARV) | −$27,600 |
| Wholesale fee | −$8,000 |
| **Maximum allowable offer to seller** | **$117,400** |

Holding costs here assume a hypothetical $1,600 per month for interest, taxes, insurance, and utilities. If the seller wants $125,000, the gap is $7,600. The wholesaler may reduce the fee, find a buyer with lower costs, or decline. Inflating ARV to "make it work" is not an option. All figures are illustrative.
:::

## Sensitivity analysis

Because ARV and repairs are estimates, test how the MAO changes when they are wrong.

| Scenario | ARV | Repairs (incl. contingency) | Resulting MAO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base case | $230,000 | $48,300 | $117,400 |
| ARV 5% lower | $218,500 | $48,300 | about $108,100 |
| Repairs 20% higher | $230,000 | $57,960 | about $107,700 |
| Both adverse | $218,500 | $57,960 | about $98,400 |

The calculations scale selling costs and buyer profit with ARV. The lesson is that relatively small estimation errors can move the safe offer by $10,000 to $20,000. Communicate ranges honestly to both seller and buyer.

# Chapter 6: Contracts, Assignment, and Double Closings

:::objectives
- Identify the key clauses in a purchase agreement used for wholesaling.
- Explain the roles of earnest money, contingencies, and closing dates.
- Compare assignment and double closing in terms of disclosure, cost, and risk.
- Work with an attorney and title company to select and document the right structure.
:::

## The contract is the product

In wholesaling, the wholesaler's only asset is a contract. If the contract is poorly drafted, unenforceable, non-assignable, or obtained without required disclosures, the asset is impaired or worthless. Several states now give sellers the right to cancel wholesale contracts that lack prescribed disclosures [1][4][6]. For that reason, contracts should be drafted or reviewed by an attorney licensed where the property is located, and state-required forms should be used where they exist.

## Key clauses

| Clause | Purpose | Practitioner notes |
|---|---|---|
| Parties and vesting | Identifies buyer and seller | Use your entity name; "and/or assigns" language must be explained to the seller |
| Assignment | Permits transfer of buyer's rights | Confirm assignment is lawful and disclosed under state law |
| Purchase price and deposit | Sets price and earnest money | Deposit held by a neutral title or escrow company |
| Inspection or due diligence contingency | Allows buyer to inspect and terminate | Must be genuine; use it for inspection, not as a hidden marketing period |
| Title contingency | Requires marketable title | Title search reveals liens, judgments, probate issues |
| Closing date | Sets timeline | Realistic for title work and buyer funding |
| Access | Allows showings to buyers and contractors | Agree on notice and conduct |
| Required disclosures | Statutory wholesale statements | Use state forms; Pennsylvania lists specific statements [4] |
| Default and remedies | What happens if a party fails to perform | Understand earnest-money consequences |

## Earnest money

Earnest money demonstrates good faith. It should be deposited with a neutral escrow holder, never paid directly to the seller or held by the wholesaler. The contract should state when it becomes non-refundable. A wholesaler who cannot close and has no valid contingency may forfeit the deposit; that risk is a real cost of doing business and should be in your financial model.

## Contingencies and good faith

Inspection contingencies exist so a buyer can learn about the property. Using an inspection contingency solely to search for a buyer, while telling the seller you are inspecting, raises honesty concerns. A more transparent approach is to disclose that you will be seeking an investor during the contract period and to state that clearly in the contract and in conversation.

## Choosing a structure

| Factor | Assignment | Double closing |
|---|---|---|
| Wholesaler takes title | No | Yes, briefly |
| Fee visibility | Typically on settlement statement | Spread may be less visible, but disclosure duties still apply |
| Capital required | Earnest money | Funds for first purchase or transactional funding |
| Closing costs | One set (paid per contract) | Two sets |
| Lender issues | End buyer's lender may restrict assignments | Some lenders restrict rapid resale |
| Title insurance | One policy | Two transactions; coordinate with title |
| Regulatory treatment | Varies by state | Varies by state; still may be regulated activity |

Counsel should advise on which structure is lawful and appropriate. Do not choose a double closing to avoid disclosure duties; several statutes are written broadly enough to reach the activity regardless of structure.

## The contract workflow

**Step 1: Prepare documents in advance.** Have counsel prepare a purchase agreement, an assignment agreement, and any required disclosure forms.

**Step 2: Deliver disclosures before signing.** Follow the state's timing rules, such as Missouri's 14-day notice [6] or Oregon's pre-contract disclosure [1].

**Step 3: Sign and open escrow.** Deliver the signed contract and earnest money to the title company within the contract's deadline.

**Step 4: Order title.** Ask the title company to begin the search immediately so that problems surface early.

**Step 5: Market only as permitted.** Follow advertising disclosure rules (Chapter 8).

**Step 6: Assign or proceed to double close.** Sign the assignment agreement with the verified buyer, collect the buyer's non-refundable deposit if applicable, and send all documents to title.

**Step 7: Close or terminate cleanly.** If you cannot perform, terminate within the contingency period and inform the seller promptly and respectfully.

:::example Illustrative: the cost of a failed contract
A hypothetical wholesaler places $2,000 in earnest money and lets the inspection period expire without securing a buyer. The contract does not permit termination after that date. The wholesaler cannot find a buyer at the required price and defaults. The seller's remedy under this hypothetical contract is to keep the deposit.

Annualized across a pilot, if the wholesaler signs 10 contracts and defaults on 3 with $2,000 deposits each, the cost is $6,000, plus damaged trust with sellers. Building a buyer list before contracting (Chapter 7) and using honest, adequately long contingency periods reduces this risk. All figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Contract readiness
- An attorney licensed where the property is located has reviewed my forms.
- I am using state-required disclosure forms where they exist.
- Earnest money will be held by a neutral escrow holder.
- My contingency periods are genuine and adequate for title work and inspection.
- I have explained assignment or resale intent to the seller in plain language.
- I know my termination deadline and have it on my calendar.
:::

:::summary
The contract is the wholesaler's only asset, so its drafting and disclosures matter. Earnest money should be escrowed, contingencies should be honest, and the choice between assignment and double closing should be made with counsel, considering disclosure, capital, lender rules, and state law.
:::

# Chapter 7: Building and Verifying a Buyer List

:::objectives
- Explain why verified buyers, not large lists, determine whether contracts close.
- Collect structured buyer criteria through an intake process.
- Verify proof of funds and track record before assigning a contract.
- Segment buyers and communicate with them responsibly.
:::

## The myth of the big list

Many wholesalers boast of buyer lists with thousands of names. A list is only useful if the people on it have money, clear criteria, a history of closing, and permission to be contacted. A list of 40 verified buyers who close is more valuable than 4,000 email addresses collected from a sign-up form.

## Where legitimate buyers come from

- **Public records.** Recent cash purchases recorded in the county can identify active investors. Contact them by mail or through their published business channels.
- **Local investor associations and meetups.** Real estate investor clubs often host members who renovate or hold rentals.
- **Property managers and contractors.** They know which investors are actively buying.
- **Licensed agents who serve investors.** Some agents specialize in investor clients and may cooperate, subject to state rules.
- **Inbound inquiries.** A buyer page on your website where investors submit their criteria and consent to contact.

## The buyer intake

An intake interview or form should capture at least:

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Legal name and entity | Needed for assignment agreement and title |
| Target areas (ZIP codes, neighborhoods) | Avoids sending irrelevant deals |
| Property types and size | Single-family, small multifamily, land |
| Strategy | Flip, rental, short-term rental, development |
| Price range and maximum repair budget | Screens for capacity |
| Funding source | Cash, private lender, hard-money loan, bank |
| Proof of funds | Bank statement or lender letter, current |
| Closing history | Recent purchases with addresses for verification |
| Decision timeline | How quickly they can inspect and commit |
| Contact consent and preferences | Email, phone, text consent recorded |

## Verification

Verification protects the seller as much as the wholesaler. Before assigning a contract:

1. **Proof of funds.** Request a recent bank statement (account numbers redacted) or a lender's letter that matches the purchase price. Confirm the lender is real by contacting it through a publicly listed number, not one supplied by the buyer.
2. **Track record.** Check one or two recent purchases in county records.
3. **Entity check.** Confirm the buyer's entity is in good standing with the state's business registry.
4. **Non-refundable deposit.** Require an assignment deposit delivered to the title company, so that a buyer who walks away bears a cost.

## Segmenting and communicating

Group buyers by area, strategy, and price. Send each deal only to buyers whose criteria fit. Over-sending trains buyers to ignore you and increases the risk of misrepresenting a deal as broadly "hot." Every deal notice should include required disclosures (Chapter 8), clear facts, photos, the basis for the ARV and repair estimate, and the assignment terms.

:::example Illustrative: list quality versus list size
Wholesaler A has 2,500 email subscribers and sends every deal to all of them. Over six months, eight deals are sent; average responses per deal: 14; contracts assigned: 2. Wholesaler B has 55 verified buyers segmented into four groups. Over the same period, eight deals are sent only to matching segments; average responses: 6; contracts assigned: 6.

B's smaller list produced three times as many assignments in this hypothetical, because buyers trusted the deals matched their criteria and were funded. The numbers are invented to illustrate the principle.
:::

# Chapter 8: Marketing the Contract Honestly

:::objectives
- Identify advertising rules that apply to wholesale contracts in example jurisdictions.
- Write property marketing that accurately describes what is being offered.
- Avoid misrepresentation of ownership, condition, value, and urgency.
- Respect the seller's privacy and property during marketing.
:::

## You are marketing a contract, not a house

Strictly speaking, a wholesaler who holds only an equitable interest is not selling the house. The wholesaler is offering to assign a contract right or to sell the property after acquiring it. Marketing should say so. Oregon requires the state wholesaler disclosure in all advertising of the property, with a limited exception for social-media posts that link to a page containing the disclosure [1]. Illinois brings marketing assignable contracts within the definition of brokerage activity once the pattern-of-business threshold is met [3]. Other states may treat advertising by a non-owner as an activity requiring a license.

## Principles of honest property marketing

- **State the interest.** "We hold a contract to purchase this property and intend to assign our contract" is accurate; "We own this property" is not.
- **Describe condition honestly.** Share known defects. If you have not inspected a system, say so.
- **Show the basis for numbers.** Provide the comparables behind ARV and the scope behind the repair estimate. Label both as estimates.
- **Avoid false urgency.** Do not claim multiple offers that do not exist.
- **Protect the seller.** Do not publish the seller's personal circumstances. Arrange showings with notice and respect occupants.
- **Follow fair housing advertising principles** [11].

## Anatomy of a compliant deal notice

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Header | Address (or general location if seller requests), property type, beds/baths, size |
| Interest statement | Nature of your interest and intent to assign or resell; state disclosure |
| Price terms | Assignment price or total purchase price; assignment fee disclosed |
| Condition | Known issues, inspection status, photos |
| Estimates | ARV range with comparables; repair scope and estimate, labelled as estimates |
| Process | Showing schedule, deposit requirements, title company, closing timeline |
| Disclaimers | Buyers must perform their own due diligence; no guarantee of value or profit |

:::example Illustrative: rewriting a misleading notice
Original (misleading): "OFF-MARKET STEAL! We own this beauty, needs light cosmetics, ARV $260K, will go fast, multiple offers!"

Rewrite: "Contract assignment available: 3 bed / 1.5 bath, 1,240 sq ft, built 1958. We hold a contract to purchase this property and intend to assign it; our assignment fee is $8,500 and is included in the price below. Total buyer price: $131,500. Condition: roof approximately 20 years old per seller; kitchen and baths dated; electrical panel not yet inspected. ARV estimate $225,000 to $238,000 based on three renovated sales (attached). Our repair estimate is $40,000 to $48,000; buyers should obtain their own bids. Showings Saturday 10 a.m. to noon. Title: [company]. Closing on or before [date]."

The rewrite removes the false ownership claim, the unsupported condition claim, and the invented competition. Details are hypothetical.
:::

:::checklist Marketing review before publishing
- The notice accurately states my interest and intent to assign or resell.
- State-required disclosures appear in the notice or linked page.
- Known defects and uninspected systems are disclosed.
- ARV and repairs are labelled as estimates with supporting data.
- No claims of competing offers or urgency are made unless true and documented.
- The seller approved showing arrangements and nothing private is published.
:::

:::summary
Wholesale marketing offers a contract right, not ownership, and should say so. Example jurisdictions require disclosures in advertising or treat such marketing as licensed activity. Honest notices state the interest, disclose condition, support estimates, and avoid false urgency.

# Chapter 9: Closing: Title, Escrow, and Timelines

:::objectives
- Describe the role of title and escrow professionals in a wholesale closing.
- Build a milestone timeline from contract to closing.
- Identify common title problems and how they delay or kill deals.
- Decide when to extend, renegotiate, or walk away.
:::

## The closing team

Depending on the state, closings are handled by title companies, escrow companies, or closing attorneys. Their job is to search title, clear defects, hold funds, prepare settlement statements, and record documents. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's homebuying guidance explains the general closing process for a mortgage-financed purchase, including reviewing the Closing Disclosure and preparing for settlement [12]. Wholesalers should bring the closing team in at contract signing, not at the end.

## Common title issues

| Issue | Why it matters | Typical resolution path |
|---|---|---|
| Unpaid mortgage or liens | Must be paid at or before closing | Payoff statements; may require lender approval if short |
| Judgments against the owner | Attach to property in many states | Payoff or negotiation |
| Probate | Heirs may need court authority to sell | Probate attorney; letters of administration |
| Missing signatures (co-owners, spouses) | All owners must sign | Locate and obtain signatures |
| Code violations and municipal liens | May transfer or block closing | Municipal payoff or compliance |
| Unrecorded or defective prior deeds | Clouds title | Corrective deed or quiet title action |

## Milestone timeline

| Day (from contract) | Milestone | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Contract signed; disclosures delivered | Wholesaler, seller |
| 1–3 | Earnest money deposited; title ordered | Wholesaler, title |
| 1–10 | Inspection and buyer showings | Wholesaler, buyers |
| 7–14 | Title commitment received; issues identified | Title |
| 10–14 | Assignment signed; buyer deposit received | Wholesaler, buyer, title |
| 14–21 | Inspection contingency deadline | Wholesaler |
| 21–30 | Title cleared; settlement statement prepared | Title |
| 30 | Closing and recording | All parties |

Real timelines vary with title complexity, financing, and state practice. Probate and lien issues can add weeks or months.

## Extend, renegotiate, or walk away

When a deadline approaches and the deal is not ready, choose deliberately:

- **Extend** when the delay is caused by a solvable title or administrative issue and the seller agrees in writing.
- **Renegotiate** when new facts, such as a failed inspection, materially change value; present the evidence honestly.
- **Walk away** when the deal cannot work at a price fair to the seller and viable for the buyer. Terminate within your contractual rights and explain clearly.

:::example Illustrative: a probate delay
A hypothetical wholesaler contracts with an heir who believes she can sell her late father's house. The title commitment shows the father is still the owner of record and no estate has been opened. The title company says a court-appointed personal representative must sign. The wholesaler (a) informs the heir in writing, (b) refers her to a probate attorney, (c) proposes a written extension of 90 days, and (d) tells the end buyer, who agrees to wait in exchange for priority. The wholesaler does not pressure the heir to sign documents she lacks authority to sign. Details are hypothetical.
:::

## Structure

Many wholesalers operate through a limited liability company for liability management and separation of finances. Forming an entity does not change licensing obligations; Illinois, for example, applies its pattern-of-business test to an individual or entity "by itself or with any combination of other individuals or entities" [3]. Choose a structure with an attorney and a tax professional.

## Bookkeeping

Use a separate business bank account and accounting software. Track at least:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Assignment fees, double-close spreads |
| Marketing | Mail, ads, website, list costs, DNC Registry fees |
| Deal costs | Earnest money forfeited, inspection fees, title charges |
| Operations | Software, phone, mileage, contractor payments |
| Professional fees | Attorney, accountant |
| Deposits in escrow | Track as assets until released or forfeited |

## Taxes in general terms

The IRS explains that self-employed individuals generally pay self-employment tax, consisting of Social Security and Medicare taxes, on net earnings from self-employment, in addition to income tax [13]. How wholesale income is characterized, and whether you are treated as a dealer or investor, can affect your tax treatment. Work with a qualified tax professional, set aside funds for estimated taxes, and keep documentation for every deal.

## Records to keep

- Regulatory log and licenses or registrations
- Disclosures delivered, with dates and signatures
- Contracts, assignments, and amendments
- Settlement statements
- Consent records and do-not-contact lists
- Marketing materials as published
- Buyer verification records

# Chapter 11: Running Wholesaling as a System

:::objectives
- Apply Dan Martell's Buyback Rate, Camcorder Method, and 1-3-1 Rule to a small real estate operation.
- Write standard operating procedures for repeatable tasks.
- Define weekly key performance indicators.
- Decide what to delegate and what to keep.
:::

## From hustle to system

Small wholesale operations often depend on one person who answers calls, analyzes deals, negotiates, markets, and coordinates closings. That dependence limits capacity and increases compliance risk, because nothing is written down. Systematizing means turning recurring work into documented procedures that others can perform consistently.

:::case Dan Martell's delegation frameworks
Software entrepreneur and coach Dan Martell describes several frameworks in his 2023 book Buy Back Your Time and related interviews. His Buyback Rate starts from the economic value of a founder's hour so that the founder can decide what work to hand off [14]. His Camcorder Method involves recording yourself doing a task while you do it, so that "it takes me no extra time," and then giving three to five recordings to a new hire as onboarding material [14]. His 1-3-1 Rule asks a team member who raises a problem to define one specific problem, present three options considered, and recommend one [14]. In a Genius Network conversation, Martell described a "Buyback Loop" of auditing time, transferring low-value tasks using recordings, and filling the freed time with higher-value work [15]. These are Martell's frameworks and experiences; any results are his own, not TTI's.
:::

## Applying the frameworks

**Buyback Rate.** One version of Martell's formula, as summarized in his workbook and secondary summaries, divides annual income by 2,000 hours and then by four. For a wholesaler who wants to earn $80,000 a year, that suggests about $10 per hour as a threshold: tasks you could pay someone less than that to do, such as data entry or mail list preparation, are candidates for delegation. Treat the formula as a thinking tool.

**Camcorder Method.** Record your screen and voice while you run comparables, scrub a list against your do-not-contact list, or prepare a deal notice. Ask your assistant to write the procedure from the recording; reviewing their draft confirms they understood it.

**1-3-1 Rule.** When an assistant raises a title delay or a buyer question, ask for the problem, three options, and a recommendation. This builds judgment and reduces bottlenecks.

## What to keep and what to delegate

| Task | Keep, delegate, or automate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Regulatory review and attorney liaison | Keep | Accountability cannot be delegated |
| Seller conversations | Keep initially | Delegate only to trained, licensed (where required) staff |
| Comparable sales research | Delegate with SOP | Owner reviews final ARV |
| List suppression and DNC scrubbing | Delegate or automate | Audit monthly |
| Deal notice drafting | Delegate with checklist | Owner approves before publishing |
| Title coordination | Delegate | Owner monitors milestones |
| Bookkeeping | Delegate to bookkeeper | Owner reviews monthly |

# Chapter 12: The 90-Day Pilot and Go/No-Go Decision

:::objectives
- Design a time-boxed pilot that tests wholesaling responsibly.
- Set decision criteria before starting.
- Evaluate results against financial, compliance, and ethical standards.
- Decide whether to continue, modify, or stop.
:::

## Why a pilot

Treat wholesaling as a hypothesis, not an identity. A 90-day pilot limits financial exposure, forces documentation, and produces evidence for a rational decision. The key discipline is setting criteria in advance, so that sunk costs and optimism do not drive the decision.

## Pilot design

| Phase | Weeks | Activities |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | 1–3 | Regulatory inquiry, attorney review, entity and bank account, contracts and disclosures, buyer intake form |
| Buyer development | 2–6 | Identify and verify 20–40 buyers |
| Seller outreach | 4–12 | Direct mail and inquiry channels; disclosure-first conversations |
| Transactions | 6–12 | Analyze, offer, contract, assign or terminate |
| Review | 13 | Evaluate against criteria; decide |

## Decision criteria (set before starting)

- **Compliance:** Zero unresolved regulatory issues; all disclosures documented; zero substantiated complaints.
- **Ethics:** Every contract passed the fairness test; cancellations handled promptly.
- **Financial:** Net result within a pre-set budget; for example, total spend capped at an amount you can afford to lose.
- **Operational:** SOPs written for at least five recurring tasks.
- **Learning:** Clear data on cost per inquiry and contract by channel.

:::example Illustrative: pilot scorecard
| Criterion | Target | Result | Pass? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regulatory issues unresolved | 0 | 0 | Yes |
| Disclosures documented | 100% | 100% | Yes |
| Verified buyers | 25 | 31 | Yes |
| Contracts signed | 3 | 2 | No |
| Net spend within cap ($8,000) | ≤ $8,000 | $6,900 | Yes |
| SOPs written | 5 | 6 | Yes |

The hypothetical operator decides to continue for another 90 days with a modified mail list, because compliance and process criteria were met and the contract shortfall appears to be a targeting issue. Had a compliance criterion failed, the decision would have been to pause. All figures are illustrative.
:::

## Making the decision

- **Continue** if compliance and ethics criteria pass and financial results are within limits.
- **Modify** if compliance passes but financial or operational results fall short for identifiable reasons.
- **Stop** if any compliance or ethics criterion fails without a clear fix, or if losses exceed the cap.

Stopping is a legitimate, successful outcome of a well-run pilot. It means you learned at a known, limited cost.

:::checklist Pilot launch
- Decision criteria are written and dated before outreach begins.
- Budget cap is set.
- Regulatory log, contracts, and disclosures are complete.
- Buyer intake and verification process is ready.
- KPIs and weekly review meeting are scheduled.
:::

:::summary
A 90-day pilot tests wholesaling with limited exposure and predetermined criteria covering compliance, ethics, finances, operations, and learning. The decision to continue, modify, or stop should follow the evidence, and stopping can be a sound result.
:::

# Appendix C: Seller Conversation Guide

This guide follows the disclosure-first structure from Chapter 3. Adapt it with your attorney to include any state-required statements.

**Opening and disclosure.** "Hello, this is [name] with [company]. You reached out about your property at [address]. Before we talk, I want to be clear about who we are. We are investors who buy properties for our own account. Sometimes we buy and close ourselves, and sometimes we sign a contract and assign it to another investor for a fee. That fee would appear on the closing statement. Is it all right to continue?"

**Understanding the situation.** "What has you thinking about selling? Is there a timeline that matters to you? Has anything been tried already, such as listing with an agent? Are there other owners or family members who should be part of this conversation?"

**Options.** "There are a few paths people in your situation consider. Listing with an agent can often reach a higher price if repairs are made or the market is strong, though it may take longer. If you are behind on payments, a HUD-approved housing counselor or attorney can explain options. Selling to an investor like us is usually faster and as-is, but the price reflects the buyer's repair, holding, and resale costs."

**Explaining the offer.** "Here is how we arrived at our number. Similar renovated homes nearby have sold for about [range]. We estimate repairs at about [range]. A buyer also pays for financing, taxes, insurance, and selling costs. After those, the price we can offer is [amount]. You are welcome to compare this with what a listing agent suggests."

**Process and exits.** "If you choose to go ahead, we would sign a written contract, place [amount] in earnest money with [title company], and close on or about [date]. We would have [number] days to inspect and find an investor. If we cannot proceed, here is what happens to the deposit: [explain]. [State any statutory cancellation right.]"

**Invitation to review.** "I will send you the contract and disclosures today. Please review them with anyone you trust, including a lawyer. There is no need to decide today."

**Closing the call.** "Thank you for your time. If you would prefer we not contact you again, just tell me, and we will remove your information."

# Glossary

**After-repair value (ARV).** An estimate of a property's market value after planned renovations, based on comparable sales.

**Assignment.** Transfer of a buyer's contractual rights under a purchase agreement to another party.

**Assignment fee.** Compensation paid to the original buyer (wholesaler) for assigning the contract.

**Comparable sale (comp).** A recent sale of a similar property used to estimate value.

**Contingency.** A contract condition that must be satisfied, or may be relied on to terminate, such as inspection or title.

**Double closing.** Two back-to-back transactions in which the wholesaler buys and immediately resells a property.

**Earnest money.** A good-faith deposit held in escrow pending closing.

**Entity-specific do-not-call list.** A company's own list of people who asked not to be called by that company.

**Equitable interest.** The contractual right of a buyer to complete a purchase; not legal ownership.

**Maximum allowable offer (MAO).** The highest price to offer a seller after subtracting all buyer costs, profit, and fees from ARV.

**National Do Not Call Registry.** The FTC-administered list of telephone numbers whose owners have opted out of most telemarketing calls.

**Pattern of business.** In Illinois law, engaging in specified real estate contract activities on two or more occasions in any 12-month period.

**Title commitment.** A title company's report of conditions that must be met to issue title insurance.

**Wholesale transaction.** As defined in Pennsylvania law, promoting the sale or purchase of an equitable interest in residential property with intent to transfer it for a fee without taking title.
