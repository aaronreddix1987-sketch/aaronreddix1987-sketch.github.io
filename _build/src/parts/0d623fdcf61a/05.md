# Chapter 9: Testing and Launching Safely

:::objectives
- Build a test set from real, anonymized cases, including edge cases and adversarial inputs.
- Define acceptance criteria before testing.
- Launch through stages: sandbox, shadow mode, supervised mode, and limited autonomy.
- Implement a kill switch and an andon-style alert for every automation.
:::

## Test before you trust

The jagged-frontier study showed that people using AI performed worse on a task outside the model's capability, largely because they trusted confident output. Testing is how a business discovers where its own automations sit relative to that frontier before customers do. It is also how you discover mapping errors, missing exceptions, and broken connections.

## Building a test set

A test set is a collection of example inputs with the correct expected output for each. For a small-business automation, 30 to 50 cases is usually enough to start. Build it from real history, anonymized where it contains personal data.

| Case type | Share of test set | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Typical cases | About half | Ordinary enquiries, standard bookings |
| Edge cases | About a quarter | Missing fields, two requests in one email, unusual names, foreign phone numbers, long messages |
| Exceptions | About a fifth | Complaints, emergencies, out-of-scope requests, upset customers |
| Adversarial cases | A few | "Ignore your instructions," requests for discounts, abusive language |

Write the expected output for each case before running the automation, so the test is not influenced by what the system happens to produce.

## Acceptance criteria

Decide in advance what results are good enough to proceed. Criteria should reflect the cost of errors.

:::callout Example acceptance criteria for an email classifier
**Routine categories:** at least 95 percent correct on typical cases.

**Exceptions:** 100 percent of complaints and emergencies routed to a person (zero misses allowed).

**Adversarial cases:** no output grants anything or departs from policy.

**Fallback:** every case the system cannot handle produces the fallback phrase rather than a guess.
:::

Note the asymmetry: a few misfiled routine emails are tolerable, but a missed emergency is not. Set the strictest criteria on the errors that cost the most.

## Staged launch

Move through four stages, advancing only when the acceptance criteria are met at each stage.

1. **Sandbox.** Run the automation on the test set with no connection to live customers or records. Fix failures and rerun until criteria are met.
2. **Shadow mode.** Run the automation on live inputs, but its outputs go only to a reviewer, not to customers or records. A person continues to do the work manually. Compare the two for one to two weeks.
3. **Supervised mode.** The automation does the work, but a person approves each output before it takes effect (for example, one-click approval of drafted replies). Track how often the reviewer edits or rejects outputs.
4. **Limited autonomy.** For categories where supervised mode showed consistently correct results, the automation acts without approval, while a person reviews a sample regularly and all exceptions still go to people.

Some automations should never reach stage 4. Anything that sends money, makes commitments, or communicates about sensitive matters may stay in supervised mode permanently, which is a sound design choice rather than a failure.

## Kill switches and andon alerts

Every automation needs two safety features, borrowed directly from Toyota's jidoka.

- **A kill switch:** a documented, fast way to stop the automation, such as a toggle in the integration platform, known to at least two people. DPD's ability to disable its chatbot's AI element immediately limited the damage from its 2024 incident.
- **An andon alert:** when the automation stops itself, fails, or produces a fallback, it alerts a named person in a channel they watch. Alerts must be rare enough that people do not ignore them; if an alert fires many times a day, fix the cause or adjust the threshold.

## Retest after every change

Rerun the full test set whenever you change the instruction, the data mapping, the connected apps, or the AI model, and periodically even without changes, since vendors update models. DPD attributed its incident to an error after a system update; a regression test after updates is the standard defense.

:::example Illustrative: launching an appointment-reminder automation
A fictional hair salon builds automated text reminders.

**Sandbox:** 40 test appointments, including a client with no mobile number, a booking made less than 24 hours ahead, and a cancelled appointment. Two failures: the cancelled appointment still got a reminder, and the short-notice booking got a "see you tomorrow" message at the wrong time. Both fixed.

**Shadow mode (one week):** reminders went to the manager's phone instead of clients. All correct.

**Supervised mode (one week):** the manager approved each day's batch with one tap. No edits needed.

**Limited autonomy:** reminders now send automatically. Each Monday the manager reviews the previous week's sent log. Kill switch: a toggle in the booking app, known to the manager and owner. Andon: failed sends post to the staff chat.
:::

- Set acceptance criteria in advance, strictest for the costliest errors.

# Chapter 10: Measuring Value Honestly

:::objectives
- Establish a baseline before automating.
- Calculate net time saved, including review and maintenance time.
- Compute monthly net benefit and payback period.
- Track quality and customer measures alongside time.
:::

## Why honest measurement matters

Automation projects are easy to declare successful and hard to evaluate. The setup work is visible and the savings feel obvious, while the hidden costs, such as reviewing drafts, fixing errors, maintaining connections, and paying subscriptions, accumulate quietly. Honest measurement protects the business from keeping automations that cost more than they save, and it builds the evidence needed to invest more in automations that work.

## Baseline first

Before switching anything on, measure the task as it is done now, over at least two weeks:

- **Volume:** how many times per week.
- **Time per instance:** measured with a timer on a sample of at least 10 instances, not estimated.
- **Error rate:** how often the task is done wrong or needs redoing.
- **Delay:** how long from trigger to completion (for example, hours until a lead gets a reply).
- **Customer measure,** where relevant: complaints, satisfaction, conversion.

Without a baseline, any later claim of time saved is a guess.

## Net time saved

Gross time saved is what the automation takes off people's plates. Net time saved subtracts the new work it creates.

:::callout Net time formula
**Net hours saved per month = (Baseline minutes per instance x Instances per month / 60) minus (Review hours + Exception-handling hours + Maintenance hours per month)**

Measure each term. Review time covers approving drafts and sampling output; exception handling covers cases routed to people; maintenance covers fixing failures, updating instructions, and retesting.
:::

## Money and payback

Convert net hours to money using the loaded hourly cost of the person whose time is saved (wage plus payroll taxes and benefits), or for the owner, the Buyback Rate or a realistic value of the owner's time. Then subtract running costs.

:::callout Net benefit and payback
**Monthly net benefit = (Net hours saved x Hourly value) minus Monthly tool and platform costs**

**Payback period in months = One-time setup cost divided by Monthly net benefit**

Setup cost includes the hours spent mapping, building, and testing, valued at the same hourly rate, plus any consultant fees.
:::

A useful rule of thumb for small businesses is to prefer automations that pay back within six months, because tools, prices, and processes change quickly enough that longer paybacks are uncertain.

:::example Illustrative: a bookkeeping practice's invoice-reminder automation
A fictional bookkeeping practice automates overdue-invoice reminders.

**Baseline:** 60 reminders a month at 6 minutes each = 6 hours a month. Error rate: about 1 in 20 reminders sent to a client who had already paid.

**After automation (measured over two months):**

- Review of the weekly reminder batch: 1 hour a month.
- Exceptions (disputes, payment plans) handled by staff: 1.5 hours a month.
- Maintenance: 0.5 hours a month.
- Net hours saved: 6 minus 3 = 3 hours a month.

**Money:** hourly value 40 dollars, so time value = 120 dollars a month. Tool cost: 30 dollars a month. Monthly net benefit = 90 dollars.

**Setup:** 12 hours at 40 dollars = 480 dollars. Payback = 480 / 90 = about 5.3 months.

**Quality:** reminders to clients who had already paid fell to zero, because the automation checks the accounting system's payment status first. Average days to payment, a secondary measure, also improved slightly, though the practice notes that other factors may have contributed.

The automation qualifies under the six-month rule, but not by much. Its main value turned out to be fewer embarrassing errors rather than time.
:::

## Quality and customer measures

Time is not the only value. Track at least one quality measure (error rate, rework, edits needed in review) and, for customer-facing work, one customer measure (response time, complaints, satisfaction). An automation that saves time while increasing errors or complaints is not a success. The Brynjolfsson study's finding that the AI assistant improved customer sentiment as well as speed is the kind of evidence worth seeking in your own measurements, and its finding of small quality declines among top performers is the kind of warning worth watching for.

## Avoiding self-deception

- **Measure, do not estimate.** Use timers and system logs, not impressions.
- **Count all the costs.** Include review, exceptions, maintenance, subscriptions, and setup.
- **Compare like with like.** Seasonal changes in volume can masquerade as automation effects.
- **Watch for shifted work.** If the automation saves the office manager an hour but creates an hour of cleanup for a technician, net savings are zero.
- **Review quarterly.** Retire automations that no longer pay their way.
