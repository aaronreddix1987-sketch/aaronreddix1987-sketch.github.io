# How to Use This Book

This book is a working text for owners of small businesses, solo operators, and client-service agencies who want to use AI agents to run routine parts of their company without losing control of quality, money, or reputation. It is written the way a good university course is taught: each chapter states what you should be able to do when you finish it, explains the underlying ideas in plain language, works through an example with numbers, and closes with a summary and review questions. The appendices contain the templates you will actually fill in.

The central argument is simple. AI agents fail in business when they are asked to "run the company." They succeed when each agent owns one narrow, written job, reads from a shared set of rules, writes a record of what it did, and stops at clearly marked lines that only a human may cross. That idea is not new. It is the same discipline good managers have always applied to people: clear roles, written procedures, an audit trail, and limits on authority. What is new is that the "employee" can now be a piece of software that works at any hour for a small marginal cost, and that makes the discipline more important, not less.

## Who this book is for

- Owner-operators of local service businesses (trades, cleaning, real estate, health and wellness, professional services) who answer their own phones and emails.
- Small agencies and consultancies that deliver the same kind of work to many clients.
- Operators inside larger firms who have been asked to "find out what AI can do for us" and need a structured method.

You do not need to be a programmer. Where a technical step is required, the book describes what must be true, not which button to press, because tools change every few months while the principles do not.

# Chapter 1: What an "AI Company" Is and Is Not

:::objectives
- Define an AI agent, a workflow, and an agentic system in operational terms.
- Distinguish tasks that are suitable for agents from tasks that are not.
- Explain why narrow, single-job design outperforms general-purpose "do everything" assistants.
- Describe the four layers of the AI Company Map used throughout this book.
:::

## The vocabulary problem

Few terms in business are used as loosely as "AI agent." Vendors use it for anything from a chatbot widget to a system that books meetings on its own. For the purposes of running a company, we need definitions that tell us who is responsible for what.

The most useful public distinction comes from Anthropic's engineering guidance, *Building Effective Agents* (2024). It separates **workflows**, "systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths," from **agents**, "systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage." Both are grouped under the umbrella term **agentic systems**. The same guidance recommends "finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed," noting that this "might mean not building agentic systems at all."

For a small business this distinction is practical, not academic. A workflow is predictable: the same input produces the same sequence of steps, so you can test it and explain it to a customer. An agent is flexible: it decides its own steps, which makes it useful for messy inputs but harder to supervise. In this book we use the word **agent** in the everyday business sense, meaning a scheduled or triggered piece of AI-assisted software that performs one defined job, but we design every agent to behave as much like a workflow as its job allows.

:::callout Working definition
An **AI agent**, in this book, is a piece of software that (1) is triggered by a schedule or an event, (2) reads trusted inputs, (3) uses a language model to make bounded judgments, (4) takes a small set of permitted actions in your existing tools, and (5) writes a record of what it did. If you cannot write down all five, you do not yet have an agent; you have an experiment.
:::

## What AI agents are good at

Language models are strong at reading unstructured text, classifying it, summarizing it, and drafting a reply in a consistent voice. Combined with ordinary software, they can move information between systems you already use. In a small business this maps to a recognizable list of jobs:

- Reading inbound messages (emails, form submissions, call transcripts) and sorting them by intent.
- Drafting first replies from an approved set of facts.
- Following up on things that have gone quiet: unpaid invoices, unanswered quotes, abandoned checkouts.
- Producing a daily or weekly summary from logs and records.
- Checking that something that should have happened did happen (a payment captured, a file delivered, a link still working).

## What AI agents are bad at

The same systems are weak, or simply inappropriate, where a mistake is expensive and hard to reverse, where the law requires a human decision, or where the facts are not written down anywhere the agent can read. These include moving money, signing contracts, giving legal, tax or medical advice, making promises about price or outcome that are not in a written policy, and anything that depends on judgment about a specific person's circumstances.

The legal system has already shown that a business remains responsible for what its software says. In *Moffatt v. Air Canada* (2024 BCCRT 149), a British Columbia tribunal held Air Canada liable after its website chatbot gave a customer incorrect information about bereavement fares. The tribunal rejected the airline's argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own actions, writing that "it should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website. It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot." The award was small, about C$812, but the principle is the one every owner should adopt: **an agent speaks for your company.**

| Suitable for an agent | Keep with a human |
|---|---|
| Sorting and tagging inbound messages | Approving refunds, discounts, or credits |
| Drafting replies from approved facts | Signing contracts or agreeing new terms |
| Polite reminders on overdue items | Changing bank, payout, or tax details |
| Daily summaries of logs and counts | Hiring, firing, or disciplining anyone |
| Checking that links, payments, files exist | Legal, tax, medical, or safety advice |
| Formatting content for approved channels | Public statements in a crisis |

## The four layers of the AI Company Map

Throughout this book we use a single picture of the company, read from the bottom up.

1. **Systems of record.** The tools you already use: inbox, phone, calendar, customer list or CRM, payment processor, accounting software, website. These remain the source of truth. Agents read from them and take narrow actions in them; they never replace them.
2. **The shared brain.** A small set of plain records that every agent reads before it acts and writes to after it acts: master rules, weekly orders, an append-only log, and a human-only queue. Chapter 3 is devoted to it.
3. **Departments of single-job agents.** Seven departments (Sales, Deals, Marketing, Customer, Onboarding, Office, Diligence), each containing a few agents with one job apiece. Chapters 5 to 7 describe them.
4. **The AI CEO desk and the human owner.** A coordinating agent that sets weekly orders and writes one morning page, and above it the owner, who reads that page and clears the items only a human may clear. Chapter 8 covers this layer.

:::example Illustrative: the same business, two designs
Consider a fictional three-person plumbing company that receives about 40 inbound calls and 25 web inquiries a week. In Design A, the owner buys one "AI assistant" and gives it access to the inbox, calendar, and payment account with the instruction "handle customers." In Design B, the owner sets up three agents: one that answers calls and logs caller name, number, and need; one that replies to web inquiries with the approved service menu and a booking link; and one that sends a polite reminder on invoices more than seven days overdue. None of the three can issue refunds or change prices.

When something goes wrong in Design A, say a customer is quoted a price that does not exist, the owner cannot tell which instruction caused it, and the assistant had the access to act on it. In Design B, the error can only occur in the inquiry agent, its log line shows exactly what it sent, and its permissions make a price change impossible. Design B is less impressive in a demonstration and far easier to run.
:::

:::summary
- Use precise terms: workflows follow fixed paths; agents choose their own steps. Prefer the simplest design that does the job.
- Agents are strong at reading, sorting, drafting, following up, and checking. They are weak where errors are costly, irreversible, or legally reserved for humans.
- Your business is responsible for what its agents say, as *Moffatt v. Air Canada* illustrates.
- The AI Company Map has four layers: systems of record, shared brain, single-job departments, and the AI CEO desk with the human owner above it.
:::

# Chapter 2: The Single-Job Principle

:::objectives
- State the single-job principle and the reasons it works.
- Apply Dan Martell's Buyback Rate and DRIP Matrix to decide which jobs to hand to agents first.
- Write a one-sentence job definition that passes the "one verb, one output" test.
- Identify and split overloaded agents.
:::

## The principle

**One agent, one written job.** Each agent has a single responsibility that can be described in one sentence with one main verb and one observable output. "Send a polite reminder for invoices more than seven days overdue" is a job. "Manage accounts receivable" is a department. "Handle my business" is a wish.

The principle works for three reasons. First, **testability**: a narrow job has a small set of correct outputs, so you can check it in seconds. Second, **containment**: an agent with one job needs only the permissions for that job, so a mistake or a manipulated input can do limited damage. Third, **legibility**: when every agent writes one log line per run, the owner can understand the whole company from a page of text. Software engineers call this idea the single-responsibility principle; operations managers call it a clear job description. The point is the same.

## Deciding what to hand off first

Before designing any agent, decide which work should leave your desk. A widely used framework for this comes from Dan Martell, a Canadian entrepreneur and founder of SaaS Academy, in his book *Buy Back Your Time* (2023). Martell's core argument is that founders should hire, and by extension automate, to buy back their own time rather than simply to grow headcount.

:::case Dan Martell's Buyback Rate and DRIP Matrix
In *Buy Back Your Time*, Martell proposes two tools that translate well to AI delegation.

**The Buyback Rate.** Take what your business pays you per year, divide by 2,000 working hours to get an effective hourly rate, and divide that by four. Martell's guidance is that tasks you can hand off at or below this rate should be handed off. His published example: someone earning $200,000 a year has an effective rate of $100 an hour and a Buyback Rate of $25 an hour.

**The DRIP Matrix.** Every task is placed on two axes, money produced and energy given or drained, creating four quadrants: **Delegation** (low money, drains energy), **Replacement** (high money, drains energy), **Investment** (low money, gives energy), and **Production** (high money, gives energy). Martell recommends clearing Delegation first, then Replacement, protecting Investment, and spending as much time as possible in Production.

Martell also recommends a time audit: log your work in 15-minute blocks for two weeks, then mark what drains you. These are his frameworks and his reported practices; the application to AI agents below is ours.

Sources: Martell, *Buy Back Your Time* (Portfolio, 2023); danmartell.com (see References).
:::

The DRIP Matrix gives a direct ordering for AI delegation. Delegation-quadrant work, low value and draining, such as chasing invoices, sorting email, and formatting posts, is usually also the most rules-based and therefore the most suitable for a single-job agent. Replacement-quadrant work, such as sales calls and onboarding, often contains steps an agent can take (preparing a brief, sending a scope document), but the core of it usually needs a person. Production work is yours to keep.

:::example Illustrative: computing a Buyback Rate and a first agent
A fictional bookkeeping firm owner pays herself $90,000 a year.

- Effective hourly rate: $90,000 / 2,000 = $45.00
- Buyback Rate: $45.00 / 4 = $11.25

Her two-week audit shows 6 hours a week spent sending invoice reminders and answering "did you get my documents?" emails, both marked as draining. An agent subscription and setup that costs her the equivalent of $150 a month replaces about 26 hours a month of this work, or roughly $5.77 an hour, which is below her Buyback Rate of $11.25. By Martell's test, this work should leave her desk. She also notes that the reminder agent must never waive late fees, because that would be a pricing decision.
:::

## Writing a job definition

A good job definition has five parts, which map to the working definition from Chapter 1.

| Part | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | When does it run? | Weekdays at 9:00 a.m. |
| Inputs | What may it read, and what is trusted? | Invoice list from accounting software; master rules |
| Judgment | What is it allowed to decide? | Which invoices are more than 7 days overdue and not yet reminded twice |
| Actions | What may it do? | Send one reminder email per invoice from the approved template |
| Record | What does it write afterward? | One log line: date, count sent, count skipped, errors |

Then test the job sentence against two rules. **One verb, one output:** if the sentence needs "and" to join two different actions ("reply to inquiries and update the CRM and book calls"), split it. **Observable success:** someone who has never seen the agent should be able to look at its output for 60 seconds and say whether it did its job.

:::checklist Is this agent overloaded?
- Its job sentence contains "and" joining two different actions.
- It needs write access to more than one system of record.
- It handles both inbound and outbound messages.
- It can affect money (charges, refunds, payouts) and also talk to customers.
- You cannot describe a correct output without saying "it depends."
- Its log line would need more than one sentence.
:::

If you tick two or more boxes, split the agent. A common split is between an agent that **reads and classifies** and an agent that **acts on one class**. For example, an inbox triage agent labels messages as "new inquiry," "existing customer," "invoice question," or "other," and three separate agents act on the first three labels, while "other" goes to the human queue.

## Why not one big assistant?

General assistants are excellent for an owner working interactively, drafting a proposal, or thinking through a problem. They are poor as unattended operators, because a single prompt that tries to cover sales, support, and billing must carry every rule for all three, so every additional instruction dilutes the others, and its permissions must cover the union of all three jobs. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025) lists "excessive agency" among the principal risks of language-model systems, alongside prompt injection; narrow jobs with narrow permissions are the most direct defense against both (Chapter 9).

:::summary
- One agent, one written job: it improves testability, containment, and legibility.
- Use Martell's Buyback Rate and DRIP Matrix to choose what to delegate: clear draining, low-value work first.
- A job definition has a trigger, inputs, a bounded judgment, permitted actions, and a record.
