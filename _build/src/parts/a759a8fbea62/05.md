
# Chapter 11: Retention, Scope, and Growth

:::objectives
- Prevent and manage scope creep with a written change process.
- Calculate client lifetime value and explain why retention drives agency economics.
- Decide when and whom to hire, using Martell's Replacement Ladder and capacity math.
- Recognize when to raise prices, add a service, or stop serving a client.
:::

## Scope creep

Scope creep is the gradual expansion of work beyond what was agreed, without a matching change in price. It rarely arrives as a big request. It arrives as "while you're in there, could you also…" repeated a dozen times. Each favor seems small; together they turn a profitable client into an unprofitable one and teach the client that the scope is negotiable.

Three habits prevent most scope creep.

1. **Write exclusions as clearly as inclusions** in the proposal and agreement (Chapter 3).
2. **Use a change request process.** When a request falls outside scope, respond warmly and in writing: "Happy to help with that. It's outside our current plan, so here are two options: we can add it as a one-time project for $X, or move you to the Plus tier, which includes it." Never simply say no, and never simply say yes.
3. **Track hours per client.** If a client's delivery time rises month after month, the numbers will show it before your resentment does.

Jonathan Stark suggests using the agreed outcome as a filter: requests that contribute to the goal can be discussed; requests that do not are parked until after the goal is achieved. That filter keeps conversations about value rather than about favors.

## Why retention matters most

Winning a client costs time and money: outreach, calls, proposals, onboarding. Keeping one costs far less. The economics show up in **client lifetime value** (LTV): the gross profit a client produces over the whole relationship.

:::callout Lifetime value
**LTV = monthly gross profit per client × average months retained**
:::

:::example Illustrative: the value of retention
An agency's average client pays $450 a month, with delivery costs of $90, for monthly gross profit of $360.

- If clients stay an average of 6 months: LTV = $360 × 6 = $2,160.
- If clients stay an average of 18 months: LTV = $360 × 18 = $6,480.

Tripling retention triples lifetime value with no change in price or delivery. It also means the agency needs far fewer new clients each year to stay the same size. With 10 clients and 6-month retention, it must replace about 20 clients a year; with 18-month retention, about 7.
:::

Retention comes from the practices in earlier chapters: a clear offer, a strong onboarding with a first win, reliable delivery, honest reporting, and quarterly reviews that connect work to goals. It also comes from small habits: responding to messages within one business day, remembering what matters to the client, and telling them about problems before they discover them.

## Raising prices

Prices should rise as your proof, skill, and systems improve, and as costs rise. Raise prices for new clients first, which carries no risk to existing revenue. For existing clients, give written notice (30 to 60 days is common), explain briefly, and consider holding the current price for long-standing clients for a period. Some clients will leave; if your floor calculation was right, the ones who stay at the new price are more profitable than the ones who left.

## When to hire

Hiring is the main way to grow capacity past the owner's limit, and it is also the main way small agencies get into financial trouble. Use capacity math and Martell's sequencing together.

**Capacity math.** Hire when you are consistently at or above about 85 percent of capacity for two to three months, have a pipeline that will fill the new capacity, and have documented the work the new person will do (Chapter 9). Hiring before documentation means you will spend your reclaimed hours training by improvisation.

**Sequencing.** Martell's Replacement Ladder (administrative assistant, delivery or operations, marketing, sales, leadership) suggests that agency owners usually benefit first from administrative help and then from a delivery person who takes over recurring production. Contractors are a lower-risk first step than employees for many small firms, but the legal distinction between contractor and employee depends on the actual working relationship; the IRS and the US Department of Labor publish guidance, and state rules differ.

:::example Illustrative: the first hire decision
Priya has 12 clients and works 50 hours a week. Delivery takes 34 hours, admin 8, selling 8. Her playbooks cover posting, review replies, and reports.

A contractor producer at $25 an hour for 20 hours a week costs about $2,170 a month. Taking 20 delivery hours off her desk frees time for selling and quarterly reviews. If she uses half the freed time to win and serve three more Standard clients at $450 each, revenue rises by $1,350 a month while her own hours fall to about 40 a week. The hire does not pay for itself immediately in cash; it pays in capacity, quality, and the owner's time, which Martell argues is the right way to frame it. Priya decides to proceed only after confirming three months of cash reserves.
:::

## Adding services

Add a second service only when the first is documented, profitable, and running without daily owner involvement. The best second service is one existing clients already ask for and that uses similar skills. Mailchimp's history, discussed in Chapter 1, is an extreme version of this pattern: its founders built the email tool because their own agency clients needed it.

## Ending client relationships

Some clients should not be retained: those who repeatedly pay late, demand work far outside scope, treat staff disrespectfully, or ask you to do something unethical or unlawful, such as posting fake reviews. End the relationship professionally: give the notice your agreement requires, deliver a final report, hand over all access promptly (within Google's seven-business-day window for Business Profiles), and keep a written record.

:::summary Chapter summary
- Prevent scope creep with written exclusions, a friendly change-request process, and hour tracking.
- Lifetime value equals monthly gross profit times months retained; retention is the strongest economic lever.
- Raise prices for new clients first; give existing clients notice.
- Hire when capacity, pipeline, documentation, and cash reserves are all in place; follow Martell's sequence of admin then delivery.
- Add services clients already request; end bad-fit relationships professionally.
:::

# Chapter 12: Using AI Responsibly in an Agency

:::objectives
- Identify agency tasks where AI tools help and where they create risk.
- Apply Google's published guidance on AI-generated content.
- Set review, disclosure, and data rules for AI use with clients.
- Explain the regulatory risks of AI claims, AI reviews, and AI voice calls.
:::

## Where AI helps, and where it does not

AI writing and image tools can speed up many routine agency tasks: first drafts of posts, variations of ad copy, summaries of reports, outlines for blog articles, image resizing, and transcriptions. Used well, they lower delivery hours per client, the key efficiency lever from Chapter 4.

They also create real risks. Language models can state false facts with confidence. They can produce generic content that sounds like every other business. They can repeat errors at scale. And when they are given client data, they raise confidentiality questions.

| Task | Good use of AI | Human must |
|---|---|---|
| Social posts | Draft options from a brief | Check facts, voice, offers, and prices; approve |
| Review replies | Draft a polite reply | Personalize, check for sensitive issues, follow approval rules |
| Reports | Summarize metrics into a draft headline | Verify every number against the source |
| Blog articles | Outline and first draft | Add real expertise and examples; fact-check |
| Ad copy | Generate variations to test | Check platform policies and claim substantiation |

## Google's guidance on AI content

Google has published guidance on AI-generated content for search. In a February 2023 post, Google Search Central wrote that "appropriate use of AI or automation is not against our guidelines," but that "using automation—including AI—to generate content with the primary purpose of manipulating ranking in search results is a violation of our spam policies." The post adds that "using AI doesn't give content any special gains," and that content succeeds if it is "useful, helpful, original," and demonstrates expertise, experience, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness. Google's later documentation warns that generating many pages "without adding value for users may violate Google's spam policy on scaled content abuse" and says it is "critical to manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content for accuracy and trustworthiness before publishing."

For an agency, the implication is straightforward: use AI to assist people who know the client's business, not to mass-produce pages.

## Agency AI rules

Write a short policy and share it with clients.

1. **Human review.** Every AI-assisted item is reviewed by a person who checks facts, voice, and claims before it goes to the client or the public.
2. **No fabrication.** AI tools must never be used to create fake reviews, testimonials, customer stories, statistics, or endorsements.
3. **Data care.** Do not paste confidential client or customer information into tools whose terms allow that data to be used for training or shared; check each tool's data settings.
4. **Disclosure.** Tell clients, in your agreement, that you use AI tools to assist production and that a person reviews all output. Follow platform rules on labeling AI-generated images and media.
5. **Accountability.** The agency is responsible for what it publishes, regardless of which tool drafted it.

:::case Regulators and AI marketing claims
US regulators have made clear that existing law applies to AI. In September 2024 the FTC announced "Operation AI Comply," a set of law enforcement actions against companies using AI claims or tools to deceive consumers, including a case involving a service that generated fake reviews. The FTC's August 2024 final rule on fake reviews also covers fake reviews regardless of how they are produced. In February 2024 the FCC ruled that AI-generated voices in robocalls are "artificial" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, meaning such calls require the same consent as other prerecorded calls.

For an agency, the lessons are: do not exaggerate what your AI-assisted service can achieve; never generate reviews; and do not use AI voice tools for outbound calls without legal advice. See References.
:::

# Chapter 13: The 90-Day Launch Plan

:::objectives
- Sequence the work of Chapters 1 to 12 into a realistic 90-day plan.
- Set weekly activity targets that you control.
- Review progress at 30, 60, and 90 days and adjust.
:::

## Principles of the plan

The plan below assumes a solo founder with 20 to 40 hours a week. It sets targets for activities you control (messages sent, calls held, playbooks written) rather than outcomes you do not (clients won, revenue). Outcomes follow activity unevenly; activity is what you can manage. Expect some weeks to slip, and adjust rather than abandon the plan.

## Days 1–30: Foundation

| Week | Focus | Deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Niche and offer | Niche matrix scored; positioning statement; core offer written (Chapters 2–3) |
| 2 | Pricing and setup | Price floor calculated; tiers priced; registration, EIN, bank account started (Chapters 4–5) |
| 3 | Proof | Own accounts improved; two labeled demonstration projects; audit template (Chapter 6) |
| 4 | Sales tools | Proposal template; agreement reviewed by attorney; discovery call script (Chapter 8) |

## Days 31–60: Outreach

| Week | Focus | Deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Warm outreach | Personal messages to every warm contact on your list |
| 6 | Content | One platform chosen; three useful posts per week begun |
| 7 | Audits | Five audits sent to well-matched local businesses, CAN-SPAM compliant |
| 8 | Calls and proposals | Every discovery call followed by a proposal within two business days |

## Days 61–90: Delivery and systems

| Week | Focus | Deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | Onboarding | Onboarding checklist and intake form finalized; first clients onboarded with a baseline |
| 10 | Playbooks | First two playbooks recorded and written (Camcorder Method) |
| 11 | Reporting | One-page report template; first reports delivered |
| 12 | Review | Monthly profit model; pipeline review; referral requests to satisfied clients |
| 13 | Plan next quarter | Decide on price, capacity, and next system to build |

## Review points

At 30, 60, and 90 days, answer the same questions in writing.

1. What did I plan, and what did I actually do?
2. Which outreach channel produced the most conversations?
3. What did prospects say that changed my understanding of the niche or the offer?
4. Is my price above my floor, and is revenue per delivery hour rising?
5. What is the single most important change for the next 30 days?

:::warning A realistic expectation
Many new agencies win no clients in their first 30 to 60 days, and some take much longer. A slow start is common and is not proof that the plan or the niche is wrong. Persistent silence after many well-targeted conversations, however, is information: revisit the niche score, the offer, and the price before working harder at the same approach.
:::
