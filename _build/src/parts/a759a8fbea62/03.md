## Registration, tax, and banking

The steps below are common for a small US marketing business. Requirements differ by state, county, and city, and this list is a starting point for your own checks, not legal or tax advice.

1. **Choose a structure.** A sole proprietorship requires the least paperwork. A limited liability company (LLC) separates business and personal liability in many situations and is a common choice for service firms. The US Small Business Administration (SBA) publishes plain-language comparisons of structures. An accountant or attorney in your state can tell you which suits your situation.
2. **Register the name.** If you operate under a name other than your own legal name, most states and counties require an assumed-name or "doing business as" filing.
3. **Get an Employer Identification Number (EIN).** The IRS issues EINs free of charge through its website. Even a solo owner often uses one to open a business bank account and to avoid giving clients a Social Security number on tax forms.
4. **Check local licenses.** Many cities and counties require a general business license even for home-based service firms.
5. **Open a separate bank account.** Never mix business and personal money. Separation makes bookkeeping, tax preparation, and any later financing far simpler.
6. **Plan for tax.** Self-employed people in the US generally pay income tax and self-employment tax, often through quarterly estimated payments. The IRS Self-Employed Individuals Tax Center explains the basics. Set aside a fixed percentage of every payment received in a separate account until you know your actual liability.
7. **Consider insurance.** Professional liability (errors and omissions) insurance covers claims that your work caused a client financial loss. General liability covers injury and property damage. Some clients will require proof of coverage in their contracts.

## The ownership principle

The single most important operating rule for a marketing agency is this: **the client owns their accounts, their data, and their ad spend.** Their Google Business Profile, ad accounts, analytics properties, social media pages, domain name, website hosting, and email lists should be registered to the client, with your agency granted access as a manager or partner. When the relationship ends, the client should be able to remove your access without losing anything.

This principle protects the client, and it protects you. An agency that holds a client's accounts hostage invites disputes, chargebacks, and complaints. An agency that sets things up correctly from the first day signals professionalism and makes it easy for satisfied clients to stay.

Ad spend deserves particular care. Wherever possible, the client's own card should be attached to the ad account so that the platform bills the client directly. If you must pay ad costs and re-bill them, require a deposit in advance and state the arrangement in writing; floating a client's ad spend on your own card can drain your cash quickly if they pay late.

:::case Google's rules for agencies managing Business Profiles
Google publishes specific policies for third parties, such as agencies, that manage Business Profiles on behalf of businesses. Among the requirements stated in Google's Business Profile third-party policies:

- "All end customers must retain ownership or co-ownership of their Business Profile at all times."
- Within seven business days of a customer's notice, the agency must give the customer the ability to disassociate the agency's account and regain exclusive control.
- If you charge a management fee, you must tell customers that Business Profile "is a service provided at no extra cost," in writing before managing the profile and on invoices.
- "To respond to reviews on behalf of the end customer, you must have an explicit approval. Verbal consent isn't sufficient."
- Agencies must not make "false, misleading, or unrealistic claims" about their services or the results customers can expect.

These rules turn the ownership principle into a written obligation for one of the most common small-agency services. Read the current version of the policy before you offer profile management, because platform policies change. See References.
:::

## A minimal tool stack

Tools should serve a documented process, not replace one. A small agency can operate well with a short list.

| Function | What it must do | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Contracts and e-signature | Send agreements and capture signatures | Store signed copies in one folder per client |
| Invoicing and payments | Recurring invoices, card and bank payments, reminders | Automatic billing reduces late payment |
| Project management | One board or list per client, recurring task templates | Chapter 9 explains how to structure it |
| Shared files | One folder per client with standard subfolders | Name files consistently |
| Password and access management | Store credentials securely and share without email | Use the platforms' own manager-access features wherever possible |
| Scheduling and design | Plan and publish posts; create graphics | Choose tools your niche uses |
| Reporting | Pull platform data into a simple monthly report | Chapter 10 covers what to include |

:::warning Security basics
Turn on two-factor authentication on every business and client-access account. Never ask clients to email passwords; use the platform's own partner or manager access, or a password manager's secure sharing. Remove your access promptly when an engagement ends, and write down that you did.
:::

- [ ] Insurance needs reviewed.

- [ ] Tool stack chosen; two-factor authentication on all accounts.

:::

# Chapter 6: Building Proof Before Your First Client

:::objectives
- Explain why proof drives perceived likelihood and how to build it honestly from zero.
- Produce three kinds of starter proof: own-account results, demonstration projects, and audits.
- Write a short, accurate case study using a standard structure.
- Apply the FTC's rules on endorsements, testimonials, and reviews.
:::

## The proof problem

A prospective client's central question is "will this work for me?" In the language of Chapter 3, they are estimating perceived likelihood. Relevant proof, evidence that you have done similar work for similar businesses, is the strongest answer. New agencies face a circular problem: they need clients to get proof and proof to get clients.

The solution is not to invent proof. It is to create genuine, clearly labeled evidence of skill before the first paid engagement, and to collect real evidence systematically once clients arrive.

## Three kinds of starter proof

**1. Your own accounts.** If you sell social media management, your agency's own accounts are a public sample of your judgment and consistency. If you sell local search, your own Business Profile (if eligible) is a sample of your standards. A neglected agency account undermines every claim on your website.

**2. Demonstration projects.** Create a realistic sample for a fictional business in your niche, or a clearly labeled redesign concept for a real public example: a month of sample posts for a fictional auto repair shop, a mock landing page, or a sample monthly report. Label demonstrations as demonstrations. Do not present them as client work.

**3. Audits.** A short, specific audit of a real business's public presence (their Google Business Profile, website basics, and review responses) demonstrates expertise and gives the prospect something useful. Audits double as an outreach tool in Chapter 7. Keep them brief, factual, and respectful.

A fourth option, discounted or free work for a small number of early clients, can produce real proof quickly, but treat it carefully. Set a clear end date and a written scope, agree in advance that you may describe the results (with the client's permission), and avoid discounts so deep that you anchor a long-term client to an unsustainable price.

## Writing an honest case study

Once you have real client work, document it in a standard one-page structure.

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Client | Type of business and location (named only with written permission) |
| Starting point | The situation before, with specific facts |
| What we did | The actions taken, in plain language |
| What changed | Measured changes over a stated period, with the data source |
| Caveats | Other factors that may have contributed; what the numbers do not show |
| Client's words | A quote, used with written permission |

:::example Illustrative: a case study written honestly
Client: A family-owned auto repair shop in a mid-sized town (name used with permission).

Starting point: Profile listed outdated hours, had 14 unanswered reviews, and showed three photos, two of them from 2019.

What we did: Corrected hours and services, added 25 current photos, replied to all reviews with wording approved by the owner, and posted weekly updates for three months.

What changed: Over the three months, the profile's reported phone calls rose from 61 to 88 per month, according to the Business Profile performance report. Direction requests rose from 140 to 171.

Caveats: The shop also began a radio spot in month two, and calls are seasonal; we cannot separate the effects. Calls are not the same as booked jobs.

This format is credible precisely because it is modest. Prospects trust a case study that names its limitations.
:::

## The legal rules on testimonials and reviews

US law treats endorsements and reviews in advertising seriously, and agencies are directly exposed because they often write marketing content and manage review responses for clients.

The Federal Trade Commission's *Guides Concerning the Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising* were revised in 2023. According to the FTC's announcement, the revisions address "procuring, suppressing, boosting, organizing, publishing, upvoting, downvoting, or editing consumer reviews so as to distort what consumers think of a product," incentivized reviews, employee reviews, and fake negative reviews of competitors. The guides state that material connections between an endorser and a seller must be disclosed when "a significant minority of the audience" does not expect the connection, and that online disclosures must be "unavoidable."

In August 2024 the FTC announced a final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials, which, among other things, prohibits fake reviews, including those attributed to people who do not exist, and the buying of positive or negative reviews.

For a small agency, the practical rules are clear:

- Never write, buy, or arrange fake reviews for a client, and never post negative reviews of a client's competitors.
- Never offer customers incentives conditioned on leaving a positive review.
- Disclose paid relationships in influencer and endorsement content clearly, in the content itself, not only on a profile page.
- Use testimonials only with permission and only when they reflect the person's honest experience.
- When a testimonial describes unusual results, do not present it as typical.

:::warning Your own marketing is covered too
The same rules apply to the agency's own website and social posts. Do not publish client logos, results, or quotes without written permission, and do not imply you have clients or results you do not have.
:::

# Chapter 7: Finding Clients

:::objectives
- Describe Alex Hormozi's Core Four lead channels and choose an order suited to a new agency.
- Run a warm-outreach campaign and a compliant cold-outreach campaign.
- Apply CAN-SPAM and telemarketing rules to outreach.
- Build a referral habit and track a simple sales pipeline.
:::

## The four ways to reach people

In *$100M Leads* (2023), Alex Hormozi organizes lead generation into four methods he calls the Core Four, defined by two questions: are you reaching one person at a time or many at once, and do they already know you?

| | One-to-one | One-to-many |
|---|---|---|
| **People who know you (warm)** | Warm outreach | Posting free content |
| **People who don't (cold)** | Cold outreach | Paid ads |

Each channel trades time, money, or both. Warm outreach costs mainly time and courage and usually produces the first clients fastest, because the people already know and trust you to some degree. Content is slow at first and compounds over time. Cold outreach and paid ads reach strangers and therefore depend most heavily on a clear, proven offer.

For a new agency, a sensible order is: warm outreach first, then one content channel, then cold outreach once the offer is proven, and paid ads only when you know what a client is worth and what you can afford to pay to acquire one.

## Warm outreach

Make a list of everyone you know who owns or influences a business in your niche, plus anyone who knows many such people: former colleagues, suppliers, family friends, members of local associations. Contact each person individually, by the channel they actually use, with a short, personal message. The aim is not to pitch but to tell people what you now do and to ask whether they know anyone it might help.

:::example Illustrative: a warm message
"Hi Dave, it's Marcus. Hope the shop's been busy. I've started a small business helping independent repair shops keep their Google profiles accurate and active: hours, photos, review replies, that kind of thing. I'm looking for two or three shops to work with this spring. Do you know anyone who might want help with that, or would a quick look at your own profile be useful? No pressure either way."

The message is personal, specific, honest about scale, and makes a small, easy request.
:::

## Content

Choose one platform where your niche's owners actually spend time, and publish useful material regularly: short explanations of common mistakes, before-and-after examples (with permission), checklists, and answers to questions you hear in sales calls. Content does three jobs. It shows your expertise to people who are not ready to buy, it warms up the people you later contact directly, and it gives prospects a reason to trust you before the first call.

## Cold outreach, done lawfully

Cold outreach means contacting businesses that do not know you. Done well, it is respectful, specific, and useful. Done badly, it is spam, and in some forms it is illegal.

**Email.** The US CAN-SPAM Act applies to commercial email, including business-to-business messages. The FTC's compliance guide lists requirements including: do not use false or misleading header information; do not use deceptive subject lines; identify the message as an advertisement where required; include a valid physical postal address; tell recipients how to opt out; honor opt-out requests promptly (within 10 business days); and monitor what others do on your behalf. Other countries' rules, such as Canada's anti-spam law and European privacy law, can be stricter and may require prior consent.

**Phone and text.** Calls and text messages are governed in the US by the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) and the national Do Not Call rules, which restrict autodialed and prerecorded calls and texts, especially to mobile numbers, without prior consent. In February 2024 the Federal Communications Commission ruled that AI-generated voices in robocalls count as "artificial" under the TCPA. For a small agency, the safe practice is to avoid automated calling and texting of prospects entirely and to make only individual, manually dialed calls to business lines, while checking state rules.

**The audit approach.** A respectful cold email offers something useful: a short audit of the prospect's public profile with two or three specific, fixable observations. Keep it brief, accurate, and free of exaggeration.

:::example Illustrative: a cold email with an audit
Subject: Two quick fixes for your Google profile

"Hi Ana, I help independent repair shops in the Tri-County area with their Google Business Profiles. I looked at yours and noticed two things that are easy to fix: your Saturday hours show as closed, though your website says 8 to 1, and nine recent reviews have no reply. I've put a one-page summary with a few more observations at the link below. If it's useful, I'm happy to talk; if not, no reply needed.

Marcus Lee, Lee Local Marketing, 123 Main St, Springfield. To stop hearing from me, reply 'no thanks.'"

The email is specific, verifiable, short, includes a physical address, and offers a simple opt-out.
:::

## Referrals and partnerships

Satisfied clients and complementary businesses are the most reliable long-term sources of new clients for small agencies. Ask for referrals at moments of visible success, such as after a strong monthly report, and make it easy: "If you know another shop owner who'd find this useful, I'd be grateful for an introduction." Build relationships with businesses that serve the same clients but do not compete with you: web developers, bookkeepers, equipment suppliers, and local trade associations. If you pay referral fees, disclose them where the law or the relationship requires it.

## Tracking the pipeline

Keep a simple pipeline with five stages and review it weekly.

| Stage | Definition | Weekly question |
|---|---|---|
| Contacted | First message sent | How many new people did I contact? |
| Replied | Any response | Which messages got replies? |
| Call booked | Discovery call scheduled | Am I booking enough calls? |
| Proposal sent | Written proposal delivered | Are proposals going out within two days? |
| Won or lost | Decision made | Why did I win or lose? |

The weekly discipline matters more than the tool. Owners who track outreach every week tend to keep selling during busy delivery months, which is what breaks the feast-and-famine cycle described in Chapter 1.

- Warm messages should be personal, specific, and make a small request.
- Cold email must comply with CAN-SPAM; avoid automated calls and texts, which the TCPA restricts, including AI-generated voices.
- Useful audits make cold outreach respectful; referrals and partners become the main long-term source.
