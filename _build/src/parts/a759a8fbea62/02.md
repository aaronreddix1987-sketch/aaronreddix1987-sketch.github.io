
# Chapter 3: Designing the Offer

:::objectives
- Distinguish a service description from an offer.
- Apply Alex Hormozi's Value Equation to strengthen an offer without exaggerating it.
- Package a core service into tiers with clear inclusions, exclusions, and turnaround times.
- Explain how a productized service works, using Design Pickle as a documented example.
:::

## A service is not an offer

A service description says what you do: "I manage Google Business Profiles." An offer says what the client gets, how quickly, with how much effort on their part, at what price, and with what assurance. Two agencies can deliver identical work and get very different responses, because one presents a vague service and the other presents a clear offer.

A complete offer answers six questions a buyer asks, usually silently:

1. What exactly will I get?
2. What result is this aimed at, and how will I know it is working?
3. How soon will I see something?
4. What do I have to do?
5. What does it cost, and how do I pay?
6. What happens if it is not right?

## The Value Equation

Alex Hormozi, an entrepreneur and author of *$100M Offers* (2021), proposes a simple model of perceived value that is widely used by small service businesses. In his formulation:

:::callout The Value Equation
**Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort and Sacrifice)**
:::

Hormozi describes the four drivers this way, in a post on his public page: the dream outcome is "the gap between their current reality and their dreams"; perceived likelihood is how likely the prospect believes it is "that they will achieve the result they're looking for if they make the purchase"; time delay is the "time between a client buying and receiving the promised benefit"; and effort and sacrifice are the "other costs accrued along the way." Value rises when the top of the fraction grows or the bottom shrinks.

The model is a thinking tool, not a law of economics, and it is easily abused. Inflating the dream outcome with promises you cannot keep raises perceived value for a month and destroys it, along with your reputation, after that. For an honest small agency the most powerful levers are usually on the bottom of the fraction and in the "likelihood" term, all of which you can improve without making a single exaggerated claim.

| Driver | Honest ways to improve it | What not to do |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | Describe the client's goal in their words ("more calls from nearby drivers") | Promise rankings, revenue, or follower counts |
| Perceived likelihood | Show relevant past work, explain your method step by step, offer a fair satisfaction term | Invent testimonials or imply results you did not produce |
| Time delay | Deliver a visible first win in week one (a corrected profile, a first report) | Claim instant results |
| Effort and sacrifice | Do the work for them; ask for information once, in one form; handle logins and posting | Hand clients a to-do list disguised as a service |

:::example Illustrative: rewriting an offer with the Value Equation
Before: "Google Business Profile management, $400 per month."

After: "Profile Care for Auto Repair Shops. In your first week we audit and correct your Google Business Profile (hours, services, categories, photos) and send you a one-page before-and-after summary. Every month after that we add fresh photos and posts, reply to every review within two business days using wording you approve, and send a plain-English report of calls, direction requests, and website clicks. You fill in one 15-minute intake form; we do the rest. $400 per month, cancel with 30 days' notice. If you are unhappy with the first month's work, tell us and we will redo it or refund that month."

Nothing in the rewrite promises a result the agency cannot control. The dream outcome is described in the client's terms, likelihood is raised by a visible method, time delay is shortened by the week-one audit, and effort is cut to a single form.
:::

## Packaging into tiers

Offering two or three tiers helps clients choose and shifts the conversation from "whether" to "which." Effective tiers differ in meaningful scope, not cosmetic extras, and each tier should be deliverable at a healthy margin on its own.

| Element | Essential | Standard | Plus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profile audit and correction | Included | Included | Included |
| Monthly posts | 2 | 4 | 8 |
| Review replies | Within 3 business days | Within 2 business days | Within 1 business day |
| Photos | Client supplies | 1 on-site visit per quarter | 1 on-site visit per month |
| Report | Monthly email | Monthly email | Monthly 20-minute call |
| Price per month (illustrative) | $250 | $400 | $700 |

Write the exclusions as clearly as the inclusions: for example, "does not include paid advertising spend, website changes, or responses to legal complaints." Exclusions prevent scope disputes later (Chapter 11).

:::case Design Pickle: a productized creative service
Design Pickle is a documented example of turning agency-style creative work into a productized service. Its founder, Russ Perry, has described in a published interview that he previously ran creative agencies and then worked as a consultant whose clients needed a steady flow of simple design requests. He set up a team and a digital request system for his consulting clients, and, in his words, "the system started working without me." According to the company's own history, Perry read *The 7 Day Startup* in December 2014 and launched Design Pickle in January 2015 as a flat-rate design subscription. Perry has written on the company blog that Design Pickle launched in 2015 as a "productized design service" offering "unlimited graphic design" for a flat monthly rate.

Two later developments are instructive. First, a podcast interview reported that turnaround time "served as a natural throttle" on unlimited requests, an example of how a productized service must build capacity limits into its design. Second, Perry later published a post titled "We invented unlimited design, and now we're killing it," announcing a change to the unlimited model. A productized offer is not fixed forever; its terms must be revised when they stop matching delivery reality.

These are Design Pickle's and Perry's own published accounts; see References. Their results are not a prediction for any other business.
:::

## Guarantees and satisfaction terms

A fair satisfaction term raises perceived likelihood without exposing you to risks you cannot control. Guarantee what you control: inputs, timeliness, and the quality of your work. Do not guarantee what you do not control: rankings, leads, sales, or the behavior of ad platforms. Examples of controllable guarantees include "every review answered within two business days or that month is free" and "if you are not satisfied with the first month's work, we will redo it or refund that month's fee." Put the exact terms in your agreement, and honor them without argument when invoked.

:::worksheet Build your offer
Offer name: ___
Client's goal, in their words: ___
What they get (inclusions): ___
[[box 3]]
What is excluded: ___
Visible first win and when it happens: ___
What the client must do (keep it short): ___
Price and payment terms: ___
Satisfaction term (controllable only): ___
:::

:::summary Chapter summary
- An offer answers what, why, how soon, how much effort, how much money, and what if it is wrong.
- Hormozi's Value Equation: raise dream outcome and likelihood; lower time delay and effort. Honest agencies win mostly on likelihood, speed to a first win, and low client effort.
- Use two or three tiers that differ in real scope, with written exclusions.
- Design Pickle shows how a productized service works, and that its terms must change when they stop matching capacity.
- Guarantee only what you control.
:::

# Chapter 4: Pricing and Unit Economics

:::objectives
- Calculate a price floor from your costs, target pay, and realistic billable capacity.
- Compare hourly billing, fixed pricing, and value-based pricing, and explain the incentives each creates.
- Use Dan Martell's Buyback Rate to decide when to delegate delivery work.
- Build a simple monthly profit model for a small agency.
:::

## Start with the floor

Every price conversation should begin with a number you will not go below: your **price floor**. It is the monthly fee at which serving one client covers its share of your costs and your own pay. Pricing below the floor means each new client makes the business poorer.

A practical floor calculation has five steps.

1. **Target owner pay.** Decide what you need to pay yourself per year, before tax.
2. **Overhead.** Add yearly costs not tied to any one client: software, insurance, accounting, phone, equipment, training, and marketing.
3. **Profit margin.** Add a margin for reserves and reinvestment; 15 to 20 percent of the total is a common planning figure for small service firms.
4. **Billable capacity.** Estimate the hours per year you can actually spend on client work. Most solo owners overestimate this. Selling, admin, and learning commonly consume 30 to 50 percent of working time.
5. **Divide.** Total yearly requirement divided by billable hours gives a minimum effective hourly rate. Multiply by the hours a client needs per month to get that client's floor price.

:::example Illustrative: computing a price floor
Priya, a fictional solo agency owner:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Target owner pay | $60,000 |
| Overhead (software $3,600, insurance $1,200, accounting $1,500, phone and equipment $1,700, marketing $2,000) | $10,000 |
| Subtotal | $70,000 |
| Margin (20% of subtotal) | $14,000 |
| Yearly requirement | $84,000 |
| Working weeks (48) × 40 hours | 1,920 hours |
| Billable share (60%) | 1,152 hours |
| Minimum effective hourly rate ($84,000 / 1,152) | $72.92 |

Her Standard package takes about 6 hours per client per month. Floor price: 6 × $72.92 = $437.50. Her planned $400 price is below the floor. She has three choices: raise the price to at least $440, cut delivery to about 5.5 hours through better systems, or accept that the package works only as a starter offer that leads to a larger engagement. She chooses to raise it to $450 and to document the process to bring hours down.
:::

## Three ways to set the price

**Hourly billing.** The client pays for time. It feels safe when scope is unclear, but it has structural problems. Income is capped by hours worked. Getting faster lowers your revenue. The client carries the risk of an estimate running long, and the incentives of the two parties point in opposite directions. Jonathan Stark's essay collection *Hourly Billing Is Nuts* makes this case at length, arguing that hourly billing "devalues the expertise of the consultant and encourages mistrust in the client because the financial incentives of the two parties are misaligned."

**Fixed pricing.** The client pays a set fee for a defined scope. The provider carries the risk of the work taking longer and keeps the benefit of doing it faster. Fixed pricing rewards systems and experience, and it gives clients the certainty most of them prefer. Its danger is scoping: a vague scope turns a fixed price into unlimited work.

**Value-based pricing.** The fee is set by reference to what the outcome is worth to the client, not what it costs to produce. Stark suggests in a recorded interview a practical starting point: calculate your floor from costs, estimate the annual value of the outcome to the client, take roughly one-tenth of that value, and charge the higher of the two numbers. Value pricing requires a genuine discovery conversation (Chapter 8) and a client who can articulate the value of the outcome. It is most useful for project work with a clear business goal, and less practical for small recurring packages.

| Method | Who carries the risk of overrun | What getting faster does | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Client | Lowers your revenue | Truly undefined advisory work, briefly |
| Fixed | Provider | Raises your margin | Productized packages and defined projects |
| Value-based | Provider | Raises your margin | Projects with a measurable business goal |

For most small agencies the practical recommendation is to sell fixed-price packages, set above a calculated floor, and use value-based reasoning to set the price of larger projects.

## Buying back delivery time

As an agency grows, the owner faces a recurring question: should I do this task myself or pay someone else to do it? Dan Martell's Buyback Rate offers a simple rule. In *Buy Back Your Time*, he proposes dividing your annual pay by 2,000 working hours to get an effective hourly rate, then dividing by four. Tasks that can be handed off at or below that rate should be handed off. Martell frames delegation as a roughly fourfold return trade rather than an expense.

:::example Illustrative: applying the Buyback Rate
Priya now pays herself $80,000 a year. Effective hourly rate: $80,000 / 2,000 = $40. Buyback Rate: $40 / 4 = $10 per hour.

She spends 20 hours a month scheduling posts and resizing images. A part-time assistant at $20 per hour would cost more than her Buyback Rate, so by Martell's strict test she should not hand off this work yet at that price. However, a scheduling tool and a template library costing $60 a month would cut the task to 8 hours, saving 12 hours at an effective cost of $5 an hour, well below the rate. She adopts the tool first and revisits hiring when her pay, and therefore her Buyback Rate, rises.
:::

## A monthly profit model

Keep a one-page monthly model. Update it at the end of every month.

| Line | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Clients | Count | 10 |
| Average monthly fee | Revenue / clients | $480 |
| Revenue | Clients × average fee | $4,800 |
| Direct delivery cost | Contractors, client-specific tools | $900 |
| Gross profit | Revenue − direct cost | $3,900 |
| Gross margin | Gross profit / revenue | 81% |
| Overhead | Monthly share of yearly overhead | $833 |
| Owner pay | Monthly draw | $2,500 |
| Net profit | Gross profit − overhead − owner pay | $567 |
| Delivery hours | Logged | 58 |
| Revenue per delivery hour | Revenue / hours | $82.76 |

Revenue per delivery hour is the most useful single number for a small agency. If it falls month after month, either prices are too low, scope is creeping, or systems are not improving.

:::warning Common pricing mistakes
- Pricing by copying a competitor's rate without knowing their costs or scope.
- Discounting the first month so steeply that the client anchors to the discounted price.
- Including unlimited revisions or "unlimited" anything without a capacity limit.
- Forgetting that you will spend unpaid hours selling, invoicing, and learning.
- Absorbing client ad spend on your own card, which creates cash-flow and collection risk.
:::
