
# Chapter 8: Sales Conversations and Proposals

:::objectives
- Run a structured discovery call that diagnoses before it prescribes.
- Qualify prospects on fit, need, budget, and decision process.
- Present three options and write a short proposal that closes.
- Identify the essential terms of a service agreement.
:::

## Diagnose before you prescribe

Blair Enns, a consultant to creative firms, sets out twelve "proclamations" in *The Win Without Pitching Manifesto* (2010). The third is "We will diagnose before we prescribe." Enns describes four phases of a client engagement (diagnose the problem or opportunity, prescribe a therapy, apply the therapy, and reapply it as necessary) and argues that prescribing without diagnosis would be treated as malpractice in most other professions. When a client arrives self-diagnosed ("we just need more posts"), the professional response, in the spirit of the manifesto, is to validate that diagnosis before acting on it: "you may be right, but let's check."

The practical meaning for a small agency is that the first sales conversation is a structured diagnostic, not a presentation. Enns also writes that the objective of early conversations is not "to sell, convince or persuade" but "simply to determine if there exists a fit suitable enough to merit a next step." That framing lowers pressure on both sides and leads to better clients.

## A discovery call in five parts

Allow 30 to 45 minutes. Send a short agenda in advance.

1. **Context (5 minutes).** How the business works: services, typical customer, busy and slow seasons, how customers find them today.
2. **Goal (5–10 minutes).** What they want to change and why now. Ask for specifics: "When you say more customers, what kind of jobs, and roughly how many more a month would make a difference?"
3. **Current state (10 minutes).** What they have tried, what worked, what did not, and who does the marketing now. Look at their accounts together if possible.
4. **Constraints (5–10 minutes).** Budget range, timeline, decision-makers, and anything that would stop them proceeding. Raise money early; Enns's ninth proclamation is "We will address issues of money early."
5. **Next step (5 minutes).** Either agree to send a proposal by a specific date, recommend someone else, or agree there is no fit.

:::callout Questions that reveal value
- "If this worked well, what would be different in six months?"
- "What is a typical new customer worth to you over a year?"
- "What happens if you do nothing about this for another year?"
- "Who else needs to be comfortable with this decision?"
:::

## Qualifying

Not every prospect should become a client. Qualify on four criteria.

| Criterion | Proceed if | Pause or decline if |
|---|---|---|
| Fit | They are in your niche and need your core service | They need something you don't offer well |
| Need | There is a clear gap your service addresses | Their real problem is elsewhere (pricing, service quality, staffing) |
| Budget | Their range is at or above your floor | They want your Plus tier at your Essential price |
| Decision | You are speaking with the decision-maker, or can reach them | Decisions are vague or controlled by someone you cannot meet |

Declining politely, and recommending another provider where possible, protects your capacity for clients you can genuinely help.

## Three options

After the call, present three options that differ in scope and price: typically your Essential, Standard, and Plus tiers, adjusted to what you learned. Three options let the client choose how much help they want rather than whether to hire you, and they make the middle option easy to justify. Jonathan Stark recommends a similar structure of small, medium, and large options, each tied to the client's goal.

## The short proposal

A proposal should confirm what was discussed, not introduce new ideas. Keep it to two to five pages.

1. **Situation.** Two or three sentences restating their context in their words.
2. **Goal.** The outcome they described and how progress will be measured.
3. **Approach.** Your method, in plain steps.
4. **Options.** The three tiers with inclusions, exclusions, and prices.
5. **Timeline.** What happens in week one, month one, and month three.
6. **Terms.** Payment, minimum term, notice period, and satisfaction term.
7. **Next step.** How to accept (signature and first payment) and when work begins.

Send the proposal within two business days of the call, and offer a 15-minute follow-up call to walk through it. Proposals that arrive a week later lose momentum.

:::example Illustrative: proposal summary page
**Situation.** Rivera Auto has served the east side for 22 years. Most new customers come from referrals and Google searches. The profile has outdated hours and unanswered reviews, and Ana handles it when she has time, which is rarely.

**Goal.** Make sure nearby drivers searching for repairs see accurate, current information and real responses to reviews, measured by profile calls, direction requests, and website clicks each month.

**Options.** Essential $250/month; Standard $450/month (recommended); Plus $750/month. Three-month minimum, then month to month with 30 days' notice.

**Next step.** Sign and pay the first month by March 8; audit and corrections completed by March 15.
:::

## The service agreement

Every engagement needs a written agreement. Have a template reviewed by an attorney in your state once, then reuse it. Essential terms include:

- **Scope.** Inclusions and exclusions, referencing the chosen tier.
- **Fees and payment.** Amount, due date, method, late-payment terms, and how ad spend is handled.
- **Term and termination.** Minimum term, notice period, and what happens on termination (access handover, final report).
- **Ownership.** The client owns accounts, data, and finished deliverables once paid; you retain your templates and methods.
- **Approvals.** Who approves content and review replies, and how quickly.
- **Client responsibilities.** Information, access, and timely feedback.
- **Confidentiality and data.** How you handle client and customer information.
- **No guarantee of results.** A plain statement that results depend on factors outside your control.
- **Limitation of liability.** Commonly capped at fees paid over a period; ask your attorney.

- Qualify on fit, need, budget, and decision process; decline politely when there is no fit.

# Chapter 9: Onboarding and Delivery Systems

:::objectives
- Design a two-week onboarding sequence that produces a visible first win.
- Document delivery tasks using Dan Martell's Camcorder Method.
- Structure recurring work in a project management system.
- Build quality control into delivery with checklists and approvals.
:::

## Onboarding sets the relationship

The first two weeks of an engagement shape how a client feels about the next twelve months. A well-run onboarding reassures the client that they chose well, collects everything you need in one pass, and delivers a visible first win quickly, which is the "time delay" lever from Chapter 3.

:::checklist Two-week onboarding sequence
- [ ] Day 0: Agreement signed, first payment received, welcome email sent with what happens next.
- [ ] Day 1: Intake form sent (brand details, logins via manager access, approvers, photos, priorities).
- [ ] Day 2: Kickoff call (30 minutes) to confirm goals, approvals, and communication rhythm.
- [ ] Days 3–5: Baseline captured (screenshots and current metrics) before any changes.
- [ ] Days 5–10: First visible improvements made (for example, profile corrected).
- [ ] Day 10: Before-and-after summary sent to the client.
- [ ] Day 14: Recurring schedule running; first month's calendar approved.
:::

Capturing a baseline before changing anything is essential. Without it, you cannot show later what changed, and you cannot write an honest case study.

## Documenting delivery: the Camcorder Method

Most small agency owners carry their processes in their heads. That works until they are sick, on holiday, overloaded, or trying to hire. Dan Martell's *Buy Back Your Time* describes what he calls the **Camcorder Method** for transferring work: record yourself doing a task (a screen recording with narration), then turn the recording into a written playbook that someone else can follow. The point is that documentation captures what you actually do, not what you think you do.

A useful playbook for each recurring task includes:

1. **Purpose.** Why the task matters and what "done well" looks like.
2. **Trigger and frequency.** When it happens.
3. **Inputs.** What is needed before starting.
4. **Steps.** Numbered, with screenshots or a link to the recording.
5. **Quality checklist.** What to verify before delivery.
6. **Time standard.** How long it should take once learned.

Start with the task you repeat most often. For many agencies that is the monthly report or the weekly posting cycle. A playbook written once saves hours every month and is the precondition for delegating the task, whether to an assistant, a contractor, or a supervised software tool.

## Structuring recurring work

Use one project or board per client, built from a standard template. A recurring template might look like this:

| Week | Tasks | Owner | Approval needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Draft next month's posts; collect photos | Producer | Client approves calendar |
| Week 2 | Schedule approved posts; reply to reviews | Producer | Review replies per approved templates |
| Week 3 | Mid-month check on metrics and profile changes | Owner | None |
| Week 4 | Build monthly report; plan next month | Owner | None |

Basecamp, discussed in Chapter 1, was built for this exact reason: 37signals' founders wrote that their "projects and client communications were a mess" when managed by email. Whatever tool you choose, the principle is the same: every task, file, and approval for a client lives in one place, and nobody has to search an inbox to know what is due.

## Approvals and quality control

Errors in client marketing are public. A misspelled business name, a wrong price, or a careless review reply can embarrass a client in front of their customers. Two controls prevent most problems.

**Approval rules.** Agree in writing what needs client approval before publishing (monthly calendars, new offers, replies to negative reviews) and what you may publish under pre-approved templates (thank-you replies to positive reviews, routine posts). Google's third-party policy, covered in Chapter 5, requires explicit approval to reply to reviews on a client's behalf, so get that approval in writing and keep it.

**Pre-publication checklist.** Before anything goes live, check: business name, address, and phone correct; prices and offers current and authorized; spelling and grammar; links working; images licensed or owned; no claims about results, health, or safety that the client cannot substantiate; required disclosures present.

:::case Martell's Camcorder Method in context
In *Buy Back Your Time* (2023), Dan Martell presents the Camcorder Method as part of a broader argument that founders should hire to buy back their time rather than simply to grow headcount. He pairs it with the **Replacement Ladder**, a sequence of five roles founders typically hand off in order: administrative assistant, delivery or operations, marketing, sales, and leadership. In Martell's sequencing, delivery is the second rung, after administrative support. That ordering is directly relevant to agency owners, whose delivery work typically consumes most of their week. Recording and documenting delivery is what makes that second rung possible. These are Martell's frameworks as summarized in his book and in published reviews; see References.
:::

# Chapter 10: Reporting and Measurement

:::objectives
- Distinguish activity, leading, and outcome metrics for a marketing engagement.
- Explain the limits of attribution and how to report honestly within them.
- Build a one-page monthly report clients actually read.
- Use reports to guide decisions and renewals.
:::

## Three levels of measurement

| Level | Examples | What it tells you | Who controls it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Activity | Posts published, reviews answered, ads launched | Whether the agreed work happened | The agency |
| Leading indicators | Profile views, calls, direction requests, clicks, form fills, cost per lead | Whether people are responding | Shared: agency, platform, market |
| Outcomes | Booked jobs, revenue, new customers | Whether the business benefited | Mostly the client |

A good report includes all three, clearly separated. Activity metrics prove you did the work. Leading indicators show whether the work is reaching people. Outcomes, which usually require data from the client, show whether it matters. Never present activity as if it were an outcome: "we posted 16 times" is not a result.

## The attribution problem

Attribution means assigning credit for a customer to a particular marketing activity. It is genuinely difficult. A customer may see a post, search the business name a week later, read reviews, ask a friend, and then call. Analytics platforms use models to assign credit, and different models give different answers. Phone calls, word of mouth, and offline activity are often invisible to the tools.

The honest approach is to state the limits plainly in your reports, use consistent definitions month to month, and where possible add simple client-side evidence, such as asking new customers "how did you hear about us?" at booking. Over several months, consistent measurement shows trends more reliably than any single month's attribution.

## The one-page monthly report

Clients rarely read long reports. A single page, delivered on the same day each month, works better.

1. **Headline.** One or two sentences: what happened this month and what it means.
2. **Work completed.** Activity metrics against the plan.
3. **Key numbers.** Three to five leading indicators, with last month and the same month last year where available.
4. **What we learned.** One or two observations.
5. **Next month.** What you will do and any decisions needed from the client.

:::example Illustrative: monthly report headline
"April was steady. Profile calls were 84, down slightly from 88 in March but up from 63 last April, according to the Business Profile performance report. We answered all 11 new reviews within two business days and published 4 posts. The post showing the new alignment machine had the most views. Next month we will add photos of the expanded waiting area; please send them by May 5."

The headline is factual, compares fairly, names its data source, and ends with a concrete request.
:::

## Using reports to make decisions

A report is a management tool, not just a deliverable. Use each month's report to answer three questions: Is the agreed work happening on time? Are leading indicators trending in the right direction over three months? Is there a change, in the offer, the content, or the channel, worth testing next month? Quarterly, review the engagement with the client against the goal agreed in the proposal. This review is also the natural moment to discuss renewal, expansion, or, when results are poor, a change of approach.

:::warning Honest reporting rules
- Report bad months as plainly as good ones.
- Keep metric definitions constant; if you change one, say so.
- Name the data source for every number.
- Do not claim credit for outcomes you cannot connect to your work.
- Do not cherry-pick date ranges to make results look better.
:::

:::worksheet Report template
Client and month: ___
Headline (two sentences): ___
Work completed vs plan: ___
Key numbers (metric, this month, last month, last year, source): ___
[[box 3]]
What we learned: ___
Next month and client decisions needed: ___
:::

:::summary Chapter summary
- Separate activity, leading indicators, and outcomes; never present activity as a result.
- Attribution is imperfect; state its limits, keep definitions consistent, and add client-side evidence.
- A one-page report with a headline, work completed, key numbers, learning, and next steps is read more often than a long one.
- Use reports and quarterly reviews to guide decisions and renewals.
:::
