# How to Use This Workbook

This exercise-based workbook is for people with some stability in early recovery who are ready to work on everyday life. You may be leaving treatment or incarceration, building recovery in the community, or using it as a peer mentor. Reflect on identity, build daily structure, and practice money and work skills. Complete chapters in any order; skip questions, write briefly, or return later. The goal is useful progress, not perfect answers.

This workbook is educational, not treatment or medical, legal, tax, or financial advice. It does not replace support from a clinician, sponsor, peer specialist, or other qualified professional. Bring concerns requiring professional help to someone qualified whom you trust.

:::warning If you are in danger right now
If you are in crisis, call or text 988. It is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day. In Spanish, call 988 and press 2, text AYUDA to 988, or use the Spanish chat. Veterans can call 988 and press 1 [1]. For an immediate emergency, call 911.

For treatment referrals and information, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. It is free, confidential, and available 24 hours a day in English and Spanish. TTY: 1-800-487-4889 [2].
:::

## Begin with safety and stability

This workbook is for people with some stabilization, often after the first few months of recovery. If you are in the first days or weeks, or withdrawal may be a concern, prioritize medical care and stabilization. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous; speak with a health care provider if you think it may affect you [3]. Workbook exercises are not a substitute for medical support.

Recovery takes many forms. People may use clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, self-care, or a combination. Progress can include setbacks [4]. Choose exercises that fit your needs and adapt your approach as circumstances change.

## How each chapter is built

Chapters pair a practical idea with a chance to apply it. Exercises are invitations, not tests. Use a notebook for more space; if writing is difficult, talk through an answer, draw a map, or ask someone to write as you dictate.

## Use the workbook in a trauma-informed way

Your safety, choice, and sense of control matter. Topics such as money, relationships, work, or past events may bring up strong feelings. Skip a question, return later, stop, take a break, or ask for support. If it feels safe, choose a private place and a workable pace.

You do not have to share answers with a mentor or group. Before sharing, agree on what will remain private and the limits of privacy. Supportive conversations make room for your voice and choices, not pressure to disclose. These practices reflect key elements of a trauma-informed approach: safety, trust, collaboration, and empowerment [5].

## From surviving to building

The core idea is **recovery first, building second**. Rebuilding identity, routines, finances, and work takes time; productivity is not a measure of recovery. A packed schedule or ambitious plan is not automatically a strong plan. Aim for a life that supports well-being without causing burnout.

Start small. A routine might begin with one regular activity; a money plan, with one bill or question; a work goal, with exploring an option. Match steps to your energy, responsibilities, and support. If a plan stops working, adjust it rather than treating that as failure.

She plans to discuss work and money questions with her peer mentor. This is an illustrative example, not a promise of any outcome.
:::

Choose a page or question before meeting; a mentor can ask whether you want listening, brainstorming, or help selecting a next step. You may decline to discuss an answer.

# Chapter 1: From Surviving to Building

:::objectives
- Describe four practical stages of early recovery without treating them as a fixed timetable.
- Use Health, Home, Purpose, and Community as building blocks for a recovery-supporting life.
- Identify personal, social, community, and cultural resources that make up recovery capital.
- Check readiness for a new goal and make a paced plan that protects recovery.
:::

## Recovery is a foundation, not a finish line

Early recovery often focuses on stabilization: getting through the day, reconnecting with support, and addressing immediate needs. Over time, many people want to build routines, relationships, work, financial stability, and a sense of who they are becoming. This chapter offers ways to begin without changing everything at once.

The stages here are a planning model, not a clinical rule or test. They are not tied to a number of days or months. People may move between them, work on several at once, or pause when life becomes difficult. A lapse or return to use does not erase what you have learned or built. Treatment may need to be restarted or adjusted; a clinician can help you consider appropriate support [6].

SAMHSA describes recovery as a process of change in which people improve health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and work toward their full potential [4]. Recovery may include clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, self-care, or a combination of supports [4]. Choose what fits your life and skip any exercise that does not feel safe or useful.

## Four building blocks

SAMHSA identifies four dimensions of recovery: **Health, Home, Purpose, and Community** [7]. They help you see progress beyond one measure: you may be gaining ground in one area while needing support in another.

- **Health** means managing health conditions or symptoms and making informed, healthy choices. Consider the routines and support that help you care for your well-being. Discuss symptoms, treatment, or medication with a doctor or clinician.
- **Home** means having a stable, safe place to live and practical conditions that make it supportive.
- **Purpose** means meaningful daily activities, such as work, school, volunteering, family care, or creative work, and the resources and independence to take part in society.
- **Community** means relationships and social networks that offer support, friendship, love, and hope.

These dimensions connect. A daily routine may support both health and purpose; a supportive person may help you stay connected while seeking stable housing. You need not address all four before taking a next step. Notice what is helping, what needs attention, and where a small action could make life more supportive.

## Recovery capital: take inventory before you build

**Recovery capital** is the sum of personal and social resources available to address substance dependence and strengthen a person's capacity and opportunities for recovery [8]. This strengths-based idea asks what you can draw on as well as what is difficult. Resources may be personal, social, community-based, or cultural: physical health, supportive relationships, recovery role models, practical skills, or places where you feel welcome [8].

An inventory is not a score of your worth. A low rating can point to support you want; it does not mean failure. Assets change, and some may be available only in certain settings. Pause or leave a line blank if writing about a resource brings distress.

Health, Home, Purpose, and Community organize the inventory; recovery capital names resources within and around them. A reliable bus route, trusted relative, cultural practice, or problem-solving experience may each be an asset. Your list will be specific to your life.

## A four-stage planning model

Use these stages to consider what kind of next step fits now. The signs are prompts, not requirements, and you may recognize yourself in more than one row. Moving forward can mean choosing a small task, not making a major life change.

| Stage | Main focus | Possible signs of readiness to move on |
|---|---|---|
| Stabilize | Immediate safety, support, and basic needs | You know who to contact for support and have a next step for urgent needs. |
| Structure | Repeatable routines and practical follow-through | Some daily anchors are more predictable, and you can notice when a routine needs adjusting. |
| Build | Skills, resources, and goals in work, money, health, or home | You can name a manageable goal, its needed support, and a first step. |
| Contribute | Connection, shared purpose, and giving back by choice | You can offer time or skill without making your worth depend on being useful. |

**Stabilize** means having enough support for immediate needs to make the next day more manageable. This may involve treatment or peer support, a safe place to be, or help with a practical problem. For medical concerns, consult a doctor or clinician rather than using this workbook to make treatment decisions.

**Structure** means developing a few reliable anchors, such as a regular check-in, a meal, time to rest, or a task at a consistent point in the day. The goal is not a perfect schedule; a routine should support you, not become a standard for self-criticism.

**Build** means putting time into a chosen goal: learning a skill, exploring work, organizing bills, or planning for housing. Choose a goal small enough to test. If it creates too much strain, scaling it back is useful information.

**Contribute** may mean offering help, sharing a skill, or joining a community activity. It is a choice, not an obligation or proof of recovery. SAMHSA includes peer support and relationships among recovery principles and notes that helping others can strengthen a person's recovery [4]. Keep your own needs in view as you decide what to offer.

## Why pacing matters

A big goal can bring energy and hope, but it can also add pressure when several parts of life are changing. In a relapse-prevention model, lifestyle imbalance and urges or cravings can be covert antecedents to a lapse. High-risk situations and coping skills are among the immediate factors that can shape what happens next [9]. Effective coping can build confidence in managing difficult situations; ineffective coping can lower confidence. A lapse may bring guilt and a sense of failure that increase risk [9].

This is not a reason to avoid ambition; it is a reason to plan for a goal's demands. A business, job search, or course may involve deadlines, money decisions, less rest, or unfamiliar people and places. Consider what support, time, and recovery practices need to remain in place.

A goal can be ambitious and still be paced: break it into actions you can review and revise. If the first version is too demanding, reduce its scope, delay a decision, or ask for help. That is information, not a verdict on your ability.

:::example Illustrative example: A business idea at 30 days
Maya wanted to start a small catering business about 30 days into recovery. She had recipes, a business name, and strong motivation. She also noticed that late-night planning left her tired and less connected to the support that was helping her.

Rather than abandon the idea or sign a lease, Maya clarified what she wanted it to provide: creative work, income, and direction. She chose one low-pressure step: list likely costs and review them with someone she trusted. She kept her support appointments and limited planning time. A few weeks later, she reviewed the pace and chose her next step.

Maya's example is illustrative, not a promise of an outcome. The useful practice is to protect what supports you while testing a goal in manageable pieces.
:::

## A readiness check before a new goal

Readiness does not require certainty or every resource. It means having enough information to make a considered choice and a way to respond if the plan becomes difficult. Before committing to a larger goal:

1. **Name the goal.** Write what you want to do and why. Separate your reasons from pressure or expectations from others.
2. **Check the foundation.** Consider Health, Home, Purpose, and Community. Which areas are supported, and which may need attention during the goal?
3. **List the demands.** Note the time, money, energy, travel, decisions, and relationships involved, including what you do not yet know.
4. **Identify high-risk situations.** Consider settings, stress, or routine changes that may make usual supports harder to use. Skip details that feel unsafe to write; focus on helpful support.
5. **Choose a first step and pace.** Make the first action specific and limited: gather information, make one call, or spend a short time on a draft.
6. **Plan for support and adjustment.** Decide whom to contact and what to do if the plan disrupts sleep, routines, or connection. If a return to use occurs, seek renewed support rather than hiding or shaming yourself [6].
7. **Set a review point.** Choose a day to ask whether the goal is worthwhile, what is working, and what needs to change. Continue, pause, or revise.

An if-then statement can make the plan concrete: **If** a situation comes up, **then** I will take a specific action. For example: “If I feel overwhelmed by the first business task, then I will stop, contact my support person, and decide whether to continue tomorrow.” Planning when, where, and how to act can help translate a goal into action [10].

# Chapter 2: Identity and Values

:::objectives
- Explore identity as something you can build through choices and repeated actions.
- Identify five personal values and describe what each looks like in daily behavior.
- Practice statements that connect the person you are becoming with actions you can take.
- Use small successes, peer examples, encouragement, and stress-management to support self-efficacy.
:::

## Identity is a process, not a verdict

Early recovery can bring the question, “Who am I now?” You may describe yourself through what happened during active substance use, ways you hurt yourself or others, or roles you lost. Those experiences matter, but they are not the whole of you. Recovery also involves noticing what you care about, what you practice, and what you are working toward.

Identity is not a fixed label you must settle before moving forward. It develops through experience. Keeping one manageable commitment can help you see yourself as dependable. Showing up consistently can be part of rebuilding trust as a parent. Asking a clinician a question or following a plan made with your care team can express a commitment to health. No single action defines a life, but repeated actions give you evidence about who you are becoming.

Recovery is a process of change in which people improve health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and work toward their potential [4]. Goals and pathways differ. Your life may include mutual help, medication, therapy, family support, faith, cultural practices, self-care, or other supports. It may also include pauses, changes in direction, or setbacks. You do not have to prove your worth by following one path.

### The risk of a shame-based identity

Shame turns an action or period of life into a total judgment: “I made a harmful choice” becomes “I am nothing but harmful.” This can make it harder to notice strengths, ask for support, or take a next step. Accountability is different: it names what happened, recognizes its effects, and looks for a responsible response without denying your dignity.

Try changing “I am a failure” into an honest, workable statement: “I missed an appointment. I can contact the office, ask what to do next, and set a reminder.” This does not erase what happened; it gives you a way to respond. If reflecting on identity brings distress, pause, skip an exercise, or return to it with someone you trust. Safety and choice matter in recovery [5].

Use language that feels right to you. Some people find “person in recovery” useful; others prefer different words. No phrase describes your whole life. The aim is to widen your story, not replace one rigid label with another.

## Values: what matters in practice

A value is a quality or direction that matters to you. Unlike a goal, which can be completed, a value can guide you toward many goals. Learning, for example, can guide you in school, while reading, or when asking someone to show you a task.

Values may come from family, culture, faith, community, experience, or reflection. You can keep values you were taught, revise them, or choose new ones. Make a value useful by describing it as behavior. Respect might mean listening without interrupting, speaking honestly, or honoring a boundary. Stability might mean making a realistic weekly plan and asking for help before a problem becomes urgent.

A gap between a value and your current behavior is information, not proof that you are a bad person. Ask what is getting in the way and what small action could bring you closer. Barriers may call for practical support with transportation, housing, or scheduling. Recovery includes health, a safe and stable home, purpose, and supportive community relationships [7]. Values are lived within real conditions, not in a vacuum.

## A step-by-step values and identity practice

Use this framework in one sitting or over several days. Write, talk it through, or leave a question unanswered.

1. **Choose words that matter.** Mark values that draw your attention and add your own. Choose what feels meaningful, not what sounds impressive.
2. **Narrow the list to five.** Consider which values you want to guide an ordinary Tuesday, or which you miss when they are absent.
3. **Define each through behavior.** Complete “For me, this value looks like…” with actions someone could notice. Keep them realistic for your life now.
4. **Choose one action for this week.** Make it small and specific. “Be responsible” is broad; “Open the mail for ten minutes on Wednesday” is observable.
5. **Write an identity statement.** Connect a quality to an action: “I am someone who values learning, so I can ask one question when I do not understand a task.” Use “I am practicing” if that feels more believable.
6. **Plan for an obstacle.** Name what could make the action harder and decide whether to ask for support, choose another time, or make the action smaller.
7. **Review without grading yourself.** Notice what you tried, what helped, and what you might adjust. The aim is learning, not a perfect score.

:::example Illustrative example: Malik chooses dependability
Malik is an illustrative person in early recovery. He chooses dependability, family, health, learning, and humor as his five values. At first, he writes, “Dependability means never letting anyone down.” That feels impossible, so he revises it: “Dependability means communicating when plans change and doing what I said I would do when I can.”

For this week, Malik chooses one action: call his sister at the time they agreed on. His identity statement is, “I am someone who is practicing dependability by keeping one clear commitment.” If he feels overwhelmed, he plans to message her to reschedule rather than disappear. At the end of the week, he can review what happened without deciding that one success or one missed call defines his character.
:::

## Self-efficacy: building trust in your capacity

Self-efficacy is a person’s belief in their capacity to carry out behaviors needed for a specific outcome [11]. It is not confidence in every area of life or a guarantee that a task will be easy. You may feel capable of asking for help but unsure about managing money, or confident at work and uncertain in family relationships.

Self-efficacy can shape what you try and how you respond to difficulty. People with higher self-efficacy tend to set larger goals, persevere, and recover from setbacks; lower self-efficacy can contribute to avoiding difficult tasks and becoming discouraged [11]. These are patterns, not permanent traits. You can build evidence of capability gradually:

- **Small successes:** Break a large task into steps and notice each one you complete. A phone call, meal, or application section can be useful evidence.
- **Peer examples:** Notice people with experiences you relate to taking a next step. Their paths need not match yours. Peer support and helping others can support recovery [4].
- **Encouragement:** Ask someone you trust to recognize effort specifically. “You asked for clarification instead of giving up” is more useful than a vague promise that everything will work out.
- **Calming the body:** When stress makes a task feel unmanageable, pause and use a grounding or calming practice that feels safe. Then decide whether to continue, simplify the task, or ask for support.

Keep the goal appropriately sized. If “find a job” feels overwhelming, list kinds of work you are open to or ask someone to review a resume. If a step does not work, adjust the plan rather than treating it as a verdict on your identity. Health concerns and treatment decisions belong with a doctor or clinician. This workbook is educational, not a substitute for treatment.

## Identity statements that lead to action

An identity statement links a chosen quality with behavior. It should feel believable enough to practice and specific enough to guide a choice. “I am strong” may feel distant; “I am someone who asks for help before I reach a crisis point” offers an action. If that feels too certain, try “I am practicing asking for help.”

These are reminders of a direction you have chosen, not affirmations you must repeat until you believe them. Use your own voice and avoid absolutes. “I always stay calm” leaves no room for a hard day; “I am learning to pause before I respond” leaves room to grow.

## Peers, role models, and belonging

You do not have to build an identity alone. A peer, mentor, family member, clinician, or community member may help you see possibilities that are hard to see during a difficult week. Peer support and relationships are important parts of recovery [4]. A role model might be someone who shows patience, keeps a boundary, or returns to a goal after a setback.

Admiring someone does not mean copying their choices or story. Ask what quality you notice, how it could look in your circumstances, and what support or resources you might need. If a relationship feels unsafe, pressuring, or shaming, seek another source of support. Choice and safety matter.

You can be a useful example without presenting yourself as an expert. Sharing one thing that helped may offer connection, but you are not responsible for another person’s recovery and do not have to share personal details to belong. Mutual support can mean listening, asking permission before offering advice, and stating your limits.

# Chapter 3: Goals That Survive Real Life

:::objectives
- Explain why vague goals are hard to act on.
- Choose goals across recovery, health, relationships, money, work, learning, and contribution.
- Turn a goal into a small, specific action with an if-then plan.
- Plan for obstacles without treating setbacks as proof of failure.
- Revise any goal that threatens recovery.
:::

## Goals are directions, not tests of worth

Early recovery can bring a wish to make up for lost time: repair relationships, find work, improve health, manage money, and change all at once. Those hopes matter, but trying to pursue them simultaneously can make it hard to see what to do today.

A goal is a chosen result or direction; an action is a step you can take. “Get my life together” does not say what to do at 10 a.m. “On Tuesday, I will call the employment center after breakfast” does. Vague goals leave unanswered questions: What will I do, when and where, and what might get in the way? A goal may also exceed your current time, energy, resources, or recovery needs. That calls for a clearer plan or a smaller first step, not a judgment about your commitment.

Make the first step small, specific, and scheduled. It should be manageable, observable, and tied to a time and place. A modest action can serve a meaningful goal.

Values describe what matters; goals turn values into choices and actions. If you value reliability, you might aim to keep one agreed appointment this week. Choose goals that matter to you, not ones that impress others.

## A range of goal types

You do not need a goal in every area. Choose what matters now, and leave room to change your mind.

- **Recovery:** attend a support meeting, contact a counselor, or plan for a high-risk situation.
- **Health:** arrange an appointment or build a daily routine. Discuss symptoms, treatment, and medication with a doctor or clinician; this workbook does not provide medical advice.
- **Relationships:** make a respectful check-in, set a boundary, or wait before replying to a difficult message.
- **Money:** list regular bills, set a spending-review reminder, or ask someone trusted to help organize paperwork.
- **Work:** update one part of a résumé, explore training, or contact a possible employer.
- **Learning:** practice a skill, read about a subject, or take a step toward a class.
- **Contribution:** help, care for a family member, volunteer, or make something. A modest contribution can still matter.

Areas can overlap: work may support purpose and financial stability; relationships may support community. The aim is not perfect categorization, but a choice suited to your priorities and current capacity.

## Make plans that include the real world

An **implementation intention** is an if-then plan: “If situation Y occurs, then I will do X.” It links a likely situation to a specific response. A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests involving more than 8,000 participants found a medium-to-large positive effect on goal attainment beyond goal intentions alone [10]. It cannot guarantee success, but naming when and how to act can make a goal more actionable.

Include an obstacle and a backup: “If the bus is delayed, then I will call to reschedule instead of abandoning the appointment.” A backup is part of planning for ordinary complications, not a lesser version of success.

Use the framework as a draft, not a contract. Revise it as you learn what works. If a plan puts recovery at risk, change it. Recovery comes before speed, approval, or a self-imposed deadline.

## A five-step goal framework

1. **Name what matters.** Choose a value or life area that feels important now. You can use Chapter 2, skip reflection, or return to it later.
2. **Choose one result for the next 90 days.** Keep it focused. “Build a steadier work routine” is more manageable than “fix my career.” The timeframe gives direction; it does not require solving everything by a set date.
3. **Define a first action.** Say what you will do, when, and where. Make it small enough to begin. For example, instead of “get healthier,” write questions for your next appointment on Thursday evening. Ask a qualified clinician for medical guidance.
4. **Write an if-then plan and obstacle response.** Name a likely situation and your response. Consider one barrier and a safe, realistic alternative. A trusted person may help you plan, if you wish.
5. **Review and adjust.** At a regular time, ask whether the plan fit your life, what helped or got in the way, and whether the goal still supports your values and recovery. Keep, shrink, change, pause, or replace it. A missed step is information for the next plan, not a verdict on your character.

## Illustrative example: Maya’s 90-day goal set

Maya is fictional and has been in recovery for several months. Wanting more structure, she chooses three goals for 90 days: keep recovery supports central, organize part of her finances, and explore work that fits her routine. She does not expect to solve every concern.

For recovery, she plans to attend a weekly support group that feels like a good fit: “If it is Wednesday afternoon, then I will check my ride plan and put the meeting address in my calendar.” If her ride falls through, she will contact the group or a trusted support person and choose a safe alternative. Different pathways and supports suit different people.

For money, Maya wants to feel less avoidant about bills, not promise to eliminate debt. She will list due dates for bills she can identify: “If I finish dinner on Sunday, then I will spend 15 minutes listing bills at the kitchen table.” If she feels stuck, she will stop after 15 minutes and ask someone trusted to sit with her another day.

For work, she wants to protect her recovery routine. First, she will list hours and conditions to discuss with a support person before applying: “If I have my support check-in on Friday, then I will bring my work questions and review them before choosing a next step.” If a job requires dropping important supports, she will pause and revise her plan.

After a few weeks, one plan works, one needs a smaller step, and one needs a different time. That is useful information, not failure. Her goals serve her life; her life does not have to serve the goals.

# Chapter 4: Daily Structure Without Burnout

:::objectives
- Build a day around a few steady anchors rather than a packed schedule.
- Use time blocks and a daily HALT check to notice needs before they build.
- Plan for energy, connection, and rest as well as work and recovery practice.
- Recognize signs of overload and adjust a weekly plan without treating change as failure.
:::

## Structure that supports recovery

A daily structure is a flexible pattern for meeting important needs, not a demand to fill every hour. A few repeatable actions—getting up, eating, attending a recovery activity, completing a work or learning task, moving, connecting, and resting—can make a day easier to navigate.

A small routine you can repeat may be more useful than an intense plan that lasts only a few days. Fit your structure to your health, housing, family responsibilities, culture, work, and recovery pathway. If a plan does not fit, use that information to revise it.

Recovery is a process of change in which people improve health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and work toward their full potential [4]. SAMHSA describes four dimensions: Health, Home, Purpose, and Community [7]. A recovery practice may support Health, a housing task Home, work or learning Purpose, and a call or group Community. One schedule need not cover all four every day.

## Start with anchors, then add blocks

An anchor is a regular activity that gives the day shape. It can happen at a set time or in a reliable sequence. With changing work hours, for example, eating after waking may be a more useful anchor than eating at a specific time.

Useful anchors include sleep and waking, meals, recovery practice, work or learning, movement, connection, and rest. Treat them as supports, not boxes to check perfectly. If a day is disrupted, focus on the next workable anchor rather than making up every missed task.

Time blocking means setting aside time for one kind of activity. Blocks can be broad, such as morning tasks, or specific, such as calling a training program. Leave space for travel, transitions, and unexpected needs; a packed plan can become a source of pressure.

### A step-by-step way to design your day

1. **Choose a wake and sleep range.** Start with a realistic window that fits your responsibilities. If sleep is difficult, do not turn the schedule into a demand to fall asleep on command. Sleep disturbances were reported by 69% of 119 patients at admission to a residential alcohol program; sleep is relevant in early recovery, though findings varied by subgroup [12]. Discuss persistent or troubling sleep concerns with a health care provider.
2. **Place basic care next.** Identify likely times for meals, personal care, and practical tasks. Keep the plan workable when energy is limited.
3. **Schedule recovery practice.** Choose an activity that fits your pathway, such as a meeting, counseling appointment, peer connection, reflection, or another recovery-supporting practice.
4. **Add one or two purpose blocks.** These might include work, a job-search task, class, household responsibility, or creative activity. Begin with the next useful step, not every step toward a large goal.
5. **Make room for movement, connection, and rest.** These belong in the plan; they are not rewards you must earn. Choose forms that suit your body, preferences, and setting.
6. **Check the load.** If every block is demanding, remove or shorten something. Include transitions and unscheduled space.
7. **Review and revise.** Notice what helped and what got in the way. Keep what worked and adjust what did not. A trusted person or clinician can help you consider changes.

## Use HALT as a brief daily check

HALT prompts you to ask whether you may be hungry, angry, lonely, or tired. It is not a diagnosis or an explanation for every difficult feeling. Use it to notice a basic need and consider one small, safe response: eating, stepping away to settle, contacting someone, or reducing the day’s demands.

Try the check at a regular time, such as before leaving home or after a work block, or when a plan suddenly feels impossible. If the answer is unclear, return to it later.

## Energy management and overload

Plan around the energy you have, not only the time you have. Place demanding tasks when you are more able to focus and simpler tasks near transitions or lower-energy periods. Rest can be a planned block: a quiet break, time outdoors, an unhurried meal, or time without obligations. Choose what feels restorative to you.

Marlatt’s relapse-prevention model includes lifestyle imbalance, urges, and cravings as covert antecedents that can contribute to risk [9]. Use this as a reason to notice when a week has become crowded or depleted, not to blame yourself. Possible signs include repeatedly dropping meals or rest, losing contact with supportive people, taking on more than you can complete, or having no room to recover between obligations. These signs do not prove that a return to use will happen; they prompt a pause, support if helpful, and a more sustainable plan.

If a schedule change brings strong distress, consider options with a clinician or trusted support person. This workbook is educational and does not replace treatment. In a crisis, call or text 988; for treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. In an emergency, call 911.

# Chapter 5: Habits and Tracking

:::objectives
- Describe how cues, routines, and rewards can shape habits.
- Design small habits that fit your current life and priorities.
- Use tracking as information, not as a score of your worth.
- Practice a weekly review and plan for restarting after a missed day.
:::

## Habits are supports, not tests

A habit is a behavior that becomes easier to repeat when linked to familiar circumstances. One useful model is **cue, routine, and reward**: a cue signals an action, the routine is the action, and the reward makes it worthwhile. Seeing your keys by the door might cue you to sort spending receipts for a few minutes; knowing where to find them later is the reward.

This model is a planning tool, not an explanation for every behavior. Needs, energy, responsibilities, and resources differ, so a routine that helps one person may not fit another. Adapt or pause a habit as needed. Recovery can involve different pathways and setbacks [4]. The aim is to make daily life more workable, not to prove you can follow a perfect schedule.

Give a habit a **tiny version** and a **full version**. If you plan to review bills, the tiny version might be opening the folder and looking at one bill; the full version might be checking due dates and updating your budget. Both keep the habit available on days with different amounts of time and energy. If the tiny version is still too demanding, make it smaller or change the cue.

## Choose habits that serve your priorities

Recovery can involve health, a safe home, meaningful activities, and supportive relationships. These connected dimensions may support one another [7]. Choose one to three habits that matter to you now rather than trying to work on every area at once.

Start with what you want the habit to help with: less rushed mornings, clearer spending records, connection with someone supportive, or progress toward work or school. Choose an action you control. You can plan to spend ten minutes on a resume, for example, but cannot control whether an employer responds.

Habit stacking links a new action to an existing one: **After I [existing action], I will [small new action].** For example, after putting away your lunch container, you might record today’s spending. This if-then plan specifies when and how you will act. A meta-analysis of 94 tests with more than 8,000 participants found that such plans had a medium-to-large positive effect on reaching goals beyond goal intentions alone [10]. Change the cue if it proves unreliable.

## A seven-step habit design framework

1. **Name the reason.** Write why the habit matters to your priorities, such as understanding your bills or feeling prepared for a work meeting.
2. **Choose one action.** Make it observable. “Take care of myself” is important but difficult to track; “write one expense in my notes” describes an action.
3. **Find a dependable cue.** Choose an event, place, or time likely to occur. “After breakfast” may work better than “at 8:00” if mornings vary. Add a backup cue if needed.
4. **Make a tiny version.** Decide what you can do on a low-energy day. It is a way to stay connected to a goal when circumstances are difficult.
5. **Describe the full version.** Set a reasonable upper limit for days when you have more capacity. You can stop there without judging yourself for not doing more.
6. **Choose a simple way to track.** Mark whether you tried, or note useful details such as minutes spent or whether you used the tiny or full version. Skip tracking that feels intrusive or distressing.
7. **Review and adjust.** Once a week, ask whether the cue appeared, the tiny version was realistic, and the habit helped. Keep, change, or drop the plan. It is a draft, not a fixed promise.

## Track for feedback, not judgment

Tracking gives you information, not a measure of character or value. It can show that a habit works better after lunch, that the plan is too large for a busy day, or that the routine is not useful enough to keep.

Track the attempt rather than an outcome you cannot control. For work, record time spent on an application, not whether you got an interview. For money, record that you looked at a bill, not whether you could pay it immediately.

A streak may encourage repetition, but it is only one way to describe it. If it breaks, ask what happened and what the next small action could be. A missed day is not the same as abandoning the plan: restart with the tiny version, change the cue, or choose a better-fitting habit.

For substance use recovery, a lapse can bring guilt or a sense of failure, which may raise the risk of further use. A practical coping step rather than all-or-nothing thinking is part of relapse-prevention planning [9]. A return to use is a reason to seek support and review the plan, not a verdict on a person. Treatment may need to be reinstated or adjusted [6]. Tracking does not diagnose or manage a substance use disorder. If a return to use or safety concern arises, contact a clinician or trusted support person if it feels safe.

**Wins** include actions and adjustments worth noticing: using the tiny version, remembering a cue, asking for help, or pausing a habit that was not helping. **Lessons** are observations without blame. Compare days when the habit happened with days when it did not. Was the cue dependable? Was the action clear? Was the plan too large, or did another responsibility take priority?

# Chapter 6: Money Triage

:::objectives
- Put immediate safety and recovery needs ahead of lower-priority bills.
- List income, fixed bills, and variable spending to see what money is available and when.
- Use a cash-flow calendar to plan around due dates and paydays.
- Choose practical safeguards that make it easier to protect recovery while managing money.
:::

## Why money triage belongs in recovery

Money problems can create pressure and conflict, making it harder to focus on recovery. Looking at the numbers is not a test of character; it helps you decide what needs attention first. Work on one part at a time or ask someone you trust to sit with you.

**Money triage** means sorting needs by urgency and importance, then planning how to use available funds. It does not mean paying every bill in full right away. Protect immediate safety, housing, and recovery needs before less urgent spending or debt payments.

Recovery is self-directed and may draw on different forms of support and care. A stable, safe place to live is a major dimension of recovery described by SAMHSA; meaningful daily activity and supportive relationships matter too [7]. A workable money plan can support these areas without solving everything at once.

This chapter is educational, not a substitute for treatment or financial, legal, or medical advice. Ask a qualified professional about bills, benefits, or debts with serious consequences. Discuss medication and treatment questions with a clinician; do not change a care plan to make a budget work.

## 1. Protect essentials before lower-priority payments

Start with what keeps you safe and supports recovery:

1. **Housing:** rent, a safe place to stay, or urgent housing costs.
2. **Food:** enough to eat, including for dependents in your care.
3. **Utilities:** services needed to keep a home safe and usable.
4. **Transportation:** travel to work, treatment, appointments, or essential responsibilities.
5. **Medication and treatment:** care costs and related transportation. Ask your provider about options if costs create a barrier.

Then consider other obligations, such as debt minimums, phone service, or agreed household costs, followed by wants and flexible spending. This is a starting point, not a rule for every household. Deadlines, safety concerns, or court requirements may change priorities; seek qualified guidance when needed.

| Category | Examples | First question |
|---|---|---|
| Needs | Housing, food, utilities | What protects safety and daily living? |
| Recovery needs | Treatment, medication, travel to care | What keeps my care plan accessible? |
| Obligations | Debt minimums, phone, agreed bills | What is due, and when? |
| Wants | Optional purchases, entertainment | What can wait or be reduced? |

If you cannot cover everything, note the shortfall and when it matters. If it feels safe, contact the provider or person you owe to ask about options. Avoid promising a payment you cannot make.

## 2. Know your numbers

Make three lists:

- **Income:** expected take-home money, with amounts and dates.
- **Fixed bills:** costs that are usually the same or follow a regular schedule.
- **Variable spending:** costs that change, such as food, transportation, and household supplies.

Use recent records, but do not wait for perfect information. Mark estimates and replace them with actual spending after a few weeks. If income varies, plan around what you can reasonably count on.

### Check your credit reports

You can get free weekly credit reports from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion through AnnualCreditReport.com. Checking does not affect your credit score, and the reports do not include a score [13]. Note unfamiliar accounts or details and use the report provider's process to ask about them. You do not need to resolve credit issues before making a basic spending plan.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's Your Money, Your Goals toolkit has 43 tools and handouts on spending, credit reports, debt decisions, and tracking income and bills. Spanish-language resources are available at consumerfinance.gov/es [14]. Choose a tool for the issue at hand rather than trying to complete everything.

## 3. Make a cash-flow calendar

A monthly total can hide timing problems, such as income arriving after rent is due. A **cash-flow calendar** shows when money comes in and expenses must be paid.

1. Add expected income dates to a calendar or list.
2. Add fixed bills and due dates, including treatment costs and transportation.
3. Plan variable spending by week.
4. Compare available money with costs due before the next income date. Identify any shortfall early and decide what to ask, adjust, or postpone.
5. Update amounts and dates as they change. The calendar is a planning tool, not a promise that every month will be predictable.

Utilities on the fifth leave $220, the phone bill on the eighth leaves $170, and treatment and medication costs on the twelfth leave $70. The second paycheck raises the balance to $770 on the fifteenth. She then sets aside $100 for food and $40 for transportation on the sixteenth, pays the $100 debt minimum on the twentieth, and sets aside another $100 for food and $40 for transportation by the twenty-fourth. The balance is $390. Household and personal items ($100), flexible spending ($190), and savings ($100) bring it to $0 by month-end.

The plan uses all of Maya's income. An unexpected expense would require a change or support, not self-blame. She could revisit flexible spending, ask about a bill's timing, or review the plan with someone she trusts. Her example is not a model every person should copy; costs, pay dates, and priorities differ.
:::

## 4. Protect recovery around money

Choose money routines that fit your situation and preserve your choices:

- **Plan for payday:** Check which essentials and due dates the money must cover. Divide cash into planned amounts or pay essential bills first if that helps.
- **Choose an accountability partner:** If safe, ask someone you trust to review the plan or check in before a stressful purchase. Agree on the help you want.
- **Reduce easy access to a trigger:** If gambling apps or another spending pattern concerns you, consider removing apps or setting a barrier you choose, with help if wanted.
- **Keep recovery costs visible:** Include treatment, medication, and transportation in the plan. Contact your provider if costs are difficult to meet.
- **Plan for a lapse without shame:** Note what happened and what needs attention next. Consider what support or adjustment could make the next decision safer.

Skip any exercise that does not feel safe. If a financial crisis involves immediate danger or thoughts of self-harm, call or text 988 for free, confidential support [1]. For U.S. treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357 [2]. In an emergency, call 911.

# Chapter 7: Repairing Financial Damage

:::objectives
- Build a judgment-free inventory of debts and account details.
- Choose a practical order for bills and repayment decisions.
- Check credit reports and contact creditors with a clear request.
- Create a cautious six-month plan with a small emergency cushion.
- Consider financial amends while protecting recovery and respecting legal guidance.
:::

## Money repair is part of recovery

Missed payments, collection notices, and low balances can bring shame or fear, but they do not measure your worth. An inventory is a tool for seeing what needs attention and choosing a next step.

This chapter offers a framework, not a rule for every situation. Housing, health, safety, family responsibilities, income, and court requirements all matter. You can work with a recovery supporter, counselor, financial counselor, or legal professional. This workbook is educational, not legal or financial advice.

A **debt inventory** lists who is owed, how much, the minimum payment, account status, and interest rate if known. A **credit report** records information about credit accounts and related history; it can contain errors, so verify entries rather than assuming they are correct.

Repair does not mean paying everything immediately. It can mean protecting basic needs, understanding obligations, correcting inaccurate information, communicating clearly, and choosing sustainable payments.

## A seven-step framework

Use these steps as a starting point. For urgent deadlines, safety concerns, or court orders, seek appropriate professional guidance; do not use this framework to decide legal questions.

1. **Stabilize essentials.** List realistic costs for housing, food, utilities, transportation needed for work or care, and health needs. If money does not cover everything, contact a qualified local advisor or service to sort urgent choices.

2. **Make the debt inventory.** Gather statements, letters, account records, and court documents you can access. Record creditor, balance, minimum payment, status, and interest rate. Mark unknown details for verification; note the date and source rather than guessing.

3. **Check credit reports.** Look for unfamiliar accounts, incorrect balances, or inaccurate payment information. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) offers a free toolkit with 43 tools and handouts on credit reports, debt, spending, income, and bills [14]. Spanish-language resources are also available. The federally authorized website for free credit reports offers free weekly reports from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion; checking a report does not affect a credit score, and the report does not include a score [13].

   For an error, save the report and supporting documents. Follow the credit reporting company’s dispute instructions and, when appropriate, contact the company that supplied the information. Identify the item and correction requested, keep copies, and record when you sent them. Ask a qualified advisor about notices you do not understand.

4. **Sort obligations by urgency and consequence.** Protect essentials first. Identify court-ordered obligations, such as child support, fines, or restitution, and get legal advice about orders, deadlines, changes, or questions about what is owed. Do not assume a payment can be paused or changed without approval. Then consider secured debts, tied to property such as a vehicle, followed by unsecured debts such as credit cards or personal loans. This is a planning sequence, not a legal priority rule.

5. **Contact creditors with a specific request.** Confirm the account, explain what you can manage, and ask about options. Check your budget before naming an amount and request terms in writing before agreeing. If a call becomes confusing or upsetting, pause and continue later.

6. **Avoid new traps.** Be cautious with high-cost loans, fast cash offers, or payments you cannot maintain. Before accepting credit, write down the total repayment, payment dates, and consequences of late payment. If terms are unclear, wait and ask someone qualified to review them.

7. **Build a cushion and review.** Set aside an amount for unexpected expenses only if essentials and required payments remain protected. Review monthly and after changes in income, housing, or obligations. A lapse does not remove your right to support or a workable next step.

| Debt type | First action | Planning note |
|---|---|---|
| Essentials or utility arrears | Protect current service and ask about options | Keep current needs in view |
| Court-ordered obligation | Confirm terms and seek legal advice | Do not assume terms can change |
| Secured debt | Check status and consequences | Consider what the property is needed for |
| Unsecured debt | Verify balance and minimum | Ask about manageable arrangements |

## Contacting creditors without overpromising

Before calling, write down the account, the amount you believe you owe, your question, and what your budget can support. You need not share recovery details. If phone calls are difficult, ask for a mailing address or another way to communicate.

Ask the representative to explain proposed amounts, due dates, fees, interest, or account-status changes. Request terms in writing and review them before committing. Keep a call log with the date, person or department, and discussion. A creditor may decline; the call can still clarify your options.

## Financial amends require care

For some people, repairing financial harm is part of recovery. It may involve acknowledging harm, planning repayment, or taking another responsible action. It does not require immediate contact, an unaffordable promise, or an action that risks safety. Consider legal issues, court orders, privacy, and the other person’s wishes.

Discuss the idea with a sponsor, counselor, or trusted recovery support, and get legal guidance when obligations or consequences may be involved. An amends plan should be specific, realistic, and respectful of boundaries. If payment is not possible, ask about other appropriate ways to take responsibility. Recovery is not measured by one dramatic gesture.

# Chapter 8: Work Paths in Recovery

:::objectives
- Connect work with purpose while respecting recovery needs.
- Compare work paths and identify questions to ask before choosing one.
- Prepare for an interview, including decisions about disclosure and a criminal record.
- Build a skills list and a brief, honest account of your experience and goals.
:::

## Work can be part of recovery

Work may mean paid employment, training, an apprenticeship, volunteering, caregiving, or building a small business. It can offer structure, income, relationships, and contribution; it can also bring pressure, conflict, fatigue, or difficult situations. A useful plan considers both.

SAMHSA describes Purpose as meaningful daily activities, including work, school, volunteering, caregiving, or creative work, as well as the independence, income, and resources to participate in society [7]. Paid employment is not required for recovery; several meaningful activities may fit together, and purpose can change over time.

Recovery involves change toward better health and wellness, a self-directed life, and reaching one’s potential [4]. A self-directed work plan begins with your priorities, not someone else’s timeline. Recovery pathways differ, and progress can include setbacks [4]. Adjusting a work goal is not personal failure.

**Recovery capital** means the personal, social, community, and cultural resources that support recovery [8]. Skills, supportive relationships, transportation, housing, treatment, and mutual help may affect what work is realistic. Some resources are already available; others can be built over time.

This chapter is educational, not a substitute for treatment. Discuss workplace or schedule-related health concerns with a doctor or clinician. You may skip an exercise and return to it later.

## Choose a pace that supports recovery

A job can add structure, but the right starting point depends on your needs and circumstances. Consider the whole week: commuting, getting ready, appointments, meals, rest, and relationships as well as scheduled hours.

Before accepting a role, consider its demands. Does it involve early mornings, long shifts, travel, physical effort, customer conflict, or changing hours? Could people or settings make it harder to maintain routines and supports? What sounds engaging, and what would need a plan?

Pacing means choosing a step that supports growth while remaining manageable. One person may start part-time, seasonally, or temporarily; another may choose training or a full-time job with predictable hours. Review the choice as your needs change.

| Area | Ask yourself | Possible next step |
|---|---|---|
| Schedule | Can I manage the hours and travel? | Ask about shifts and commute |
| Support | Can I keep useful routines? | Map appointments and support |
| Setting | What conditions help me do well? | Ask about daily tasks |
| Flexibility | What if my needs change? | Identify a contact or plan |

## A step-by-step work-planning framework

1. **Name what work should support.** Choose what matters now: income, routine, learning, contribution, connection, or a longer-term career goal.
2. **Take stock of your capacity.** Consider energy, schedule, transportation, caregiving, housing, and existing commitments. This is planning, not a test of worth.
3. **List strengths and skills.** Include paid and unpaid experience: caring for family, solving problems, organizing, repairing, learning a tool, or following through.
4. **Compare work paths.** Consider tasks, schedule, training, support, and stress; explore more than one option.
5. **Prepare your application and interview.** Practice likely questions, decide what to share, and prepare questions for the employer. If a record may come up, plan a truthful, concise response.
6. **Ask about the workplace.** Learn about a typical day, scheduling, training, and support. Recovery-ready workplaces may expand employment, facilitate help-seeking, and support access to treatment and mutual aid [15].
7. **Review and adjust.** Notice what works and what is difficult. Consider a small adjustment and who can help you assess it.

## Compare work paths

A path is a starting point, not a permanent label. You might work part-time while training or explore a business while holding an entry-level job. Consider current needs and future interests.

| Work path | Possible advantages | Cautions to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Entry job | A direct way to build routine and experience | Schedule or tasks may not fit your current needs |
| Apprenticeship or training | Time to learn a trade or skill | Check the time, costs, and attendance demands |
| Peer role | Uses lived experience to support others | Requirements and role duties vary by state and employer |
| Self-employment | More control over tasks and schedule | Income and workload may be less predictable |

Peer specialist and recovery coach roles are possible paths. Requirements vary by state and employer. Before choosing, ask about training, supervision, boundaries, and daily duties, including the demands of supporting others.

Self-employment may include freelance work, a small service, or selling something you make. Before investing, write down basic costs, time demands, and first steps. Seek qualified guidance for financial or legal questions; a small test can help you assess interest before taking on a larger commitment.

## Disclosure, records, and employer supports

**Disclosure** is the choice to share personal information. You need not give an interviewer every detail of your history. Prepare a brief, honest statement about what you have learned, what you offer, and how you are moving forward. Do not promise more than you can know. You may choose not to discuss personal history unless it relates to a question or job requirement.

If you have a criminal record, prepare rather than improvising. Keep your response truthful and brief: acknowledge the record, describe a change you have made, and return to your readiness for the job. Share only what you choose while answering direct questions honestly. Practice with someone you trust and use your own words.

Some people may have rights to reasonable accommodations or other workplace protections; what applies depends on the circumstances. Recovery-ready workplaces may inform employees about possible rights and make it easier to seek help [15]. For guidance on a specific situation, contact a qualified professional or relevant public agency. This chapter cannot determine which protections apply.

The Work Opportunity Tax Credit (WOTC) is a federal employer tax credit for hiring people certified as members of certain targeted groups. These include qualified ex-felons, qualified veterans, SNAP recipients, vocational rehabilitation referrals, SSI recipients, and long-term unemployment recipients. State workforce agencies handle certification [16]. Recovery alone does not establish eligibility.

If you may fit a listed group, you can ask about WOTC without promising you qualify: ‘I may qualify for the Work Opportunity Tax Credit based on my background. State workforce agencies handle certification. Is there someone in your hiring process I should speak with about it?’ You need not explain personal details to an interviewer if you choose not to. Ask the state workforce agency about certification; do not assume the employer knows the program or that you qualify.

The example does not suggest that every employer will respond in the same way. Tasha cannot control the hiring decision, but she can prepare a clear account, ask useful questions, and consider whether the role fits the life she is building.

# Chapter 9: Starting Small, Safely

:::objectives
- Decide whether a small business or side project fits your recovery priorities right now.
- Identify a service you can offer using skills and tools you already have.
- Test an idea with limited time and money before making a larger commitment.
- Make a plan to protect recovery, personal finances, and your safety while you test.
:::

## Work can be one part of recovery

A side project may offer structure, purpose, income, or a chance to practice skills. It is an option, not a requirement. Work, school, caregiving, volunteering, creative activity, and other daily roles can all support a meaningful life.

SAMHSA describes recovery through four dimensions: Health, Home, Purpose, and Community. Purpose includes meaningful daily activities and resources to participate in society; the other dimensions include managing health, having a safe place to live, and supportive relationships [7]. Consider a business idea only if it fits alongside the parts of life that help you stay well and supported.

A **side project** is a limited test of an idea, such as offering a service to a few customers. **Self-employment** means working for yourself rather than as an employee. A test may show that an idea needs changes, the timing is wrong, or you prefer another path. Each is useful information.

Recovery is a process of change, and people may follow different pathways and experience setbacks [4]. If you are uncertain, discuss the idea with someone you trust, such as a peer, counselor, or other support person. You can also skip these exercises and return later.

This book is educational, not a substitute for treatment. Discuss questions about health, treatment, or the effects of a work plan on your care with a doctor or clinician. If you are in crisis, call or text 988. For treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. In an emergency, call 911.

## A recovery-first test

Before spending money or taking on customers, check stability, support, time, and money you can afford to lose. This is not a pass-or-fail test; it helps identify what needs attention before you act.

- **Stability:** Do your routines leave room for sleep, meals, appointments, and recovery activities? If not, consider a smaller test or a later start.
- **Support:** Who could you contact if work becomes stressful or isolating? Choose a peer, family member, counselor, or other person who feels safe and useful.
- **Time:** What hours can you reliably offer without displacing essential responsibilities? Set a limit before accepting work.
- **Money:** What can you spend without affecting rent, food, transportation, bills, or other essentials? If no amount is safe, try a no-cost test or wait.

There is no need to prove commitment by taking a large risk. If a project disrupts sleep, support, health routines, or basic expenses, pause and review the plan. Asking for help is a practical safeguard.

## A five-step idea test

Write down your choices before spending money or promising work.

1. **Name a service you can already do.** Begin with a practiced skill, not a business identity you feel you must create. Consider tasks people ask you to do, skills from past work, or an ability you can demonstrate safely. Keep the offer narrow: one task, one kind of customer, and a defined area or setting.

2. **Describe one likely customer and problem.** A customer might need a small outdoor surface cleaned. Ask a few potential customers what they need, what they currently do, and what would make your offer useful. Interest is not a sale, and avoid promising results you cannot control.

3. **Set a small test limit.** Decide dates, number of customers, hours, and maximum spending in advance. Choose a test that can answer a real question: whether people will book, how long the task takes, or whether you enjoy the work. Do not take on debt or spend money needed for essentials.

4. **Track time and money separately.** Record every expense, payment, and hour. Keep business activity distinct from personal spending; at minimum, maintain clear written records and do not treat every payment as personal spending. For tax, legal, or licensing questions, seek qualified guidance for your situation.

5. **Review before expanding.** Compare results with your plan. Did customers book? Did the task fit your hours? Were costs higher than expected? Did the work leave room for recovery and daily needs? Stop, adjust, repeat the small test, or learn more before investing. You do not have to scale up.

:::example Illustrative example: A weekend pressure-washing test
Maya is a fictional person considering a pressure-washing service. She checks that it will not displace recovery support or essential routines. For a two-weekend test, she limits herself to two jobs, four hours of work per weekend, and $80 in spending.

For this hypothetical example only, she already has access to a pressure washer and estimates fuel and cleaning supplies at $15 per job. She offers small-area cleaning for a hypothetical $60 per job. Two jobs would bring in $120, with $30 in listed supplies, before other costs, taxes, or the value of her time. These figures are for practice, not a forecast or income promise.

Maya records hours, customer questions, and unexpected costs. If the work takes longer than planned or creates stress, she can stop after the test. Before spending more, she can review the results and identify what else she needs to know.
:::

## Common risks and practical safeguards

Self-employment can bring freedom but blur the line between work and the rest of life. Plan for predictable pressures while the project is small.

| Risk to watch | A safeguard to plan |
|---|---|
| Isolation | Schedule regular contact with supportive people |
| All-night work | Set a stop time and limit work hours |
| Cash handling | Keep a written record of payments and expenses |
| Stress or overbooking | Cap jobs and leave room between commitments |

These safeguards are planning prompts, not guarantees. Change a limit if the plan proves unsafe or unrealistic. Supportive relationships and social networks are part of SAMHSA’s Community dimension [7]. Protect time for connection rather than letting work quietly replace it.

# Chapter 10: Mentors and Giving Back

:::objectives
- Tell the difference between a recovery mentor and a career mentor.
- Practice asking for guidance and preparing for a useful first conversation.
- Identify ways to offer service without taking on more than your recovery can carry.
- Make a mentor map and choose one manageable way to give back this month.
:::

## Guidance and service in early recovery

A mentor shares experience, perspective, or practical knowledge to help you make your own decisions. A mentor is not a substitute for a clinician, counselor, sponsor, or other support person. One person may fill several roles, but it helps to be clear about the support you want.

A **recovery mentor** focuses on recovery choices and routines, such as building a support network or staying connected to your goals. They need not share your history or pathway; what matters is that the relationship feels respectful, safe, and useful.

A **career mentor** focuses on work, education, or a chosen craft. They might help you explore a job search, describe your strengths, or understand a role. They are not responsible for managing your recovery. If work stress affects your recovery, seek support from a recovery resource or clinician as well.

It is reasonable to have several sources of support. SAMHSA describes recovery as having many pathways, including clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, and self-care. Progress can include setbacks. [4] Choose supports that fit your needs and values; that mix can change over time.

Helping others can strengthen a person’s recovery, but service is optional. Your health, housing, work, relationships, and basic routines also deserve attention. Service should offer meaning and connection, not become a test of your worth. This chapter provides practical education, not treatment or clinical advice. If a helping role may be affecting your recovery, discuss it with a clinician or trusted support person. You can pause, set a limit, or skip an exercise.

## Step-by-step: building a support and service plan

1. **Name the need.** Be specific: “I want ideas for structuring my mornings” is easier to act on than “I need to get my life together.”

2. **Choose the kind of support.** Is the need mainly about recovery, work or education, or both? A recovery mentor may help with a support routine; a career mentor may help you prepare for an interview. Bring concerns requiring professional treatment or clinical judgment to a clinician.

3. **Look for a good fit.** Consider people you know, peer support settings, community organizations, or people in a field you want to explore. Notice whether they listen, respect your choices, and focus on your goals. You need not share personal history to ask for help.

4. **Make a clear request.** Say what guidance you want and how much time you are asking for: “Would you be open to a 20-minute conversation about how you built a steady weekly routine? It is completely fine if now is not a good time.”

5. **Prepare.** Bring two or three questions. Decide what you are comfortable sharing and what you want to keep private. Ask about the person’s experience without treating it as a rule.

6. **Listen, then choose.** Take notes if useful. You can thank someone for an idea without using it. Mentorship is guidance, not control; your choices remain yours.

7. **Follow up and set a boundary.** If the conversation helped, send a brief thank-you and say what you plan to try. Discuss whether another conversation makes sense; one meeting does not create an ongoing commitment.

8. **Review your capacity before serving.** Start small. Decide how much time you can offer and what is outside your role. If your recovery needs more attention, step back.

## What to ask different support people

Use this table as a prompt, not a script. Adapt the questions to your culture, values, and comfort level.

| Support person | Main focus | A useful question | Keep in mind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recovery mentor | Recovery routines and choices | “What helped you stay connected to support?” | Their pathway is not a rule for you. |
| Career mentor | Work, school, or a trade | “What skills should I build for this role?” | They are not responsible for your recovery plan. |
| Clinician or counselor | Professional care and well-being | “Can we talk about what support fits this concern?” | Seek clinical guidance for clinical concerns. |
| Peer support person | Shared experience and connection | “Would you be available to listen or think this through?” | Mutual support still needs boundaries. |

## Asking well and being mentorable

Being mentorable does not mean being agreeable. Approach advice with curiosity while keeping your right to decide. Before meeting, consider what you hope to leave with: one idea to try, feedback on a resume, or another person’s perspective. Share only what is relevant. You can say, “I would rather not discuss that today.”

During the conversation, ask follow-up questions such as, “What did that look like in practice?” or “What would you suggest I try first?” If advice does not fit, say, “I appreciate the idea. I need to think about whether it works for me.”

Afterward, choose one next step rather than making a list of promises. If the conversation felt respectful and useful, ask whether another would be welcome. If it did not feel right, end the connection politely or seek someone else. A good mentor relationship should leave you with more clarity and choice.

:::example Illustrative example: Maya’s first mentoring conversation
Maya is building a reliable weekly routine and considering returning to work. She wants career guidance without making one person responsible for both career advice and recovery support. She identifies a peer for recovery conversations and asks a former coworker for a short career conversation.

Maya prepares two questions: “What entry-level roles helped you learn the field?” and “What is one skill I could practice before applying?” She tells the coworker, “I’m exploring my next work step. I’m comfortable talking about my goals, but I’m keeping some personal details private.”

The coworker suggests a skill to practice and offers to review a short resume draft. Maya thanks them and decides to try the skill before asking for another meeting. She also checks in with her peer about keeping job-search stress from crowding out her recovery routines. She takes useful ideas from both conversations while choosing her own pace.
:::

Maya is illustrative; not every first conversation will go smoothly. You can slow down, change the subject, or end a conversation if it stops feeling safe or useful.

## Giving back without overextending

Service can be small: welcome someone at a group, set up chairs, share a useful resource, take on a limited task, or support a community activity. Before considering sponsorship or another ongoing role, think through the time and emotional responsibility involved. Ask an experienced support person what the role requires.

Some recovery community centers offer structured ways to participate. Recovery Cafés, for example, use a membership model with agreements to keep the space drug- and alcohol-free, participate in a weekly Recovery Circle, and contribute to the Café. Their practices include “loving accountability” and “radical hospitality.” [20] Expectations vary by center, so ask directly before joining.

A boundary protects your time, safety, and responsibilities. In a helping role, it can define when you are available, how long you can talk, what help you can offer, and what you cannot take responsibility for. You can care about someone without being available at all hours or taking charge of their decisions. Encourage someone who needs clinical, crisis, or specialized help to contact an appropriate professional or service.

Before agreeing, ask: Do I have time? Do I feel steady enough today? Do I understand the role? Could I say no or pause? If you sense strain, choose a smaller task or wait. You do not need to earn your place in recovery by taking on more than you can carry.

Friends supported him into an extended treatment program. He has been sober since August 1, 2008. In 2011, he founded the Herren Project, a nonprofit addressing substance use disorder, and he speaks publicly. [21]

Their story and results are their own; they are not a promise of any outcome for the reader and are not results of TTI.

## When support needs to change

A mentor relationship may no longer fit as your needs change or the person’s time or skills prove insufficient. You can thank them and seek another kind of support. If someone pressures you to share more than you want, dismisses your choices, or asks you to ignore your safety, pause contact and discuss what to do with someone you trust.

There is no single correct amount of service. Some people are ready for a regular commitment; others need to focus on rest, appointments, housing, work, or family. Both are valid. SAMHSA’s recovery principles emphasize safety, choice, relationships, respect, and peer support. [4] Let those values guide how you ask for help and offer it.

If you are in crisis, call or text 988 for free, confidential support. [1] For treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. [2] In an emergency, call 911.

# Chapter 11: Your 90-Day Plan and 12-Month Review

:::objectives
- Bring values, goals, routines, money, work, and support into one practical plan.
- Divide the next 90 days into three manageable 30-day sprints.
- Use weekly and monthly reviews to adjust your plan without judging yourself.
- Review recovery capital and the four dimensions of recovery at 12 months.
:::

## Make the plan serve your life

A plan is a tool, not a test of character. It helps you choose what matters, make room for it in ordinary days, and notice when something needs to change. Recovery is a process of change in which people improve health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and work toward their potential. It may involve different pathways and setbacks, and should respect your choices and strengths [4].

This chapter brings together values, goals, routines, money, work, and support. Choose a few meaningful steps, make them specific, and revise them as needed. If writing about something feels unsafe or overwhelming, skip it or work through it with someone you trust.

Recovery capital means the personal and social resources available to address substance use and strengthen recovery opportunities. It can include health, supportive relationships, community resources, and cultural strengths [8]. Rating these resources is not a measure of your worth; it helps identify what is helping and where support may be useful.

SAMHSA describes four dimensions of recovery: Health, Home, Purpose, and Community. Health includes managing symptoms and making informed choices; Home is a stable, safe place to live; Purpose includes meaningful daily activities and resources to participate in society; Community includes supportive relationships and networks [7]. Use these as broad areas for reflection, not as a checklist to complete perfectly.

## A framework for building your 90-day plan

Use these steps to keep your plan manageable and actionable.

1. **Name what matters.** Choose two or three values you want your choices to express, such as reliability, learning, family connection, creativity, or health. A value is a direction, not a finish line. Identify one small action that reflects it this week.
2. **Choose a few outcomes.** Pick no more than three goals for the 90 days. Examples include creating a weekly spending routine, attending a support meeting, progressing toward work or school, or building a steadier morning routine. Choose goals that matter to you.
3. **Turn outcomes into actions.** Specify what you will do, when, and what might get in the way. For example: On Sunday afternoon, I will review my bills and write down the next week’s spending. Plans specifying when, where, and how to act can help people follow through on goals [10].
4. **Build support around the plan.** Identify people, services, or groups you can contact. Recovery may involve clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, and self-care; no single pathway fits everyone [4]. Discuss treatment or medication decisions with a doctor or clinician. This workbook is educational, not a substitute for treatment.
5. **Review and adjust.** Each week, note what helped, what got in the way, and one change that could make the next week more workable. Revising a plan is part of using it well.

Choose a clear next step and a reasonable way to notice progress. Instead of the broad goal of getting your life together, call the workforce center on Tuesday and ask what documents to bring. If a goal feels too large, shrink the next step.

## Three 30-day sprints

A sprint is a short period with a main focus. These focus areas are options; change the order or repeat a sprint to fit your circumstances.

| Sprint | Main focus | Possible milestones |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–30 | Stabilize routines and support | Set a weekly review time; choose a support contact; track key spending for two weeks |
| Days 31–60 | Build skills and forward movement | Practice a useful routine; complete one work, school, or training step; revise a spending plan |
| Days 61–90 | Strengthen and sustain | Keep helpful habits; address one barrier; plan support and goals for the next 90 days |

At each sprint’s start, choose one or two milestones. At its end, note what happened and what you learned. A milestone might be a task, repeated habit, useful conversation, or clearer understanding of needed support. Avoid adding tasks just to make the plan look ambitious.

:::example Illustrative example: Maya’s first 90 days
Maya, a fictional person in recovery, chooses steadiness and independence as values. For days 1–30, she plans a weekly calendar review, attends one support group each week, and records purchases without judging them. Her milestone is to complete three reviews and identify one spending pattern.

For days 31–60, Maya plans a weekly amount for food and transportation, asks a trusted person to check in, and contacts a local employment service. Her milestones are four spending check-ins and the employment call. For days 61–90, she plans to keep the calendar and spending routines, follow up about work options, and review which support to continue. If the call takes longer than expected or a routine slips, she will revise the next step rather than abandon the plan.

This is one possible plan, not a model everyone should copy. Maya’s goals reflect her circumstances and choices.
:::

## Use review rhythms that fit real life

A **weekly review** can take 10 to 20 minutes. Look at the coming week, note fixed commitments, choose a few priorities, and identify a likely obstacle. Check whether you have time for sleep, meals, transportation, support, and responsibilities. The aim is to make the week more navigable, not to grade it.

A **monthly check-in** looks at patterns. Which routines are easier to repeat? Which bills or work tasks need attention? Have your support needs changed? Is a goal still important, or does it need a smaller step? Keep what works and revise what does not.

A **12-month review** is a wider view, not a verdict. If you completed the Chapter 1 recovery-capital rating, compare the ratings and note what changed. Progress may include greater awareness of a need, a stronger relationship, or a clearer plan for getting help. Review Health, Home, Purpose, and Community, then choose what deserves attention in the coming year.

## When a plan falls apart

A return to use, conflict, illness, housing changes, or financial stress can interrupt a plan. Focus first on safety and connection: contact a trusted support person, clinician, or recovery service, and choose one immediate next step. A lapse does not mean treatment has failed; it can signal a need to renew or adjust support [6]. You do not need to wait until you feel ready to ask for help.

If alcohol withdrawal may be a concern, speak with a health care provider; alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous [3]. In the United States, call the free SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357 for treatment referrals [2]. In a crisis, call or text 988; in an emergency, call 911 [1]. These contacts do not replace medical care when it is needed.

## Celebrate progress without demanding perfection

Notice effort as well as outcomes: showing up, asking for help, returning to a routine, or making a plan more realistic. Mark progress in a meaningful way, such as sharing it with someone supportive, taking time for a valued activity, or noting what you learned. No milestone proves recovery is complete, and a difficult week does not erase your work.

- Review weekly for next steps, monthly for patterns, and at 12 months for changes in recovery capital and the four dimensions of recovery.
- If plans fall apart, reconnect with support, attend to safety, and adjust the plan. Progress does not require perfection.
:::

# Glossary

These plain-language definitions explain how this workbook uses each term. They support planning, not labeling.

| Term | Meaning in this workbook |
|---|---|
| Budget | A revisable written plan for income and spending over a chosen period. |
| Cash-flow calendar | A calendar of expected income and bill or cost due dates that makes timing visible. |
| Credit report | A record of credit-file information, distinct from a credit score; free reports do not include a score [13]. |
| Debt inventory | A private list of debts, known balances, due dates, and follow-up questions. |
| Emergency contact | A person or service you may contact for urgent help or support. |
| Habit | A repeated action that can become routine; a missed day is not failure. |
| Habit stacking | Pairing a small action with an existing routine, such as reviewing a plan after breakfast. This is a planning idea, not a guarantee. |
| HALT check | A pause to notice basic needs and emotional strain, including need for food, rest, connection, or support. |
| High-risk situation | A situation in which following your recovery plan may be harder; naming it can help you plan support or coping responses [9]. |
| Implementation intention | A specific if-then plan stating when, where, and how you will act. Research summarized in the fact sheet found these plans can support goal attainment beyond goal intentions alone [10]. |
| Lapse | A return to use after a period of not using. It may signal a need to renew or adjust support; it does not mean failure [6]. |
| Mentor | Someone who offers experience, encouragement, or practical guidance; not a substitute for needed clinical care. |
| Mutual help | Support through shared experience and group or fellowship participation; approaches differ. |
| Peer support | Support from people with relevant lived experience, which can be part of a broader recovery plan [4]. |
| Priority | A task or value chosen for attention during a period. A short list can make a week easier to approach. |
| Recovery capital | Personal, social, community, and cultural resources that support recovery, such as health, relationships, role models, and community connections [8]. |
| Recovery commitment | An action or support contact you choose to include in your recovery plan. |
| Recovery-ready workplace | A workplace with policies supporting employment, help-seeking, and access to treatment and mutual aid [15]. |
| Reference | Someone who agrees to discuss your work, skills, or character when an employer asks. |
| Savings goal | An amount to set aside for a chosen purpose over a suitable time frame. |
| Self-efficacy | Belief in your ability to carry out actions for a specific outcome; it can influence goal-setting and persistence [11]. |
| Sinking fund | Money set aside over time for a known future expense; amount and timing depend on your plan and resources. |
| Support network | People and services you choose for practical, social, or recovery support; different needs may call for different supports. |
| Values | Qualities or principles that guide choices, such as honesty, creativity, care, or independence. You decide which fit your life. |
| WOTC | Work Opportunity Tax Credit, a federal tax credit for employers hiring people certified as members of specified targeted groups. State workforce agencies handle certification [16]. |
| Weekly review | A brief look at what happened, what helped, and what to adjust in the coming week. |
