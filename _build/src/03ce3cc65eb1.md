# How to Use This Book

Health coaching sits at an unusual intersection. It draws on behavioral science and public health, yet most health coaches work as independent small-business owners who must find clients, set prices, sign contracts, and keep records. Many talented coaches struggle not because they lack coaching skill but because they lack a clear model of the business, or because they drift outside their scope and expose themselves and their clients to risk. This book addresses both halves of the work: how to coach well within professional boundaries, and how to build a sustainable practice around that coaching.

The book is written for people who are starting or formalizing a health or wellness coaching practice, including certified coaches, fitness and wellness professionals adding coaching, and licensed professionals who want to understand the coaching model. It assumes no business training. Concepts are defined in plain language and turned into step-by-step methods.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this book, you should be able to:

- Define health coaching using the research literature and distinguish it from clinical care.
- Explain the scope of practice that professional standards set for coaches and build a referral process.
- Apply core behavior-change models in sessions and program design.
- Choose a focused client group and design an offer with a clear outcome process.
- Price services based on capacity, costs, and value rather than guesswork.
- Set up contracts, payments, and records responsibly.
- Market honestly under FTC standards for health claims and testimonials.
- Build client acquisition channels, run consistent sessions, measure service quality, and scale through groups and systems.

## How each chapter works

Each chapter opens with learning objectives and closes with a summary and review questions. Inside, you will find definitions, a step-by-step framework, at least one table, a worked example, and a checklist or worksheet. Worked examples marked "illustrative" use invented coaches, clients, and numbers to show how a method works. Case studies describe real, publicly documented organizations, with sources in the References. Their results are their own, not those of Total Transformation Inc. (TTI), and are included to illustrate principles.

Chapters 1 through 3 build the professional foundation: what coaching is, where its boundaries lie, and how behavior change works. Chapters 4 through 7 cover the business design: offer, pricing, setup, and privacy. Chapters 8 and 9 address marketing and client acquisition. Chapters 10 through 12 cover delivery, measurement, and growth. The appendices provide intake and agreement templates, a referral map, a pricing calculator, and a 90-day launch plan.

Laws on professional practice, privacy, and consumer protection vary by state; consult an attorney and the relevant licensing boards before relying on any approach. No income, client, or health results are promised.

# Chapter 1: What Health Coaching Is, and What It Is Not

:::objectives
- Define health and wellness coaching using the research literature.
- Distinguish coaching from education, therapy, and clinical care.
- Describe the core elements of a coaching relationship.
- Explain why clear definitions protect clients and coaches.
:::

The phrase "health coach" is used loosely. It appears on the business cards of nurses, personal trainers, nutrition enthusiasts, and influencers selling supplements. That looseness creates confusion for clients and risk for coaches. A professional practice starts with a precise definition.

## A research-based definition

In 2013, Ruth Wolever and colleagues published a systematic review of the medical literature on health and wellness coaching, including 284 full-text articles, to find a consensus definition [1]. They found that the literature described coaching as a process that was fully or partially patient-centered (86 percent of articles), included patient-determined goals (71 percent), used self-discovery and active learning rather than passive advice (63 percent), encouraged accountability for behaviors (86 percent), and provided some education along with coaching (91 percent) [1]. Most articles (78 percent) described coaching as happening within a consistent, ongoing relationship with a coach trained in behavior change, communication, and motivational skills [1].

From these findings the authors described health and wellness coaching as a patient-centered approach in which clients at least partially determine their goals, use self-discovery and active learning together with content education, and self-monitor behaviors to increase accountability, all within an interpersonal relationship with a trained coach [1].

## What coaching is not

| Role | Primary activity | Who directs goals | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Health coach | Facilitates self-directed behavior change | Client | Helps a client plan and track walking three days a week |
| Health educator | Teaches information and skills | Educator, often by curriculum | Teaches a class on reading food labels |
| Physician or nurse practitioner | Diagnoses, treats, prescribes | Clinician, with patient input | Diagnoses prediabetes; prescribes medication |
| Registered dietitian | Provides medical nutrition therapy and meal plans | Dietitian, with client input | Writes a meal plan for kidney disease |
| Licensed therapist | Treats mental health conditions | Therapist and client | Treats depression with psychotherapy |

The distinction matters because coaches who drift into diagnosing, prescribing, or treating may be practicing a licensed profession without a license, and because clients may delay necessary care if they believe a coach is managing a medical problem.

## Core elements of a coaching relationship

A coaching relationship has four elements: a partnership in which the client is the expert on their own life; client-chosen goals connected to what matters to them; a structured process of planning, action, and reflection; and accountability through regular check-ins and self-monitoring. Coaches may share evidence-based information from recognized authorities, but information supports the process rather than replacing it.

## What the evidence says, and does not say

Wolever and colleagues noted that health and wellness coaching had been inconsistently defined and had shown mixed results, and that a clear definition was needed so that researchers could assess its effectiveness more accurately [1]. This is an important point for a new coach. Some structured lifestyle programs that include coaching have strong evidence behind them (Chapter 4), but that evidence belongs to specific programs, populations, and protocols. It does not automatically transfer to every coaching practice. A responsible coach speaks about the research honestly, avoids implying that coaching produces particular health outcomes, and focuses on the process: helping clients clarify goals, design realistic experiments, and follow through.

## Five steps to define your practice

1. Write a one-sentence definition of what you do, using the research elements above.
2. List what you do not do, using the comparison table.
3. Identify any additional credentials you hold and what they permit.
4. Write a short client-facing explanation of coaching for your website and intake.
5. Review the definition with a mentor or attorney familiar with your state.

:::example Illustrative: two descriptions of the same coach
Before: "I help people heal their gut and fix their hormones naturally." After: "I'm a health coach who helps busy professionals build sustainable habits around sleep, movement, and eating, working alongside your healthcare team. I don't diagnose or treat conditions; I help you follow through on the changes you choose." The second description is accurate, sets expectations, and avoids implied medical claims.
:::

:::checklist Practice definition
- My definition includes client-led goals, self-discovery, and accountability.
- I have written what I do not do.
- My website does not imply diagnosis or treatment.
- My intake explains the coaching role in plain language.
- I know which additional credentials, if any, change my scope.
:::

:::summary
Research describes health coaching as a client-centered, goal-directed, accountability-based process within an ongoing relationship. Coaching differs from education, therapy, and clinical care. A precise definition protects clients from confusion and coaches from risk.
:::

# Chapter 2: Scope of Practice, Credentials, and Referral

:::objectives
- Explain the scope of practice described by the National Board for Health & Wellness Coaching.
- Understand what national board certification is and is not.
- Recognize red flags that require referral.
- Build a referral network and process.
:::

Scope of practice is the boundary of activities a professional is trained and permitted to perform. For licensed professions, scope is set by state law. Health coaching is not a licensed profession in most states, so the most widely cited standard comes from the profession itself.

## The NBHWC scope of practice

The National Board for Health & Wellness Coaching (NBHWC) describes the coach's role as "accountability partner, not director," supporting clients to achieve self-directed goals consistent with their vision for health and informed by any treatment plans from their healthcare team [2]. Coaches may offer evidence-based resources or information from nationally recognized authorities [2]. On their own, however, coaches do not diagnose, interpret medical data, prescribe or de-prescribe, recommend supplements, provide nutrition consultation or create meal plans, provide exercise prescription or instruction, consult and advise, or provide psychological therapeutic interventions or treatment [2]. Coaches who hold additional credentials, such as a nursing license, may work within that credential's scope, but should disclose the role to the client [2].

## National board certification

NBHWC offers the National Board Certified Health & Wellness Coach (NBC-HWC) credential. To be eligible for the board exam, applicants must complete an NBHWC-approved training program, log 50 qualifying coaching sessions, and hold an associate's degree or higher or 4,000 hours of work experience in any field [3]. At the time of writing, NBHWC listed a $100 application fee and a $400 exam fee [3]. Certification is voluntary in most settings; it is a professional credential, not a license to practice medicine or any other licensed profession.

| Credential type | Example | Legal effect |
|---|---|---|
| License | Registered nurse, registered dietitian (in many states), psychologist | Permits regulated activities under state law |
| Board certification | NBC-HWC | Demonstrates training and competence; does not license clinical practice |
| Training certificate | Program completion certificate | Shows coursework completed |

## Red flags that require referral

Coaches should recognize warning signs and refer promptly. Examples include chest pain, fainting, sudden severe symptoms, signs of disordered eating, thoughts of self-harm, requests for meal plans or supplement recommendations, questions about lab results or medications, and trauma that surfaces in sessions. For any emergency, direct the client to call 911 or, for mental health crises, the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.

## Building a referral network

A referral network includes primary care clinicians, registered dietitians, licensed mental health professionals, physical therapists, and community resources. Build relationships before you need them: introduce yourself, explain your scope, and ask how they prefer to receive referrals. A respectful network also becomes a source of client referrals over time.

## Six steps to a scope and referral system

1. Read the NBHWC scope of practice and your state's rules for related licensed professions.
2. Write a scope statement for your client agreement.
3. Add health-screening questions to your intake form.
4. List red flags and the action for each.
5. Build a referral list of at least five professionals and resources.
6. Document every referral in the client record.

:::example Illustrative: handling an out-of-scope request
A client asks coach Maya, "Can you tell me what my cholesterol numbers mean and what supplements to take?" Maya responds: "Interpreting lab results and recommending supplements are outside my role as a coach. Your doctor or a registered dietitian can help with that. What I can do is help you prepare questions for that appointment and then support whatever plan you decide on." She notes the referral in the client file and follows up at the next session.
:::

:::summary
NBHWC describes coaches as accountability partners who do not diagnose, prescribe, create meal plans, or provide therapy. Board certification demonstrates competence but is not a clinical license. Recognize red flags, refer promptly, and build a professional referral network.

# Chapter 3: The Science of Behavior Change

:::objectives
- Apply the stages-of-change model to client readiness.
- Use the COM-B model to diagnose barriers to behavior.
- Practice coaching techniques that support autonomy.
- Design small, measurable behavior experiments.
:::

Coaching is applied behavioral science. Clients rarely lack information; most know that sleep, movement, and vegetables help. The challenge is translating intention into consistent action. Two well-established models help coaches understand where a client is and what might help.

## Stages of change

Psychologists James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente studied how people quit smoking, with and without professional help, and described change as moving through stages rather than happening in a single decision [4]. Their transtheoretical model is commonly summarized in the stages shown below. Matching coaching approaches to stage avoids pushing action plans on clients who are still deciding whether to change.

| Stage | Client mindset | Coaching approach |
|---|---|---|
| Precontemplation | Not considering change | Explore values; respect autonomy; no pressure |
| Contemplation | Considering, ambivalent | Explore pros and cons; strengthen reasons |
| Preparation | Planning to act soon | Build specific, small plans |
| Action | Actively changing | Problem-solve; track; celebrate progress |
| Maintenance | Sustaining change | Plan for setbacks; reinforce identity |

## The COM-B model

In 2011, Susan Michie and colleagues proposed the "behaviour change wheel," centered on the COM-B model: behavior occurs when a person has the capability, opportunity, and motivation to perform it [5]. Capability can be physical or psychological (knowledge, skill); opportunity can be physical (time, environment, resources) or social (norms, support); motivation can be reflective (plans, beliefs) or automatic (habits, emotions) [5]. When a client is not following through, COM-B helps the coach and client identify which component is missing rather than assuming a lack of willpower.

## Autonomy-supportive techniques

Coaching techniques that support autonomy include open questions ("What would make this week a success for you?"), reflective listening (restating the client's meaning), affirmations of effort and strengths, and summaries that connect behavior to the client's stated values. These techniques draw on motivational interviewing, which the coaching literature frequently pairs with health coaching [1]. Avoid lecturing, persuading, or prescribing; clients are more likely to act on plans they design.

## How long habits take

Clients often ask how long it takes to form a habit, and popular culture offers a confident answer of 21 days. Research is less tidy. In a 2010 study, Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London followed 96 volunteers who chose a new eating, drinking, or activity behavior to perform daily in the same context, and modeled how automatic the behavior felt over 12 weeks [20]. The median time to reach a plateau of automaticity was 66 days, with a range of 18 to 254 days, and missing a single opportunity did not materially affect the habit-formation process [20]. For coaches, the practical lessons are to set realistic expectations, to attach new behaviors to consistent cues, and to reassure clients that an occasional missed day is not failure.

## Behavior experiments

Rather than setting large goals, help clients design small experiments: a specific behavior, a time and place, a way to track it, and a date to review. Treat results as data, not success or failure.

## Six steps to a behavior plan

1. Ask the client to describe what matters most and why.
2. Assess the stage of change for the target behavior.
3. Use COM-B to identify the main barrier.
4. Co-design a small experiment addressing that barrier.
5. Agree on tracking and a review date.
6. Review results, reflect, and adjust.

:::example Illustrative: a COM-B diagnosis
A hypothetical client, Luis, wants to walk after dinner but rarely does. Capability is fine; he enjoys walking. Motivation is strong; he wants energy for his kids. The barrier is opportunity: dishes and homework fill the evening. He and his coach design an experiment: a 15-minute family walk right after dinner on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, with dishes afterward. He tracks walks on a paper calendar. After two weeks he has walked five of six planned days.
:::

Behavior change unfolds in stages, and behavior depends on capability, opportunity, and motivation. Coaches support autonomy through open questions, reflection, and client-designed experiments, treating results as data for the next step.

# Chapter 4: Choosing a Niche and Designing Your Offer

:::objectives
- Choose a focused client group based on need, access, and fit.
- Define the problem your coaching helps clients work on.
- Design an offer with a clear process, duration, and deliverables.
- Learn from a public program that uses group-based coaching at scale.
:::

New coaches often try to serve everyone interested in "health." The result is vague marketing and difficulty getting referrals. A focused client group, sometimes called a niche, makes it easier to understand clients, explain your value, and build partnerships.

## Choosing a client group

Evaluate potential client groups on four criteria: a recognizable shared situation; a behavior-change challenge coaching can address within scope; your access to those clients through networks or partners; and your genuine interest and relevant experience.

| Candidate group | Shared situation | In scope? | Access | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New parents returning to work | Disrupted sleep, routines | Yes, habits and routines | Parent groups, employers | Coach is a parent |
| Shift workers at local hospitals | Irregular schedules | Yes, routines and planning | Hospital wellness office | Coach worked in healthcare |
| People seeking weight-loss drugs | Medical decision | Partly; medical aspects out of scope | Clinics | Requires care with scope |
| Retirees starting exercise | New routines, social needs | Yes, with clearance for exercise | Senior centers | Coach enjoys group work |

## Defining the problem and the process

Describe the problem in the client's words and the process you use, not a guaranteed result. "Busy parents who want to build consistent movement and sleep routines, through a 12-week coaching program with biweekly sessions and weekly check-ins" is clear and honest. Avoid promising specific health outcomes such as weight loss amounts or disease reversal.

:::case The National Diabetes Prevention Program
The CDC-led National Diabetes Prevention Program (National DPP) offers a structured lifestyle change program for adults with prediabetes, led by trained lifestyle coaches using a CDC-approved curriculum with group support over a year [6][7]. The program is based on the Diabetes Prevention Program research study, a randomized controlled trial in which participants who completed the lifestyle program reduced their chances of developing type 2 diabetes by 58 percent compared with placebo (71 percent for those 60 and older), about twice the reduction seen with the drug metformin (31 percent) [7]. The CDC recognizes only programs that use trained coaches and an approved curriculum [7]. The program illustrates principles that transfer to independent practice: a clearly defined client group, a structured process with a set duration, trained coaches, peer support, and outcomes measured by research rather than promised in marketing. The National DPP's results are its own and come from a specific, research-based program, not from health coaching in general or from TTI.
:::

## Testing demand before building

Before investing in a website, branded materials, or a long curriculum, test whether your chosen group wants what you plan to offer. Describe the program in one paragraph and share it with the people you interviewed, referral partners, and your network. Ask whether they would join, what would make it more useful, and what price feels reasonable. Offer a small pilot cohort at a reduced founding price in exchange for detailed feedback. If interest is weak, revise the offer or the client group rather than pushing harder on marketing.

## Offer structure

An offer has a duration (for example, 12 weeks), a format (individual, group, or hybrid), a cadence (sessions and check-ins), deliverables (session notes, trackers, resources), and boundaries (response times, rescheduling). Programs with a defined start and end help clients commit and help coaches plan capacity.

## Six steps to design an offer

1. Choose one client group using the four criteria.
2. Interview at least eight people in that group about their challenges.
3. Write the problem statement in their words.
4. Define duration, format, cadence, and deliverables.
5. Write scope boundaries into the offer description.
6. Pilot with three to five clients and refine.

:::example Illustrative: an offer description
"Steady Shift is a 12-week coaching program for hospital shift workers who want more consistent sleep, meals, and movement despite rotating schedules. You'll meet with your coach every two weeks by video, check in weekly by message, and use a simple shift-planning tracker. Coaching complements, and does not replace, care from your healthcare providers." The description names the group, process, and boundaries without promising health outcomes.
:::

# Chapter 5: Pricing, Packages, and Capacity

:::objectives
- Calculate the true cost and capacity of a coaching practice.
- Set prices using costs, capacity, market context, and value.
- Structure packages that support client commitment.
- Avoid common pricing mistakes.
:::

Pricing is one of the most anxious decisions for new coaches. Many set prices by copying others or by what feels comfortable, then discover that the numbers cannot support a sustainable business. A disciplined approach starts with capacity and costs, then considers the market and the value clients perceive.

## Capacity first

A coach's capacity is limited by hours, energy, and the administrative work around each client. A full-time coach rarely delivers more than 20 to 25 client sessions a week sustainably, because preparation, notes, messages, marketing, and administration consume the remaining time.

| Item | Illustrative assumption |
|---|---|
| Working weeks per year | 46 (allowing vacation, illness, training) |
| Client-facing hours per week | 20 |
| Annual client-facing hours | 920 |
| Annual business expenses | $9,000 (software, insurance, training, marketing) |
| Desired annual pay before taxes | $60,000 |
| Required revenue | $69,000 |
| Required revenue per client-facing hour | about $75 |

These figures are illustrative, not a benchmark. Your numbers will differ by market, experience, and model. The point is that hourly rates must cover non-billable time and expenses.

## Value and the client's perspective

Alex Hormozi's book $100M Offers proposes a "value equation" in which perceived value rises with the dream outcome and the perceived likelihood of achieving it, and falls with the time delay and the effort and sacrifice required [8]. For coaches, the honest application is not to promise bigger outcomes but to reduce friction: make the process simpler, give clients early wins, and remove effort through good tools and support. Value grows when coaching feels achievable.

## Package structures

| Structure | Description | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single sessions | Pay per session | Low commitment | Weak follow-through; unpredictable income |
| Fixed program | E.g., 12 weeks, paid upfront or in installments | Clear commitment; predictable | Requires clear refund terms |
| Ongoing membership | Monthly fee for continued support | Recurring revenue | Must clearly disclose renewal and cancellation |
| Group program | Cohort meets together | Lower price per client; peer support | Requires enrollment minimums |

Whatever structure you choose, state the price, payment schedule, refund policy, and cancellation terms in writing before the client pays. For recurring charges, obtain clear consent and make cancellation easy; state laws on automatic renewal vary.

## Common pricing mistakes

Common mistakes include pricing below cost, offering unlimited messaging that consumes evenings, discounting heavily to fill a schedule, and changing prices without notice. Raise prices for new clients first and give existing clients advance notice.

## Six steps to set prices

1. Estimate annual client-facing hours realistically.
2. List annual business expenses.
3. Set a desired annual pay and calculate required revenue per hour.
4. Research local and online price ranges for comparable offers.
5. Design packages that support commitment and reduce client effort.
6. Write payment, refund, and cancellation terms.

:::example Illustrative: from hourly rate to package
Using the illustrative figures above, coach Priya needs about $75 per client-facing hour. Her 12-week program includes six 50-minute sessions, weekly check-ins (about 10 minutes each), and preparation and notes (about 15 minutes per session), totaling roughly 9.5 hours per client. At $75 per hour, the program's cost-based floor is about $710. After reviewing comparable programs, she prices it at $780, payable upfront or in three installments of $270.
:::

# Chapter 6: Setting Up the Business

:::objectives
- Choose an appropriate business structure and register the business.
- Prepare a client agreement covering scope, payment, and boundaries.
- Consider insurance and payment processing risks.
- Learn from a coaching company's early cash-flow crisis.
:::

A coaching practice is a business, with the same legal and financial obligations as any other. Setting it up correctly protects personal assets, clarifies expectations with clients, and keeps cash flowing.

## Business structure and registration

The U.S. Small Business Administration explains that the business structure you choose affects day-to-day operations, taxes, and how much of your personal assets are at risk; common options include sole proprietorships, partnerships, limited liability companies (LLCs), and corporations [9]. Many solo coaches start as sole proprietors or single-member LLCs. Register your business name as required by your state and locality, obtain any required local business license, and open a separate business bank account. A tax professional can advise on structure and estimated taxes.

| Structure | Personal liability protection | Complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | None | Low |
| Single-member LLC | Generally protects personal assets from business debts | Moderate |
| S corporation election | Same as LLC or corporation; may change tax treatment | Higher |

## The client agreement

Every client should sign a written agreement before coaching begins. It should cover the nature of coaching and its scope; that coaching does not replace medical or mental health care; the client's responsibility to consult healthcare providers; fees, payment schedule, refunds, and cancellations; session scheduling and rescheduling; communication boundaries and response times; confidentiality and its limits; how records are kept; and termination. Have an attorney review your template.

## Taxes and bookkeeping

Self-employed coaches are generally responsible for paying income tax and self-employment tax, and the IRS explains that self-employed individuals may need to make quarterly estimated tax payments [21]. Set aside a percentage of each payment in a separate savings account for taxes, keep receipts for business expenses, and use simple bookkeeping software or a spreadsheet updated weekly. A tax professional can help you estimate payments and identify deductible expenses for your situation.

## Insurance

Professional liability insurance (sometimes called errors and omissions) covers claims arising from your professional services; general liability covers injuries or property damage, which matters if you see clients in person. Some coaching certification bodies and associations offer access to group policies. Read exclusions carefully, particularly for activities outside coaching scope.

:::case Precision Nutrition's payment processor hold
Precision Nutrition, co-founded by John Berardi and Phil Caravaggio, grew from a free health and fitness newsletter into a large nutrition coaching and certification company [10][16]. In an interview with NPE Fitness, Berardi described launching the company's first paid product to about 30,000 newsletter subscribers, generating about $140,000 in sales in the first week [10]. The company's merchant services provider then withheld its funds for six months because of the sudden increase in sales; Berardi said the company fulfilled thousands of orders without receiving that money, and that he sold his apartment and borrowed from friends and family to get through the period [10]. The episode illustrates a risk that health and coaching businesses face: payment processors may hold funds when sales patterns change suddenly. Precision Nutrition's experience is its own, not TTI's. Practical lessons for small practices include telling your processor in advance about planned launches, keeping a cash reserve, using clear refund policies to reduce chargebacks, and not relying on a single payment channel.
:::

## Payments and cash reserves

Choose a payment processor that supports your package structures and recurring billing, understand its rules on holds and chargebacks, and keep receipts and signed agreements to respond to disputes. Aim to keep a reserve of several months of expenses before leaving other income.

## Six steps to set up the business

1. Choose a structure with input from a tax professional.
2. Register your business name and obtain local licenses.
3. Open a separate business bank account.
4. Draft a client agreement and have it reviewed.
5. Obtain appropriate insurance.
6. Set up payment processing and build a cash reserve.

:::example Illustrative: a setup timeline
Coach Darnell forms a single-member LLC in week 1, registers with his city in week 2, opens a business account and payment processor in week 3, buys professional liability insurance in week 4, and has an attorney review his client agreement in week 5 for a flat fee. Before his first group program launch, he emails his payment processor to explain the expected sales volume.
:::

:::checklist Business setup
- Business structure chosen and registered.
- Local licenses obtained as required.
- Separate business bank account open.
- Client agreement reviewed by an attorney.
- Professional liability insurance in place.
- Payment processor rules understood; cash reserve started.
:::

:::summary
Choose a structure, register properly, and separate business finances. Use a written client agreement covering scope, payment, and boundaries. Insure the practice, and plan for payment holds and chargebacks, as Precision Nutrition's early experience illustrates.
:::

# Chapter 7: Client Privacy and Records

:::objectives
- Understand when HIPAA applies and when it does not.
- Apply privacy principles regardless of legal requirements.
- Decide what to record and how to store it securely.
- Write a clear privacy notice for clients.
:::

Clients share sensitive information with coaches: health conditions, weight, habits, emotions, family situations. Protecting that information is an ethical obligation and, in some situations, a legal one.

## When HIPAA applies

The HIPAA Rules apply to covered entities and their business associates [11]. Covered entities are health plans, health care clearinghouses, and health care providers that transmit health information electronically in connection with certain standard transactions, such as insurance claims [11]. If an entity is not a covered entity or business associate, it does not have to comply with the HIPAA Rules [11]. Many independent coaches who are paid directly by clients are therefore not covered by HIPAA. However, a coach who works under contract for a covered entity, such as a clinic or health plan, may be a business associate and must follow contractual and regulatory requirements [11]. State privacy and consumer protection laws may also apply regardless of HIPAA.

## Privacy principles for every coach

| Principle | Practice |
|---|---|
| Collect the minimum | Ask only for information needed to coach |
| Secure storage | Use password-protected, encrypted tools with two-factor authentication |
| Limit access | Only you, or staff who need it, can view records |
| Consent for sharing | Share information with others only with written client consent, except where law requires |
| Retention and deletion | Set how long you keep records and delete securely afterward |
| Transparency | Tell clients what you collect, why, and how it is stored |

## What to record

Keep session notes brief and factual: date, topics discussed, goals and experiments agreed, referrals made, and follow-up items. Avoid recording diagnoses or medical details beyond what clients share as context. Document scope-related conversations and referrals carefully; they show that you practiced responsibly.

## Six steps to a privacy system

1. Determine whether you are a covered entity or business associate in any role.
2. Choose secure tools for notes, messaging, and video.
3. Revise intake forms to collect only necessary information.
4. Write a short privacy notice for clients.
5. Set a retention period and deletion process.
6. Review access and security settings quarterly.

:::example Illustrative: a minimal session note
"3/12 — Session 4 (50 min). Reviewed walking experiment: 5 of 6 planned walks. Client identified late meetings as a barrier on Thursdays. New experiment: morning walk on Thursdays. Client plans to ask her doctor about knee discomfort at next appointment (referral discussed). Next session 3/26." The note is factual, brief, and documents the referral.
:::

:::checklist Privacy practices
- I know whether HIPAA applies to any of my roles.
- My tools use encryption and two-factor authentication.
- My intake asks only for necessary information.
- Clients receive a written privacy notice.
- I have a records retention and deletion policy.
- I obtain written consent before sharing client information.
:::

:::summary
HIPAA applies to covered entities and business associates, which many independent coaches are not, but coaches working for clinics or health plans may be. Regardless, collect minimal information, store it securely, share only with consent, and tell clients how their information is handled.
:::

# Chapter 8: Honest Marketing and Health Claims

:::objectives
- Apply FTC standards for truthful, substantiated health claims.
- Use testimonials and before-and-after content responsibly.
- Avoid the FTC-prohibited review practices.
- Write marketing that is persuasive and accurate.
:::

Health marketing is one of the most scrutinized areas of advertising. The temptation to promise dramatic transformations is strong, and the consequences of misleading claims, for clients and for coaches, are serious.

## The FTC standard for health claims

The FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance explains that advertising must be truthful and not misleading, and that before an ad runs, advertisers must have adequate substantiation for all objective claims, express or implied [12]. For health-related claims, the FTC generally requires competent and reliable scientific evidence [12]. The guidance advises marketers to consider what express and implied messages consumers are likely to take from an ad, to qualify claims carefully, and to make sure supporting evidence is relevant to the specific claim [12]. Although the guidance focuses on health products, its principles apply to anyone making health-related claims, and coaching marketing frequently implies health outcomes.

## Testimonials and typical results

The FTC's Endorsement Guides FAQ explains that testimonials describing specific results will generally be understood to convey that those results are typical, unless the advertiser clearly discloses what consumers can generally expect [13]. A testimonial saying "I lost 40 pounds in 12 weeks" implies that other clients can expect similar results. If that is not typical, the testimonial is likely misleading even with a "results not typical" disclaimer [13]. Material connections, such as free coaching in exchange for a testimonial, must be disclosed [13].

## Fake reviews

In August 2024, the FTC announced a final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials, including reviews by people who do not exist or did not have actual experience with the business, AI-generated fake reviews, and buying positive reviews; the rule allows civil penalties for knowing violations [14].

| Risky claim | Why it is risky | Better alternative |
|---|---|---|
| "Reverse your diabetes in 90 days" | Disease claim; outside scope; requires strong evidence | "Support for building habits alongside your care team" |
| "Lose 30 pounds guaranteed" | Specific outcome; implies typical result | "A 12-week program for consistent eating and movement habits" |
| "Detox your body" | Vague health claim without evidence | Describe the actual behaviors coached |
| Client photo "down 40 lbs!" | Implies typical results | Share process-focused stories with clear context |
| "Doctor recommended" | Requires substantiation of the endorsement | Name a specific endorsement only with permission and accuracy |

## Process-focused marketing

Honest marketing describes who you help, the process you use, what clients do in the program, and what working with you feels like. Stories can focus on behaviors clients built ("Jordan now walks four mornings a week and plans meals on Sundays") rather than medical outcomes, with permission and appropriate context.

## Six steps to compliant marketing

1. List every claim on your website, social profiles, and materials.
2. Mark claims that imply health outcomes or disease effects.
3. Remove or rewrite claims you cannot substantiate with appropriate evidence.
4. Review testimonials for implied typical results and material connections.
5. Collect reviews honestly from all clients.
6. Repeat the audit quarterly.

:::example Illustrative: rewriting a sales page headline
Before: "Melt belly fat and balance your hormones in 8 weeks." After: "An 8-week coaching program for women in midlife who want steadier routines around food, movement, and sleep, designed to complement care from your doctor." The rewrite names the audience and process and removes unsubstantiated physiological claims.
:::

:::checklist Marketing compliance
- No disease, cure, or treatment claims.
- No guaranteed weight or health outcomes.
- Testimonials reflect typical experience or are clearly qualified.
- Material connections are disclosed.
- Reviews are genuine and not purchased or incentivized for positivity.
- Claims are reviewed quarterly.
:::

:::summary
The FTC requires truthful, substantiated advertising and generally competent and reliable scientific evidence for health claims. Testimonials imply typical results, material connections must be disclosed, and fake reviews are banned. Market the process and the experience, not guaranteed health outcomes.
:::

# Chapter 9: Finding Clients Ethically

:::objectives
- Build a client acquisition plan around referrals, partnerships, and content.
- Choose online channels based on where clients spend time.
- Run consultation calls that help clients decide without pressure.
- Track where clients come from.
:::

Most coaching practices grow through trust: personal networks, professional referrals, and content that demonstrates how the coach thinks. Paid advertising can help later, but it rarely replaces relationships, and health ads face additional platform and legal scrutiny.

## Four acquisition channels

| Channel | How it works | Time to results | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal network | Tell people what you do and whom you help | Fast | Ask for introductions, not purchases |
| Professional referrals | Clinicians, trainers, therapists refer clients | Moderate | Build two-way relationships; respect scope |
| Partnerships | Employers, gyms, community groups host programs | Moderate | Group workshops introduce your approach |
| Content | Educational posts, newsletters, talks | Slow, compounding | Share evidence-based information from recognized authorities |

## Choosing online platforms

Choose platforms based on where your client group spends time. Pew Research Center's 2025 survey of U.S. adults found that 84 percent used YouTube, 71 percent Facebook, 50 percent Instagram, and 37 percent TikTok, with usage varying substantially by age [15]. A coach serving retirees might focus on Facebook and local events; one serving young professionals might favor Instagram or LinkedIn. Consistency on one or two platforms usually beats sporadic activity everywhere.

## Content that builds trust

Useful content answers common questions, explains behavior-change ideas simply, shares practical tools, and models your coaching style. Cite recognized authorities such as the CDC or NIH when sharing health information, avoid medical advice, and keep claims within scope (Chapter 8).

## Working with employers and organizations

Employers, unions, schools, and community organizations sometimes hire coaches to deliver workshops or programs. These engagements can introduce many potential clients at once, but they bring additional responsibilities. Clarify in writing who the client is (the organization or the individual), what information, if any, will be reported back to the organization, and how participant privacy will be protected. Report only aggregated, non-identifying participation data unless participants consent otherwise. If the organization is a health plan or health care provider, determine whether you will be acting as a HIPAA business associate (Chapter 7) [11].

## The consultation call

A consultation call helps a prospective client decide whether coaching is right for them. Structure it as a mini coaching conversation: ask about their goals and what they have tried, explain how your program works, check for scope concerns, answer questions, and invite them to decide. Avoid high-pressure tactics, false scarcity, or pushing people who are not ready. If someone needs clinical care first, say so.

## Six steps to a client acquisition plan

1. List 50 people in your network and tell them clearly whom you help.
2. Meet three potential referral partners per month.
3. Offer one free workshop per quarter through a partner organization.
4. Publish useful content weekly on one or two platforms.
5. Run consultation calls with a consistent structure.
6. Ask every new client how they heard about you, and track results.

:::example Illustrative: a partnership workshop
Coach Elena offers a free 45-minute lunchtime workshop, "Building Routines Around Shift Work," for a local hospital's employee wellness program. Twenty-two staff attend; nine request a follow-up resource; four book consultations; two enroll in her program over the next month. She records these numbers to compare with other channels. The figures are illustrative, not typical results.
:::

# Chapter 10: Running Sessions and Programs

:::objectives
- Design an intake process that screens for scope and sets expectations.
- Structure coaching sessions consistently.
- Support clients between sessions without unlimited availability.
- Close programs with reflection and maintenance plans.
:::

Consistent delivery is what turns a coaching offer into a reliable service. A clear structure helps clients know what to expect, helps coaches stay within scope, and makes the practice easier to manage as it grows.

## Intake

Intake includes the signed client agreement, a health-screening questionnaire (including whether the client is under medical care and whether their provider is aware of planned changes such as new exercise), the privacy notice, and a short values and goals questionnaire. Review intake responses before the first session and refer when red flags appear (Chapter 2).

## A session structure

| Segment | Time (50-min session) | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Check-in | 5 min | Connection; how the client is doing |
| Review | 10 min | Results of last experiment; what was learned |
| Focus | 20 min | Explore the client's chosen topic; COM-B barriers |
| Plan | 10 min | Co-design the next experiment; tracking |
| Close | 5 min | Summary; confidence check; next session |

A confidence check, asking "On a scale of 0 to 10, how confident are you that you'll do this?", helps adjust plans. If confidence is below 7, make the experiment smaller or address the barrier.

## Between-session support

Between-session support might include a weekly check-in message, a simple tracker, and resources. Set boundaries: response times (for example, within one business day), channels, and what to do in an emergency (call 911 or the client's provider, not the coach).

## When clients struggle

Every client hits weeks where experiments do not happen. Treat these weeks as information. Ask what got in the way, revisit the COM-B diagnosis, and shrink the next experiment if needed. Watch for patterns that suggest something beyond coaching scope, such as persistent low mood or symptoms, and refer when appropriate. If a client repeatedly misses sessions, have an honest, nonjudgmental conversation about whether now is the right time for coaching; pausing a program can be better for the client than continuing without engagement.

## Closing a program

The final sessions should review progress against the client's goals, identify what worked, plan for likely setbacks, and set up maintenance strategies. Offer an optional continuation if appropriate, without pressure.

## Six steps to consistent delivery

1. Build an intake packet with agreement, screening, privacy notice, and goals form.
2. Review intake before the first session.
3. Use a consistent session structure.
4. Set up between-session check-ins with clear boundaries.
5. Document each session briefly.
6. Close each program with a maintenance plan.

:::example Illustrative: the first session
Coach Ben opens with the client's values questionnaire: "You wrote that you want energy to coach your daughter's soccer team. Tell me more." After exploring, the client chooses sleep as a first focus. They identify that late screen time is the barrier and design an experiment: phone charging in the kitchen at 10 p.m. on weeknights. Confidence is 6, so they shrink it to three weeknights. The client rates confidence at 8.
:::

:::checklist Session delivery
- Intake reviewed and scope screened before the first session.
- Each session follows the five-segment structure.
- Every session ends with a client-designed experiment and confidence check.
- Between-session boundaries are written and shared.
- Notes are documented after every session.
- Final sessions include a maintenance plan.
:::

:::summary
Consistent intake, session structure, between-session support, and program closure create a reliable client experience. Confidence checks and small experiments keep plans realistic, and clear boundaries protect both client and coach.
:::

# Chapter 11: Measuring Service Quality and Client Progress

:::objectives
- Distinguish service measures from health outcome promises.
- Track client-defined progress and behavior consistency.
- Collect client feedback systematically.
- Use a simple dashboard to improve the practice.
:::

Measurement helps coaches improve and helps clients see progress. But coaches should be careful about how they measure and talk about health outcomes, both because outcomes depend on many factors outside coaching and because publicizing outcomes creates marketing claims that must be substantiated (Chapter 8).

## What to measure

| Category | Measure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Client behavior | Experiment completion rate | 18 of 24 planned walks completed |
| Client-defined progress | Self-rated progress toward goals (0–10) | Sleep consistency goal: 4 to 7 |
| Engagement | Session attendance; check-in response rate | 92 percent attendance |
| Satisfaction | End-of-program survey; likelihood to recommend | Average 9.1 of 10 |
| Business | Consultation-to-client conversion; retention; referrals | 35 percent conversion |

Clients may choose to track health measures with their healthcare providers, such as weight or blood pressure, but coaches should avoid interpreting medical data (Chapter 2) and should not market aggregated health outcomes without appropriate evidence and disclosures.

## Collecting feedback

Ask brief questions at midpoint and at program end: What has been most helpful? What should change? How likely are you to recommend this program? Would you be willing to share your experience publicly, and if so, may I use it? Act on themes, not single comments.

## A practice dashboard

Review a one-page dashboard monthly: number of active clients, sessions delivered, attendance rate, average experiment completion, satisfaction, consultations and conversions, referrals received, revenue, and hours worked. Over time, trends reveal where the practice needs attention: delivery, marketing, or capacity.

## Six steps to a measurement system

1. Choose five or six measures across behavior, engagement, satisfaction, and business.
2. Build a simple tracker for each client.
3. Run midpoint and end-of-program surveys.
4. Record monthly totals on a dashboard.
5. Review trends quarterly and choose one improvement.
6. Use feedback in marketing only with permission and accurate context.

:::example Illustrative: using feedback to improve
After two cohorts, coach Sana's surveys show high satisfaction but a recurring comment: "The weekly check-in felt like homework." Experiment completion drops in weeks 5 to 8. She shortens the check-in to three quick questions and adds a midpoint group call. In the next cohort, attendance and completion stay steadier through week 8. The figures are illustrative.
:::

Active clients this month: ___
Session attendance rate: ___
Average experiment completion: ___
Average satisfaction score: ___
Consultations and conversions: ___
Referrals received: ___
Revenue and hours worked: ___
One improvement for next quarter: ___
:::

:::summary
Measure what coaching influences: behaviors, client-defined progress, engagement, satisfaction, and business health. Avoid interpreting medical data or marketing health outcomes without substantiation. Use feedback and a monthly dashboard to improve.
:::

# Chapter 12: Growing the Practice Through Groups, Systems, and Time

:::objectives
- Evaluate group programs, partnerships, and digital tools as growth paths.
- Learn from a coaching company that scaled through systems and software.
- Apply time-management principles to protect coaching capacity.
- Create a growth plan that preserves quality and scope.
:::

A one-to-one practice is limited by the coach's hours. Growth requires either raising prices, serving clients in groups, delegating work, or using systems and technology. Each path has trade-offs for quality and client experience.

## Growth paths

| Path | How it scales | Risks |
|---|---|---|
| Group programs | One coach serves several clients at once | Less individual attention; requires facilitation skill |
| Employer or community partnerships | Programs delivered to organizations | Longer sales cycles; contracts and privacy obligations |
| Hiring coaches | More coaching hours | Quality control; management time |
| Digital tools and courses | Content delivered without live time | Lower engagement; must avoid overclaiming |
| Higher prices | More revenue per hour | Requires clear value and demand |

:::case Precision Nutrition's growth through certification and software
According to a 2017 announcement of an investment by BV Investment Partners, Precision Nutrition began in 2005 as a scalable nutrition coaching program, launched a professional certification in 2010, and launched ProCoach in 2016, a software platform that lets health and nutrition coaches deliver behavior-change curricula and manage client data [16]. The announcement stated that its certification and ProCoach had educated and supported more than 45,000 health and nutrition coaches [16]. Co-founder John Berardi has described the company as known for its use of jobs-to-be-done research and as fully remote [17]. The case shows a progression many coaching businesses consider: from delivering coaching directly, to teaching other professionals, to providing systems they can use. Precision Nutrition's experience as a venture-backed company is its own, not TTI's, and an individual coach's practice will look very different. The transferable principle is that documented, repeatable processes make growth possible.
:::

## Protecting your time

Entrepreneur Dan Martell's book Buy Back Your Time argues that business owners should identify low-value, draining tasks and pay others to handle them so they can focus on their highest-value work [18]. In an interview, Martell described a "buyback loop" of auditing how time is spent, transferring tasks to others, and filling the freed time with higher-value work, and suggested estimating a "buyback rate" by dividing annual income by 2,000 hours and then by four, as a guide to which tasks to delegate [19]. For coaches, delegable tasks often include scheduling, bookkeeping, website updates, and social media formatting; coaching itself, scope decisions, and client relationships usually stay with the coach. Martell's results and frameworks are his own, not TTI's.

## Running group programs

Group programs work well when clients share a situation and when the coach can facilitate discussion. Keep groups small enough for participation (often 6 to 12), use a structured curriculum with time for individual planning, set group agreements about confidentiality, and offer brief individual check-ins where needed.

## Six steps to a growth plan

1. Review your dashboard to identify the main constraint: demand, capacity, or price.
2. Choose one growth path that addresses that constraint.
3. Document your coaching process so it can be repeated.
4. Audit your time and delegate low-value tasks.
5. Pilot the growth path with a small group.
6. Measure quality before expanding further.

:::example Illustrative: from one-to-one to groups
Coach Grace has 22 one-to-one clients and a waitlist. Using a time audit, she delegates scheduling and bookkeeping to a part-time assistant. She converts her 12-week program into a group version for eight clients with biweekly group sessions and two individual check-ins, priced lower per client. After one pilot cohort, satisfaction remains high, and she offers both formats.
:::

:::checklist Growth readiness
- My coaching process is documented and repeatable.
- I know my main constraint from dashboard data.
- I have delegated at least two low-value tasks.
- Growth pilots are measured for quality.
- Scope and privacy rules apply to every new format and partnership.
:::

:::summary
Coaching practices grow through groups, partnerships, hiring, digital tools, or pricing, each with trade-offs. Precision Nutrition illustrates growth through documented systems. Martell's buyback approach helps coaches delegate low-value work. Pilot and measure growth before expanding.
:::

# Glossary

**Accountability.** Regular follow-up and self-monitoring that help clients act on commitments.

**Automaticity.** The degree to which a behavior happens with little conscious effort, a key feature of habits [20].

**Behavior experiment.** A small, specific, time-bound behavior a client tries and reviews.

**Business associate.** Under HIPAA, a person or organization that performs certain functions for a covered entity involving protected health information [11].

**COM-B model.** A model holding that behavior depends on capability, opportunity, and motivation [5].

**Confidence check.** A 0–10 rating of how confident a client is that they will carry out a plan.

**Covered entity.** Under HIPAA, a health plan, health care clearinghouse, or health care provider conducting certain electronic transactions [11].

**Material connection.** A relationship between an endorser and a business, such as payment or free services, that must be disclosed [13].

**Motivational interviewing.** A collaborative conversational approach for strengthening a person's own motivation for change.

**NBC-HWC.** National Board Certified Health & Wellness Coach, a credential from NBHWC [3].

**Professional liability insurance.** Insurance covering claims arising from professional services, sometimes called errors and omissions coverage.

**Referral network.** Professionals and resources to whom a coach refers clients for needs outside coaching scope.

**Scope of practice.** The range of activities a professional is trained and permitted to perform [2].

**Self-employment tax.** Tax paid by self-employed individuals for Social Security and Medicare, in addition to income tax [21].

**Stages of change.** The transtheoretical model's description of change as progressing through stages from precontemplation to maintenance [4].

**Substantiation.** Evidence supporting an advertising claim before it is made [12].

**Typical results.** What consumers can generally expect; testimonials are presumed to represent them unless clearly qualified [13].
