# How to Use This Book

This book is for two overlapping groups. The first is people in recovery from substance use or mental health conditions—and their families and allies—who want to start a small, legitimate business. The second is anyone launching an ordinary service business whose customers include recovery-community organizations, such as recovery community centers, mutual-aid meeting hosts, sober event organizers, and nonprofits. Both groups face the same core design questions: what useful service to offer, where its boundaries lie, how to handle sensitive information, what to do when a request falls outside the business, and how to build at a pace that protects the founder's health.

The book's argument is simple. A small business can be valuable to its owner and to the recovery community without providing care. The service can be ordinary—packing supply kits, setting up events, printing, cleaning, bookkeeping, delivery—while the way it is run reflects respect: clear scope, minimal data, safe hand-offs, honest marketing, and a pace the founder can sustain. Businesses that blur the line into counseling, treatment, housing placement, or crisis work without proper authorization create risk for everyone, including the people they intend to help.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this book you should be able to:

- Explain how meaningful work fits into recovery, using public definitions.
- Choose a nonclinical service with a clear buyer and a written scope.
- Recognize activities that are regulated and must be verified with authorities before offering them.
- Address legal setup, records, and common second-chance concerns such as background checks and licensing.
- Interview buyers about their work without asking for personal stories.
- Write an honest, accessible offer and price a small test.
- Collect only the data you need and protect it.
- Use a hand-off script and verified resources when someone needs help the business does not provide.
- Build a personal sustainability plan and business stop rules.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, defines key terms, and presents a step-by-step framework. Every chapter includes a worked example with real numbers, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Several chapters include case studies of real people and organizations drawn from public sources—among them Dave's Killer Bread, Homeboy Industries, Greyston Bakery, and entrepreneur Dan Martell. Their results are their own, not TTI's, and they are presented with their difficulties as well as their successes.

The worked examples follow a fictional founder, Maya Torres, who has been in recovery for four years and is launching an event supply and setup business called Steady Hands. Maya is illustrative only; she is not a TTI customer, and her numbers are not predictions.

:::callout Scope and limits
This book is educational. It is not legal, tax, financial, medical, or clinical advice, and it promises no income, contract, funding, or business outcome. It does not explain how to provide counseling, treatment, peer-support services, recovery housing, or any other clinical or regulated service, and nothing here authorizes you to do so. Requirements differ by state and city; verify them with the appropriate authority before acting. If you or someone you are with is in crisis, call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911 in an emergency. Total Transformation Inc. is a for-profit company and is not a treatment provider.
:::

# Chapter 1: Recovery, Work, and Business

:::objectives
- Describe SAMHSA's working definition of recovery and its four dimensions.
- Explain how work and purpose relate to recovery.
- Weigh the benefits and risks of self-employment compared with employment.
- Decide whether a small business is the right next step now.
:::

Recovery is commonly understood as more than the absence of symptoms. The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), the federal agency that leads public health efforts on behavioral health, defines recovery as "a process of change through which individuals improve their health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and strive to reach their full potential" [1]. SAMHSA identifies four major dimensions that support a life in recovery: health, home (a stable and safe place to live), purpose (meaningful daily activities, such as a job, school, volunteering, family caretaking, or creative endeavors, and the independence, income, and resources to participate in society), and community (relationships and social networks that provide support, friendship, love, and hope) [1].

Work touches all four. A job or business can provide income for stable housing, structure for each day, a sense of purpose, and relationships. It can also create stress, isolation, and exposure to old patterns. Understanding both sides is the starting point for designing a business that supports recovery rather than threatening it.

## Why some people choose self-employment

People in recovery may choose self-employment for several reasons: barriers to conventional employment, such as a criminal record or gaps in work history; a desire for flexible schedules that accommodate treatment, meetings, or family responsibilities; a skill or trade they want to use directly; or a wish to build something of their own. Employers are also changing. The U.S. Department of Labor promotes "recovery-ready workplaces" that expand employment opportunities for people in or seeking recovery, facilitate help-seeking, and reduce stigma [3]. For some readers, a recovery-ready employer may be a better near-term choice than a business; this chapter helps you decide.

## The risks are real

Entrepreneurship is demanding. In a 2015 study led by psychiatrist Michael Freeman of the University of California, San Francisco, nearly half of 242 surveyed entrepreneurs reported one or more mental health conditions, including depression, ADHD, bipolar conditions, and substance use problems; including those with significant family histories, Freeman estimated that 72 percent were affected directly or indirectly [4]. The study was small and not designed to establish cause, but it is consistent with what many founders report: irregular income, long hours, isolation, and the weight of being responsible for everything.

| Factor | Employment | Self-employment |
|---|---|---|
| Income stability | Usually steady paycheck | Irregular, especially early |
| Schedule | Set by employer | Flexible, but work can expand to fill all hours |
| Structure | Provided | Must be created |
| Benefits | Often included | Must be arranged and paid for |
| Social contact | Built in | Can be isolating |
| Background checks | Common at hiring | Less common, but some clients and licenses require them |
| Upside | Raises, promotion | Ownership, independence, potential growth |

:::case Dan Martell: from detention to a slow start (real, cited)
Dan Martell, now a Canadian software entrepreneur, investor, and author, has described his early life publicly. In a 2023 interview he said he grew up with a mother who had a severe drinking problem, was placed in a crisis center at 12, was introduced to drugs at 13, and was in juvenile detention at 15; after promising himself he would change, he "lasted 24 hours" before returning to selling drugs and was later sentenced to almost two years [5]. During rehabilitation he found a computer and, in his words, "got sober and then replaced it with writing code" [5]. He began writing code and starting companies at 17, but "it took seven years, until I was about 24, until I finally found any business success" [5]. The lesson for this chapter is not that recovery leads to wealth—Martell's results are his own and are not typical or predictable—but that his first ventures failed, that the timeline was long, and that a new obsession can carry its own risks, a theme he returns to when describing later burnout (Chapter 10).
:::

## A readiness check

Before launching, consider honestly whether the timing supports your recovery. There is no universal rule about how long someone should be in recovery before starting a business, and this book does not offer one. What helps is a clear-eyed look at your supports, finances, and stressors, ideally discussed with people who know you well—a sponsor, counselor, peer, or trusted friend.

:::example Maya's readiness check (illustrative)
Maya, 38, has four years in recovery and works 30 hours a week at a warehouse for $18 an hour. She wants to start Steady Hands on weekends, assembling supply kits and setting up events for community organizations. She scores herself from 1 (weak) to 3 (strong):

| Area | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|

| Financial cushion | 2 | $2,400 in savings; rent covered by job |
| Time available | 2 | About 10 hours a week without cutting meetings or sleep |
| Relevant skills | 3 | Warehouse inventory, packing, event volunteering |
| Current stressors | 2 | Recent move; otherwise stable |
| Total | 12 of 15 | |

Maya decides to start as a side business while keeping her job, with a rule that the business may not take more than 10 hours a week or replace any recovery commitment during the first six months. She shares the plan with her sponsor and asks to be told if the business seems to be crowding out what keeps her well.

# Chapter 2: Choosing a Nonclinical Service

:::objectives
- Distinguish buyers, users, and beneficiaries.
- Generate and screen service ideas for usefulness, feasibility, and scope risk.
- Identify activities that are regulated and must be verified before offering.
- Choose one service to test.
:::

A good first offer is easy to describe, has a clear buyer, and can be delivered without collecting sensitive information or making clinical judgments. "We help people in recovery" is a mission, not a service. "We pack and deliver event supply kits ordered by organizations" is a service. It tells a buyer exactly what is being purchased and gives the business a boundary.

## Buyers, users, and beneficiaries

Three roles are easy to confuse:

1. The buyer selects the service, approves the price, and pays—often an organization.
2. The user receives or uses the service—sometimes the buyer, sometimes staff or attendees.
3. The beneficiary benefits indirectly, such as people attending an event.

Do not assume that beneficiaries are your customers or that the buyer can share their private details. A recovery community center that orders 40 supply kits for a workshop is your customer. The workshop attendees are not, and you need nothing about them except, perhaps, a headcount.

## Generating and screening ideas

1. List ten ordinary services you could deliver safely and repeatedly: assembly, printing, event setup and teardown, cleaning, landscaping, moving, bookkeeping, graphic design, photography, food service with the required permits, delivery, or skilled trades.
2. For each, name a likely buyer and what they would pay for.
3. Note equipment, travel, time, licensing, and insurance needs.
4. Mark any idea that involves providing care, making clinical or behavioral judgments, housing people, transporting people in vulnerable situations, handling medications, or receiving payment for referrals. These carry scope and regulatory risk (Chapter 3).
5. Score the remaining ideas and choose one to test, not a bundle.

:::case Greyston Bakery: an ordinary product with a mission in how it operates (real, cited)
Greyston Bakery in Yonkers, New York, was founded in 1982 by Zen Buddhist teacher Bernie Glassman to employ people facing barriers to work [30]. Its hiring practice, which it calls Open Hiring, means "no interviews, no background checks, and no resumes are required to get a job" for entry-level baker positions [30]. What Greyston sells, however, is ordinary: brownies and blondies sold online, in retailers, and as an ingredient in Ben & Jerry's ice cream [30]. The mission lives in how the business operates, not in a claim that the product heals anyone. The organization's history also shows the value of narrowing scope: its affiliated foundation, which had expanded into community services, announced that it was scaling back to its original focus on employment, while the bakery continued its Open Hiring model [30]. Greyston's outcomes are its own; the point here is the structure—an ordinary product, a clear buyer, and values expressed through operations.
:::

## Regulated activities

Some services that seem informal are regulated. Examples include counseling and therapy, peer-support services billed to insurance or public programs (which typically require state certification), recovery residences and sober living homes (which some states and localities license or certify), transportation of passengers for hire, food service, childcare, and many trades. The U.S. Small Business Administration notes that licenses and permits depend on business activity and location [14]. Do not infer that a service is permitted because another business offers it, because a buyer requests it, or because it seems informal. If an idea touches a regulated area, identify the exact activity and the responsible authority, and verify requirements directly before offering it—or choose a different service.

| Service idea | Likely buyer | Scope risk | Regulatory check | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Event supply kits | Nonprofits, community centers | Low | General business license | Test |
| Event setup and teardown | Event organizers, churches | Low | Business license; liability insurance | Consider later |
| Rides to appointments | Individuals | High (vulnerable passengers) | Passenger transport rules; insurance | Exclude |
| "Recovery coaching" | Individuals | High (care, judgment) | State certification rules vary | Exclude |
| Sober-living house | Residents | Very high | State/local licensing or certification | Exclude |
| Printing and flyers | Organizations | Low | General business license | Consider later |

:::example Maya chooses one service (illustrative)
Maya scores her low-risk ideas from 1 to 3 on demand (based on what she has heard from organizers), feasibility (skills and equipment), and margin (likely profit per hour):

| Idea | Demand | Feasibility | Margin | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Event supply kits | 3 | 3 | 2 | 8 |
| Event setup/teardown | 2 | 2 | 3 | 7 |
| Printing and flyers | 2 | 1 (no equipment) | 1 | 4 |

She chooses supply kits, assembled from buyer-approved lists, with materials either supplied by the buyer or purchased by Maya at cost plus a stated handling fee. She sets an initial limit of 50 kits per order and a delivery radius of 15 miles. Setup and teardown go on a "later" list once she has liability insurance and a helper.
:::

:::checklist Service selection
- My service is described as a task, not a mission.
- I can name the buyer, the user, and any beneficiaries.
- The service does not require care, clinical judgment, or housing.
- I have checked whether the activity is regulated where I operate.
- I have set an initial quantity limit and delivery area.
- I am testing one service, not several.
:::

:::summary
Describe the service as a task with a clear buyer. Distinguish buyers, users, and beneficiaries, and collect nothing about beneficiaries that the work does not require. Generate ideas broadly, screen out those involving care, judgment, housing, or vulnerable transport, and verify any regulated activity with the responsible authority. Choose one service to test, with explicit limits.
:::

# Chapter 3: Scope Boundaries and Role Clarity

:::objectives
- Write a three-part scope card.
- Explain dual relationships and why they create risk.
- Hold boundaries under pressure with prepared language.
- Separate the founder's personal recovery role from the business role.
:::

A boundary is useful only if it changes what the business does. Scope is the written definition of what the business provides, what it does not, and what it does when asked for something excluded. In businesses connected to the recovery community, clear scope protects three parties: the people who might otherwise receive help from someone unqualified, the buyer who needs reliable service, and the founder, whose own recovery can be strained by taking on roles that belong to others.

## The scope card

A scope card is a short document—small enough for a wallet or the first page of a binder—with three parts:

1. We do: observable tasks. "We assemble up to 50 buyer-approved event kits and deliver them within 15 miles during a confirmed window."
2. We do not: adjacent services that could be misunderstood. "We do not provide counseling, treatment, recovery coaching, housing placement, transportation of participants, health screening, crisis response, or referrals for payment."
3. We will do instead: a calm alternative. "That's outside our service. I can share a verified public resource, or let the event organizer know if you'd like."

## Dual relationships

A dual relationship exists when you hold two different roles with the same person—for example, being someone's sponsor or peer and also their employer, vendor, or landlord. Dual relationships are not always wrong, but they create risks: blurred expectations, conflicts of interest, pressure on the less powerful person, and strain on the relationship that matters more. Mutual-aid traditions and professional ethics codes for peer workers generally caution against mixing roles. A practical rule for a small business: do not sell to, hire, or contract with people you sponsor or support in a recovery role, and do not use meetings or peer relationships to market the business.

## Holding boundaries under pressure

Requests to step outside scope often arrive with urgency and good intentions: "Could you just check on the attendees?" "Can you give my cousin a ride?" "You've been there—would you talk to him?" Prepare language in advance, use it consistently, and train anyone who represents the business to use it too. A disclaimer does not make an out-of-scope service safe or authorized. If a buyer asks for an activity you have not verified, say no for now.

| Request | Why it is out of scope | Prepared response |
|---|---|---|
| "Can you keep an eye on attendees and tell us who seems unwell?" | Requires judgment and personal information | "That's outside what we do. I'll deliver the kits; your team or a qualified provider should handle participant support." |
| "Could you drive a participant home?" | Passenger transport; vulnerability | "We don't transport people. The organizer may have a ride resource." |
| "You understand—could you talk to my son?" | Counseling-type role | "I'm here for the order today. If you'd like, I can share the national helpline number." |
| "We'll pay you a bonus for every person you send to our program." | Referral payment; potential legal and ethical problems | "We don't accept payment for referrals." |

:::case Homeboy Industries: businesses and services kept distinct (real, cited)
Homeboy Industries was started by Father Greg Boyle in 1988 at Dolores Mission Parish in Boyle Heights, East Los Angeles, and describes itself as the largest gang intervention, rehabilitation, and reentry program in the world [12][13]. Its social enterprises—a bakery, silkscreen and embroidery, a café, catering, merchandise, farmers markets, a diner at Los Angeles City Hall, and electronics recycling—sell ordinary goods and services while paying trainees to learn job skills [12]. Its support services—case management, tattoo removal, legal services, education, and mental health services including individual therapy and substance abuse counseling—are offered separately as program services [12]. The structural lesson for a small founder is that care and commerce can coexist in one mission, but Homeboy delivers clinical services through a program designed for them, not through the bakery counter. A one-person business without that infrastructure should keep its service to the commercial side and hand off the rest. Homeboy's results are its own.
:::

## Your recovery and your business

Many founders in recovery want their business to "give back," and some are open about their own history. Both can be healthy. But the business role and the personal recovery role should be distinct. Telling your story is your choice, never a condition of doing business, and you should never ask customers or their participants to share theirs. Keep business records separate from any personal or peer-support activity, and do not let customer conversations become informal counseling sessions—for the customer's sake and your own.

:::example Maya holds the line (illustrative)
Two weeks before a delivery, a buyer emails: "Since you're in recovery too, could you stay for the workshop and share your story? Also, could you note anyone who looks like they're struggling?"

Maya compares the request with her scope card. Staying to share her story is a personal choice outside the order; noting who seems to be struggling would require judgments and personal information. She replies:

"Thank you for thinking of me. For this order, I'll deliver the 40 kits at 9:00 a.m. and confirm the count with your staff. Observing or reporting on participants is outside our service—your team or a qualified provider is the right fit for that. Speaking at events isn't part of Steady Hands' work; if I ever do that, it would be separately and on my own time."

She records the exchange in the order file and adds "observing participants" to the "We do not" list on her scope card.
:::

:::worksheet Scope card
We do (observable tasks, with limits): ___
We do not (adjacent services): ___
If asked for something excluded, we say: ___
Payments we never accept: ___
Roles I keep separate from the business: ___
Authority to check before changing scope: ___
:::

:::summary
A scope card states what the business does, what it does not, and what it says instead. Dual relationships—holding business and recovery roles with the same person—create risks and are best avoided. Prepare language for common out-of-scope requests and never accept referral payments. Keep the founder's personal recovery role separate from the business, and never make storytelling a condition of business.
:::

# Chapter 4: Legal Setup and Second Chances

:::objectives
- Choose a basic business structure and register appropriately.
- Set up records that satisfy tax and practical needs from the first day.
- Understand how criminal records can affect licensing, contracts, and financing.
- Locate record-clearing and licensing-review options in your state.
:::

A small service business needs a legal form, the right registrations, separate finances, and records. For founders with criminal records, there is a second layer: understanding which licenses, contracts, or loans might involve a background check, and what the law in their state allows. None of this requires perfection on the first day, but each item is easier to set up correctly at the start than to repair later.

## Business structure

The U.S. Small Business Administration describes the common structures: a sole proprietorship (simplest; no separation between owner and business for liability), a partnership, a limited liability company (LLC), and corporations [15]. Many very small service businesses begin as sole proprietorships or single-member LLCs. An LLC can separate business liabilities from personal assets in many situations, though it does not protect against everything, and fees and rules vary by state. A local Small Business Development Center (SBDC) or SCORE mentor can explain the options without charge, and a qualified attorney or tax professional can advise on your circumstances.

## Registrations, licenses, and insurance

1. Choose a name and check that it is available in your state.
2. Register the business with your state if required (LLCs must; sole proprietors using a name other than their own often file a "doing business as" registration).
3. Obtain an Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS if needed; many owners get one even when not required so they can avoid giving their Social Security number to clients.
4. Check city and county licensing and any permits for your activity [14].
5. Ask an insurance agent about general liability coverage; many organizations require a certificate of insurance before contracting.
6. Open a separate business bank account.

## Records from day one

IRS Publication 583 explains that business owners must keep records that support income, deductions, and credits shown on tax returns, and describes supporting documents such as sales slips, invoices, receipts, deposit slips, and canceled checks [16]. The IRS also advises keeping records that identify the source of receipts and that support the basis of assets [17]. For a small service business, a simple system works: a separate bank account, a numbered invoice for every sale, a photograph of every receipt filed by month, and a monthly summary of income and expenses. Records are also part of recovery-friendly structure—fewer surprises and less anxiety at tax time.

## Criminal records and second-chance considerations

Many people in recovery have arrest or conviction records related to substance use. A record can affect a small business in three main places.

Occupational licensing. Many trades and professions require state licenses, and licensing boards have historically been able to deny applicants with records. The National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL) reports that many states have changed these laws—for example, by requiring that offenses be directly related to the occupation, limiting how far back boards may look, or allowing applicants to request a preliminary determination before investing in training [18]. Check your state's rules before choosing a licensed trade.

Record clearing. NCSL tracks state laws that automatically seal or clear certain records, often called "clean slate" laws, and reports that a majority of states have at least one automatic record-clearing provision, alongside petition-based expungement and sealing [19]. Legal aid organizations and reentry programs can help you understand eligibility.

Financing. Until 2023, the SBA's loan programs included "character" standards that considered criminal history. In April 2023 the SBA issued a final rule, effective in May 2023, that removed the requirement for lenders to consider character in this way for its 7(a) and 504 programs [20][21]. Lenders still make their own credit decisions, and SBA rules can change, so ask lenders directly about current policies rather than assuming either a yes or a no.

Client background checks. Some buyers—schools, hospitals, government agencies—require background checks for vendors who work on site. Others do not. Ask at the start of a sales conversation rather than discovering a requirement after the work is scheduled.

:::case Dave's Killer Bread: a second chance and a second-chance employer (real, cited)
Dave Dahl grew up in a family of bakers but spent a total of 15 years in prison [7]; the Los Angeles Times reported convictions for burglary, armed robbery, and drug dealing [32]. When he was released for the last time in December 2004, his older brother Glenn gave him a ride from the bus station and a $12-an-hour job at the family bakery [8]. In 2005, Dave and his nephew Shobi took loaves to the Portland Farmers Market, and Dave's Killer Bread was born [7]. The company grew into a national organic brand; Flowers Foods completed its acquisition in 2015 for about $275 million [9]. The company describes itself as a Second Chance Employer that hires "the best person for the job, regardless of criminal history" [10], and in 2015 it created a foundation to promote second-chance employment, later acquired by the nonprofit Jobs for the Future [11]. Its story also includes setbacks: in 2013 Dahl experienced a public mental health crisis and arrest, which Willamette Week reported on in detail [8] (Chapter 10). The results are Dave's Killer Bread's own. For this chapter, the takeaway is that the company's start depended on an existing, legal, properly run bakery, and that second-chance hiring became a deliberate business policy rather than an informal favor.
:::

# Chapter 5: Research Without Intrusion

:::objectives
- Plan buyer interviews that focus on work, not personal history.
- Use past-behavior questions to avoid misleading answers.
- Record findings without collecting sensitive information.
- Decide whether evidence supports a test.
:::

Market research for a recovery-adjacent business has an ethical dimension that ordinary market research does not. The people you want to serve may have private health histories, and organizations that serve them hold confidential information. Your research should be about the buyer's operational problem—how they currently get kits, cleaning, printing, or setup done—not about anyone's recovery. You do not need anyone's story to learn whether your service is useful.

## Ask about the past, not opinions

Rob Fitzpatrick's book The Mom Test (2013) is a widely used guide to customer interviews. Its core idea is that people are polite about ideas, so asking "Would you buy this?" produces misleading encouragement; instead, ask about the customer's life and specific past behavior, talk less, and listen for commitments [28]. In practice, that means questions like "Tell me about the last time you prepared supplies for an event" rather than "Do you think a kit service is a good idea?"

The SBA also recommends combining direct research, such as interviews and surveys, with secondary data on your market and competitors [29].

## A research framework

1. Define who you will speak with: people who buy or approve the service (event coordinators, office managers, program directors), not participants.
2. Prepare five to eight questions about recent, specific events: what they did, how long it took, what it cost, what went wrong, and what they tried.
3. Ask for 15 to 20 minutes, state your purpose plainly, and say you are not asking about clients or participants.
4. Take notes on the process and costs, not on people. If someone volunteers personal or participant information, do not record it.
5. Look for patterns across at least eight to ten conversations.
6. Ask for a small commitment at the end: a follow-up meeting, a sample order, or an introduction.

| Instead of asking | Ask |
|---|---|
| "Would you use a kit service?" | "How did you prepare supplies for your last event?" |
| "How much would you pay?" | "What did that cost you, including staff time?" |
| "Do you serve a lot of people in recovery?" | "How many attendees did you plan for?" |

The lesson for research is that structured preparation, a mentor who knows the market, and a clear organizational buyer can replace guesswork.

# Chapter 6: An Accessible, Honest Offer

:::objectives
- Write an offer that states the task, limits, price, and timeline.
- Avoid health, recovery, and outcome claims that require substantiation.
- Decide whether and how to use your own story in marketing.
- Make the offer accessible to people with disabilities.
:::

An offer is a short, specific description of what a buyer receives, at what price, by when, and under what conditions. A clear offer reduces back-and-forth, prevents misunderstandings, and makes it easier to say no to out-of-scope requests. An honest offer avoids claims that the business cannot support.

## Components of a clear offer

1. The task: "Assembly and delivery of up to 50 event supply kits from a buyer-approved list."
2. Limits: quantity, area, lead time, what is excluded.
3. Price: a base fee, a per-unit rate, and how materials are handled.
4. Timeline: order deadline, delivery window, confirmation process.
5. Changes and cancellations: what happens if quantities change or the event is canceled.
6. Contact: one channel for questions, with response times.

## Claims and substantiation

The Federal Trade Commission's advertising guidance says that advertising must be truthful and not misleading and that businesses must have evidence to back up their claims [22]. The FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance, updated in December 2022, explains that health-related claims—express or implied—generally require competent and reliable scientific evidence [23]. For a nonclinical business, the safest approach is not to make health, recovery, or wellness claims at all. "Our kits help people stay sober" is a health claim. "Kits arrive assembled, counted, and on time" is a service claim you can control.

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| "Supporting your recovery journey" | "Event kits assembled and delivered" |
| "Healing through service" | "Clear pricing; on-time delivery" |
| "Our team understands addiction" | "Reliable vendor for community events" |
| "Proven to reduce relapse" | (Do not make this claim) |
| "Guaranteed results" | "Count confirmed at delivery" |

## Your story in your marketing

Some founders choose to share their recovery in public; others do not. Both are legitimate. Consider three questions before putting your story into the brand. First, is the story yours to tell, without exposing others? Second, are you comfortable with it being permanent and public? Third, what happens to the business if your recovery has a difficult period? Dave's Killer Bread built its packaging around Dave Dahl's redemption story; when he experienced a mental health crisis in 2013, Willamette Week noted the risks of turning a person's recovery into a brand symbol [8]. A business can express its values without making the founder's health its product.

## Accessibility

The U.S. Department of Justice's guidance on web accessibility explains that businesses open to the public must provide equal access to people with disabilities, including online, and points to common problems such as missing alternative text for images, poor color contrast, videos without captions, and forms that cannot be used without a mouse [24]. A one-page offer should use plain language, readable fonts, sufficient contrast, descriptive link text, and an alternative way to order (phone or email) for anyone who cannot use an online form.

:::case Dave's Killer Bread: when the founder's story is the label (real, cited)
Every Dave's Killer Bread wrapper once carried a cartoon of Dave Dahl and a short narrative describing how "a whole lot of suffering" had transformed "an ex-con into an honest man," according to Willamette Week's 2014 report [8]. The story distinguished the brand in a crowded bread aisle. In November 2013, however, Dahl had a public mental health crisis and was arrested; Willamette Week reported that people who knew him said he had been drinking for some time [8]. The company's CEO said Dahl was "a real person with real challenges" and had been public about his struggles [8]. The brand continued and was acquired in 2015 [9]. The case is not a judgment of Dahl; it shows that tying a business's identity to one person's recovery places a heavy, public burden on that person. The results described are the company's own.
:::

:::example Maya's one-page offer (illustrative)
Steady Hands — Event Supply Kits

What you get: Up to 50 kits assembled from your approved item list, packed in labeled bags, counted, and delivered within 15 miles.

Price: $60 order fee plus $2.50 per kit for assembly. Materials: you supply them, or we purchase at receipt cost plus 10 percent handling.

Timeline: Order and item list 10 days before the event. Delivery in a confirmed two-hour window. Count confirmed with your staff at delivery.

Changes: Quantity changes accepted up to 5 days before. Cancellations within 5 days: order fee plus any materials purchased.

Not included: Event staffing, participant contact, transportation of people, or any counseling or support services.

Order by email or phone; replies within one business day. A large-print or plain-text version is available on request.

For a 40-kit order with buyer-supplied materials, the price is $60 plus 40 times $2.50, or $160.
:::

:::checklist Offer review
- Task, limits, price, timeline, changes, and contact are stated.
- No health, recovery, or outcome claims.
- Exclusions match the scope card.
- Founder's story is optional and has been considered carefully.
- Plain language, readable layout, contrast checked.
- Phone or email alternative to any online form.
:::

:::summary
A clear offer states the task, limits, price, timeline, change rules, and contact. FTC guidance requires truthful claims backed by evidence, and health claims require scientific substantiation, so a nonclinical business should make service claims, not health claims. Consider carefully before making your recovery part of the brand. Make the offer accessible, including a non-web way to order.

# Chapter 7: Pricing a Small Test

:::objectives
- Build a price from cost, time, and buyer value.
- Calculate break-even for a test order.
- Design a small, bounded paid test.
- Value your time with a simple hourly benchmark.
:::

Pricing in a mission-minded business often goes wrong in one direction: the founder underprices because they want to help, then burns out or cannot cover costs. A sustainable price covers direct costs and the founder's time, and it sits below what the buyer currently spends in money and staff time. Discounts can be offered deliberately; they should not be the default.

## Building a price

1. List direct costs per order: materials (if you buy them), packaging, mileage, and payment fees.
2. Estimate your time per order, including shopping, assembly, travel, and administration.
3. Choose an hourly rate for your time that reflects the work and local pay.
4. Add a margin for overhead such as insurance, licenses, and tools.
5. Compare with the buyer's current cost, including staff time.
6. Set the price, then test it with real orders.

## Valuing your time

Dan Martell's "buyback rate" is a simple benchmark: take your annual income, divide it by the hours you work in a year, and use a fraction of that hourly figure to decide which tasks are worth paying someone else to do [6]. In Buy Back Your Time, Martell argues that founders should delegate tasks that are worth less than that rate so they can spend time on higher-value work [6]. For a new founder the same arithmetic helps in reverse: it shows the minimum value of an hour, so you can see when a price is not covering your time.

## The small test

A small test is a limited number of paid orders—often three to five—under a written offer, with predefined measures. It answers questions that interviews cannot: will buyers pay, how long does the work really take, what goes wrong, and what is the true margin? Limit the test so failure is affordable.

| Measure | Target for test | How measured |
|---|---|---|
| Paid orders | 3 to 5 | Invoices |
| On-time delivery | 100% | Delivery log |
| Count accuracy | 100% | Signed count |
| Hours per 40 kits | 3 or fewer | Time log |
| Margin per order | $70 or more | Order ledger |
| Buyer willing to reorder | 2 or more | Follow-up |

:::case Dan Martell's buyback rate (real, cited)
In a 2023 Entrepreneur podcast interview, Martell explained his buyback rate as starting with what you make in a year—for an entrepreneur, roughly what you pay yourself plus profit and distributions—and converting it to an hourly figure [6]. He described the purpose as building a company that does not consume the founder's calendar: "we don't hire people to grow our business—we hire people to buy back time out of our calendar," he said, so founders avoid "building a business we don't grow to hate" [6]. His coaching business results are his own. For a founder in recovery, the relevant point is that time is a limited resource that protects both income and well-being; pricing should account for it.
:::

# Chapter 8: Minimum Data and Privacy

:::objectives
- Apply data minimization to orders, invoices, and communications.
- Build a simple data inventory and retention schedule.
- Protect information using the FTC's five principles.
- Avoid marketing practices that expose sensitive information.
:::

A recovery-adjacent business can learn sensitive facts without trying: an organization's name on an invoice ("Hope Recovery Community Center"), an email signature, a delivery address, or a passing comment. The safest data is data you never collect. When you must collect it, keep it only as long as needed and protect it.

## Data minimization

Data minimization means collecting only the information required to deliver the service and meet legal obligations. For a kit business, that is usually the buyer's organization name, a contact person, a billing address, a delivery address and window, the item list, quantity, and payment records. It does not include participant names, diagnoses, recovery status, attendance lists, or stories. If a buyer offers that information, decline it.

## The FTC's five principles

The FTC's guide Protecting Personal Information: A Guide for Business organizes data security into five principles [25]:

1. Take stock: know what personal information you have and where it is.
2. Scale down: keep only what you need for your business.
3. Lock it: protect what you keep, physically and electronically.
4. Pitch it: properly dispose of what you no longer need.
5. Plan ahead: create a plan to respond to security incidents.

## Practical protections for a one-person business

- Use a business email account with two-factor authentication.
- Keep order records in one place, not scattered across texts and personal email.
- Lock your phone and computer; use a password manager.
- Do not post photos of deliveries, venues, or attendees without written permission, and never in a way that identifies a recovery setting.
- Do not add buyers to marketing lists without consent, and do not upload customer lists to advertising platforms.
- Shred paper records you no longer need.
- Write down who to notify and what to do if a device is lost or an account is compromised.

## Marketing and sensitive inferences

Advertising tools can reveal more than you intend. Tracking pixels and customer-list uploads can tell an advertising platform that a person interacted with a recovery-related business. Even though your service is not clinical, the context may be sensitive. Keep marketing simple: a basic website without third-party advertising trackers, direct outreach to organizations, and referrals from satisfied buyers.

:::case BetterHelp and the FTC: a privacy promise broken (real, cited)
In March 2023, the FTC announced a proposed order against BetterHelp, Inc., an online counseling service, requiring it to pay $7.8 million to settle charges that it revealed consumers' sensitive data, including information about mental health, to third parties such as Facebook and Snapchat for advertising after promising to keep such data private [33]. The FTC said the order would ban the company from sharing consumers' health data for advertising and from sharing personal information with certain third parties for re-targeting, including visitors who never signed up for the service [33]. BetterHelp is a clinical service and much larger than any reader's business, but the lesson applies to small firms: advertising tools can turn a website visit into a sensitive inference, and privacy promises must match practice. The facts are the FTC's allegations and order, not a TTI result.

# Chapter 9: Safe Hand-offs

:::objectives
- Recognize when a request or situation falls outside the business.
- Use a short, calm hand-off script.
- Maintain a verified resource card.
- Respond appropriately to emergencies.
:::

Sooner or later, someone may ask the business for help it does not provide, or a situation may arise that worries you. A hand-off is a brief, respectful redirection to a resource that can help, without the business taking on a role it is not qualified for. A good hand-off is short, offers a verified option, respects the person's choice, and records only the fact that a hand-off occurred.

## Verified public resources

SAMHSA lists two national resources for support [26]:

- The SAMHSA National Helpline, 1-800-662-HELP (4357), which provides free, confidential, 24/7, 365-day-a-year treatment referral and information in English and Spanish.
- The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org, for free and confidential support for people in suicidal crisis or emotional distress, including substance use concerns.

SAMHSA's FindTreatment.gov is a confidential and anonymous locator for treatment facilities [27]. For immediate danger, call 911. Local resources—a county crisis line, a recovery community center—can be added after you verify them directly.

## The hand-off framework

1. Notice: a request outside scope, or signs that someone needs help you cannot provide.
2. Stay calm and respectful; do not diagnose, counsel, or argue.
3. Say what you can and cannot do in one or two sentences.
4. Offer a verified resource and, if appropriate, offer to let the organizer or staff know.
5. Respect the person's choice.
6. If there is immediate danger, call 911.
7. Record only that a hand-off occurred, with no personal details.

## Scripts

| Situation | Script |
|---|---|
| Customer asks for advice about a loved one | "I'm not able to advise on that. The SAMHSA National Helpline is free and confidential, 24/7: 1-800-662-4357." |
| Someone seems distressed at a delivery | "I'm just here with the kits, but would you like me to get someone from the staff?" |
| Someone mentions thoughts of suicide | "Thank you for telling me. You can call or text 988 right now, and I can stay while you do or find a staff member." |
| Immediate danger | Call 911. |
| Buyer asks for a treatment recommendation | "We don't make recommendations. FindTreatment.gov is a confidential locator." |

:::case 988: why public crisis infrastructure exists (real, cited)
In 2020, Congress designated 988 as a new three-digit dialing code, operated through the existing National Suicide Prevention Lifeline; SAMHSA describes it as a first step toward a transformed crisis care system [34]. The Lifeline now offers 24/7 call, text, and chat support for mental health and substance use concerns, with access for Deaf and hard-of-hearing callers and Spanish speakers [34]. SAMHSA reported that in 2020 the United States had one death by suicide every 11 minutes [34]. The system exists because crisis response requires trained responders, coverage at every hour, and links to local services—capabilities a small business cannot and should not try to replicate. The relevant lesson for a founder is humility: your role is to know the number and offer it calmly, not to be the responder.
:::

# Chapter 10: Protecting Your Recovery

:::objectives
- Identify business stressors that can affect recovery.
- Build a personal sustainability plan with warning signs and responses.
- Set work limits and accountability.
- Plan for a difficult period without losing the business.
:::

The business exists to support a good life, not the reverse. SAMHSA's guiding principles of recovery describe recovery as emerging from hope, as person-driven, as occurring through many pathways, as holistic, and as supported by peers, allies, relationships, and social networks [2]. A business plan for a founder in recovery should include those supports explicitly, because entrepreneurship tends to erode them: long hours crowd out meetings, money stress builds, and isolation replaces community.

## Common stressors

- Irregular income and debt.
- Long, unpredictable hours and lost sleep.
- Isolation from coworkers and peers.
- Rejection from buyers and investors.
- Events and networking where alcohol is common.
- Perfectionism and the belief that you must handle everything alone.
- Success itself, which can bring new pressures and temptations.

## A personal sustainability plan

1. List your non-negotiables: meetings, appointments, sleep, family time, exercise.
2. Set work limits: maximum hours per week and days off.
3. Name warning signs: skipping commitments, working through the night, hiding problems, irritability, isolation.
4. Decide responses for each warning sign: call a named person, reduce hours, pause new orders.
5. Choose accountability partners and how often you will check in.
6. Create a continuity plan: who could fulfill or cancel orders if you needed to step away.
7. Review monthly.

:::case Dan Martell and Dave Dahl: success did not end the struggle (real, cited)
Martell has described his years of failed ventures and, later, a period of intense stress in which he believed he was "this mentally tough person" but experienced physical symptoms he could not stop—"it felt like I was having a heart attack"—until he admitted, "I need some help" [5]. He has also described coding as becoming "my new addiction" after he got sober [5], a candid acknowledgment that work can take on compulsive qualities. Dave Dahl's story includes success followed by a 2013 crisis: Willamette Week reported that people who knew him said he had been drinking since at least 2011, during a period of rapid company growth, before a mental health breakdown and arrest [8]. In 2009, his brother Glenn had told Inc. that the company's rise depended on Dave staying clean [8]. Both men's experiences are their own. They show that business success is not a substitute for recovery supports, and that growth can bring new pressure.
:::

Research reinforces the point that founders face real mental health risks: in Freeman's survey, a large share of entrepreneurs reported mental health conditions, including substance use problems [4].

| Warning sign | Response | Person to call |
|---|---|---|
| Skipped a meeting for work | Reschedule work, not meeting | Sponsor |
| Worked past midnight twice in a week | Cap hours; decline one order | Accountability partner |
| Hiding a financial problem | Review numbers with adviser | SBDC adviser |
| Irritability or isolation | Take a full day off; reconnect | Peer or friend |
| Cravings or thoughts of using | Use your recovery plan | Sponsor, counselor, or helpline |

# Chapter 11: Dry Runs, Reviews, and Stop Rules

:::objectives
- Conduct a dry run before the first paid order.
- Review each order with a short after-action format.
- Write stop rules for the business and for yourself.
- Decide whether to continue, change, or stop after the test.
:::

A dry run is a rehearsal of the full service—order intake, purchasing, assembly, packing, delivery, invoicing—with no customer at risk. A review is a short, structured look back after each order. A stop rule is a condition, written in advance, that triggers a pause or change. Together, they replace hope with evidence.

## The dry run

1. Take a sample order through your intake form and confirm it by email.
2. Buy or gather materials for five kits and time each step.
3. Assemble, label, and count.
4. Drive the route and time it.
5. Prepare a sample invoice and record it in your books.
6. Practice your scope response and hand-off script aloud.
7. Note what took longer or went wrong, and fix it before the first real order.

## After-action reviews

After each order, answer four questions in five minutes: What did we intend? What happened? Why was there a difference? What will we do next time? Record numbers—hours, costs, count accuracy, on-time status—alongside notes.

## Stop rules

Stop rules work because they are set when you are calm. Business stop rules might include: stop accepting orders if two in a row lose money; pause if any count error reaches a buyer twice; stop and seek advice if a buyer requests something that may be regulated. Personal stop rules connect to the sustainability plan: pause new orders if you miss recovery commitments or exceed your hours for two weeks.

| Rule type | Trigger | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | Two orders in a row with negative contribution | Stop new orders; reprice |
| Quality | Count error reaches a buyer twice | Pause; fix process |
| Scope | Buyer requests possibly regulated service | Decline; verify with authority before reconsidering |
| Privacy | Any data incident | Follow incident plan; pause marketing |
| Personal | Missed recovery commitments two weeks running | Pause new orders; call sponsor |
| Test end | Four orders completed | Decide: continue, change, or stop |

:::case Dave's Killer Bread: staged growth and deliberate decisions (real, cited)
Dave's Killer Bread did not begin as a national brand. It started with loaves at the Portland Farmers Market in 2005 [7], inside an existing family bakery. By late 2012, when it was sold in 14 Western states, the family sold a 50 percent stake to a New York investment firm to expand further, according to Willamette Week [8]; Flowers Foods completed its acquisition in 2015 [9]. Each stage involved a decision to expand rather than an automatic progression, and later reporting showed that growth coincided with real strain on its founder [8]. The company's results are its own. The lesson for a small test is that growth should be a decision made against evidence and personal capacity, and that a founder can choose to stay small.
:::

:::example Maya's test decision (illustrative)
After eight weeks, Maya reviews four orders:

| Measure | Target | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Paid orders | 3–5 | 4 |
| On-time delivery | 100% | 4 of 4 |
| Count accuracy | 100% | 3 of 4 (one kit short; replaced same day) |
| Hours per 40 kits | 3 or fewer | 3.4 average |
| Contribution per order | $70+ | $118 average |
| Buyers willing to reorder | 2+ | 3 |
| Business hours per month | 40 or fewer | 36 average |
| Meetings attended | All | All |

Decision: continue, with changes. She adds a final count check with a checklist before loading (fixing the shortage), raises the per-kit rate to $2.75 for orders under 30 kits to cover fixed delivery time, and keeps her 10-hour weekly limit. She decides not to add setup and teardown for another six months.
:::

- Dry run completed and timed.
- Scope card and hand-off card printed.
- Resource card verified.
- After-action review template ready.
- Business and personal stop rules written and shared with an accountability partner.
- Test end date and decision meeting scheduled.
:::

:::summary
A dry run rehearses the entire service before a customer is at risk. Four-question after-action reviews after each order turn experience into improvements. Stop rules—financial, quality, scope, privacy, and personal—are written in advance and trigger pauses or changes. At the end of the test, decide to continue, change, or stop based on evidence and personal capacity.
:::

# Appendix A: Scope Card and Boundary Scripts

Use this template to write your own scope card. Keep it to one page and review it every quarter or whenever a new kind of request arrives.

:::worksheet Scope card template
Business name: ___
We do (task, quantity limit, area, lead time): ___
We do not (list at least six adjacent services): ___
If asked for something excluded, we say: ___
We never accept: ___
Regulated activities we have checked, and the authority consulted: ___
Date of last review: ___
:::

Sample "we do not" list for a nonclinical service business: counseling or therapy; treatment or medical advice; recovery coaching or peer-support services; housing placement or sober-living arrangements; transportation of participants; health screening or observing participants; crisis response; referral fees or commissions.

Boundary scripts to practice aloud:

- "That's outside what we do. I can share a public resource if that would help."
- "We don't collect participant information. The count and timing are all we need."
- "We don't accept payment for referrals."
- "I'm here for the order today; your staff is the right contact for that."
- "Speaking at events isn't part of our service."

# Appendix B: Buyer Interview Guide

Introduction: "I'm starting a small business that does [task] for organizations. I'd like to learn how you handle [task] today. I won't ask about clients or participants. Would 15 minutes work?"

Questions:

1. Tell me about the last time you [did the task]. What happened, step by step?
2. Who did the work, and how long did it take?
3. What did it cost, including materials and staff time?
4. What went wrong, or what was frustrating?
5. What have you tried to make it easier?
6. How often does this come up in a year?
7. Who approves spending on something like this?
8. Would you be open to a sample order or a follow-up conversation?

Rules: listen more than you talk; ask about the past, not hypothetical futures; do not pitch until the end; record process and costs only; decline any participant details.

# Glossary

Accessibility: Designing materials and services so people with disabilities can use them, including online.

After-action review: A short structured review of what was intended, what happened, why, and what to change.

Beneficiary: A person who benefits indirectly from a service but does not buy or directly use it.

Buyback rate: Dan Martell's term for an hourly value derived from annual income, used to decide which tasks to delegate.

Buyer: The person or organization that selects and pays for a service.

Clean slate law: A state law that automatically seals or clears certain criminal records.

Contribution: Price minus direct costs; the amount available for fixed costs and profit.

Data minimization: Collecting only the information needed to deliver a service and meet legal obligations.

Dry run: A rehearsal of the full service without a customer at risk.

Dual relationship: Holding two different roles, such as peer supporter and vendor, with the same person.

Hand-off: A brief, respectful redirection to a qualified resource for help the business does not provide.

Nonclinical service: A service that does not involve diagnosis, treatment, counseling, or other care.

Open Hiring: Greyston Bakery's practice of hiring for entry-level jobs without interviews, background checks, or resumes.

Recovery: In SAMHSA's working definition, a process of change through which individuals improve their health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and strive to reach their full potential.

Recovery-ready workplace: An employer approach promoted by the U.S. Department of Labor that supports workers in or seeking recovery.

Scope card: A short document stating what a business does, does not do, and says instead.

Second-chance employer: An employer that considers applicants regardless of criminal history.

Small test: A limited number of paid orders under a written offer with predefined measures.

Stop rule: A condition set in advance that triggers a pause or change.

Substantiation: Evidence that supports an advertising claim.

User: The person who receives or uses a service.
