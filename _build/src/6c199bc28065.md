# How to Use This Book

Coaching is one of the easiest businesses to start and one of the hardest to sustain. Anyone can call themselves a coach; very few build practices that deliver consistent client outcomes, earn a reasonable living, and survive their second year. The difference is rarely charisma. It is design: a clear client and outcome, an offer built around that outcome, honest pricing, a repeatable delivery method, ethical marketing and sales, and operations that protect the coach's time.

This book is written for new and early-stage coaches in any specialty (business, career, leadership, wellness, life transitions), for professionals considering coaching as a second career, and for experienced coaches who want to rebuild a practice on firmer foundations. It treats coaching as a professional service business that deserves the same rigor as consulting, accounting, or therapy, while respecting the boundaries between them.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Define coaching precisely and keep your work inside its professional and legal boundaries.
- Select a niche with a specific client, problem, and measurable outcome.
- Design offers using a structured value framework and package them for delivery.
- Price your services from unit economics instead of guesswork.
- Write client agreements, handle confidentiality, and comply with advertising rules on earnings claims and testimonials.
- Run sessions with a repeatable structure and measure progress.
- Market and sell without hype, and track client results ethically.
- Protect your time using delegation frameworks popularized by Dan Martell.
- Decide when and how to scale from one-to-one to group programs.
- Execute a 90-day launch or relaunch plan.

## How Each Chapter Is Built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, explains its concepts in plain language, presents a step-by-step framework, and includes at least one worked example with numbers. Worked examples use invented coaches and clients and are labelled "illustrative." Real, publicly documented cases, such as Dan Martell's Buyback Loop and SaaS Academy, and Federal Trade Commission enforcement actions against coaching schemes, appear in case-study boxes with citations. Each chapter ends with a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. The appendices contain templates you can use directly: a niche scorecard, offer canvas, pricing calculator, client agreement outline, intake form, session notes, discovery-call script, and launch calendar.

## Sources and Limits

Industry data come from the International Coaching Federation (ICF); legal and regulatory statements come from official sources such as the FTC, IRS, and SBA; frameworks from named practitioners are drawn from their own published material and interviews. Citations appear as bracketed numbers keyed to the References chapter.

# Chapter 1: Coaching as a Profession

:::objectives
- Define coaching using a recognized professional definition.
- Distinguish coaching from consulting, mentoring, training, and therapy.
- Describe the size and structure of the coaching profession using ICF research.
- Identify the scope boundaries that protect clients and coaches.
:::

## A Working Definition

The International Coaching Federation, the largest professional body for coaches, defines coaching as "partnering with Clients in a thought-provoking and creative process that inspires them to maximize their personal and professional potential" [1]. Three features of that definition matter for business design. First, coaching is a partnership: the client does the work and owns the decisions. Second, it is a process, which implies structure over time rather than a single conversation. Third, it is oriented toward potential and future action, not diagnosis of the past.

## Coaching and Its Neighbors

| Discipline | Primary role of the professional | Who supplies answers | Typical regulation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coaching | Facilitates client's thinking, goals, accountability | Mostly the client | Largely unregulated; voluntary credentials |
| Consulting | Diagnoses problems, recommends or implements solutions | The consultant | Varies by field |
| Mentoring | Shares personal experience and advice | The mentor | None |
| Training | Teaches defined skills or knowledge | The trainer | Varies |
| Therapy / counseling | Treats mental-health conditions | Clinical process | State licensure required |

Many successful coaching businesses blend coaching with some teaching or advising, especially in business coaching. That is acceptable if you are honest with clients about which mode you are in and if you stay out of licensed territory such as psychotherapy, medical treatment, legal advice, and investment advice.

## The Profession by the Numbers

The ICF's 2025 Global Coaching Study estimates a record 122,974 coach practitioners worldwide, up about 15% since 2023, and estimated total annual revenue of $5.34 billion USD [2]. The study's final report states that individual active coach practitioners earned average annual revenue of $49,283 USD from coaching activities [3]. Averages hide wide variation: many coaches work part-time, and a smaller number earn far more. The ICF also reports that the number of coaches grew 54% from 2019 to 2023 [2], which means competition is increasing and differentiation matters.

## Scope Boundaries

Scope boundaries protect clients from harm and coaches from liability. Write yours down and share them in your client agreement. Typical boundaries include:

- **Mental health.** If a client shows signs of depression, trauma, substance dependence, or risk of self-harm, pause coaching on that topic and refer to a licensed professional or crisis resource.
- **Medical.** Wellness coaches support behavior change; they do not diagnose or prescribe.
- **Legal, tax, investment.** Business coaches can help clients think through decisions, but should refer clients to licensed professionals for specific advice.
- **Confidentiality limits.** Explain what you keep confidential and the legal exceptions in your jurisdiction.

:::example Classifying Client Requests (illustrative)
Marisol, an invented career coach, receives four requests in one week.

1. "Help me decide between two job offers." This is coaching: she facilitates the client's criteria and decision.
2. "Rewrite my résumé." This is a service (writing) that she may offer as an add-on, clearly priced separately.
3. "I can't get out of bed most days and I've stopped eating." This suggests a possible mental-health condition. She expresses care, pauses coaching on this topic, and provides referral resources.
4. "Should I put my severance in an index fund?" This is investment advice. She helps the client list questions for a licensed financial adviser.

By classifying requests quickly, Marisol protects her clients and keeps her service well defined.
:::

:::checklist Professional Boundary Checklist
- I can state the ICF definition of coaching in my own words.
- My agreement explains what coaching is and is not.
- I have a written referral list for mental-health, medical, legal, and financial issues.
- I know what I will do if a client discloses risk of harm.
- I describe any non-coaching services (teaching, consulting, writing) separately.
:::

:::summary
Coaching is a partnership in a structured process aimed at a client's potential. It differs from consulting, mentoring, training, and especially therapy, which is licensed. The ICF estimates about 123,000 coach practitioners worldwide and $5.34 billion in annual revenue, with average practitioner revenue near $49,000, so differentiation and professionalism matter. Clear scope boundaries protect clients and coaches alike.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choosing a Niche and Defining the Outcome

:::objectives
- Explain why a specific niche increases referrals, pricing power, and delivery quality.
- Define a niche using the client-problem-outcome formula.
- Score candidate niches on demand, access, credibility, and ability to pay.
- Write a one-sentence positioning statement.
:::

## Why Specificity Wins

A generalist "life coach" competes with everyone and is remembered by no one. A coach who helps "newly promoted engineering managers lead their first team" is easy to refer, easy to find, and able to build materials that fit the client's situation precisely. Specificity also improves delivery: you meet the same problems repeatedly, so your methods improve faster.

## The Client-Problem-Outcome Formula

A niche has three parts:

1. **Client:** a definable group you can reach, described by role, stage, or situation.
2. **Problem:** a painful, specific challenge the client already recognizes and wants solved.
3. **Outcome:** a measurable or observable change the client wants within a defined time.

Example: "I help independent physical therapy clinic owners (client) who are stuck doing all the admin themselves (problem) build a front-desk system so they can treat patients full-time within 90 days (outcome)."

## Scoring Candidate Niches

Rate each candidate from 1 to 5 on five criteria.

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Pain | Does the client feel the problem strongly and urgently? |
| Ability to pay | Does the client or their employer have budget? |
| Access | Can I reach these people through networks, communities, or channels? |
| Credibility | Do I have relevant experience, training, or proof? |
| Interest | Will I enjoy working with this group for years? |

A strong niche typically scores 4 or 5 on pain, ability to pay, and access. Credibility can be built; low pain cannot be fixed.

## Validating Before Building

Before designing a program, hold 10 to 15 conversations with people in the candidate niche. Ask about the problem, what they have tried, what it costs them, and what a solution would be worth. Do not pitch. You are collecting the language clients use, which will later shape your marketing.

:::example Scoring Three Niches (illustrative)
Jordan, an invented former HR director, considers three niches.

| Niche | Pain | Pay | Access | Credibility | Interest | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| General life coaching | 2 | 2 | 3 | 2 | 4 | 13 |
| First-time managers at tech companies | 4 | 4 (employer budgets) | 4 (former colleagues) | 5 | 4 | 21 |
| Retirees seeking purpose | 3 | 3 | 2 | 2 | 4 | 14 |

Jordan chooses first-time managers. In 12 validation calls, she hears the phrase "I got promoted because I was a good engineer, and now I have no idea how to run a one-on-one" seven times. That phrase becomes the opening line of her website.
:::

# Chapter 3: Designing the Offer

:::objectives
- Apply Alex Hormozi's value equation to coaching offers.
- Translate a client outcome into a structured program with milestones.
- Choose a delivery format: one-to-one, group, hybrid, or intensive.
- Package support elements without inflating promises.
:::

An offer is more than a price and a number of sessions. It is the complete set of what the client receives, how it leads to the outcome, and what makes it believable.

## The Value Equation

Entrepreneur and author Alex Hormozi describes four drivers of value in what he calls the Value Equation: the dream outcome, the perceived likelihood of achievement, the time delay between purchase and result, and the effort and sacrifice required of the customer [4]. In his formulation, value rises as the dream outcome and perceived likelihood increase and as time delay and effort decrease.

Applied to coaching, the equation suggests four design questions:

| Driver | Design question for a coach | Ethical guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | What specific change does the client want? | Describe outcomes the client controls; avoid promising results that depend on others |
| Perceived likelihood | What makes success more likely and credible? | Use real methods and honest evidence, never fabricated testimonials |
| Time delay | How can the client see early progress? | Design genuine early wins, not artificial ones |
| Effort and sacrifice | How can I reduce friction for the client? | Templates, tools, and support, not shortcuts that skip necessary work |

## From Outcome to Program

1. **State the end outcome** in observable terms ("runs weekly one-on-ones with all five direct reports using a consistent agenda").
2. **Work backward to milestones**, typically three to six, each representing a capability or result.
3. **Assign sessions and resources** to each milestone.
4. **Define the client's work** between sessions.
5. **Decide how progress will be measured** at each milestone.

## Delivery Formats

| Format | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| One-to-one | Deep personalization; highest price per client | Time-bound capacity |
| Group | Peer learning; lower price per client; more capacity | Less personalization; group dynamics |
| Hybrid (group plus periodic one-to-one) | Balance of scale and depth | More complex to run |
| Intensive (half-day or full-day) | Fast progress; premium pricing | Requires strong preparation |
| Self-paced course plus coaching | Scalable content | Completion rates vary |

## Support Elements

Common elements include session recordings, worksheets, messaging support between sessions, a resource library, and assessments. Include only what you will deliver reliably. Unlimited messaging, for example, can erode your time; consider defined response windows.

:::example Designing a 12-Week Program (illustrative)
Jordan's outcome for first-time managers: "Within 12 weeks, you will run effective weekly one-on-ones, give feedback using a consistent model, and have a written development plan for each direct report."

| Weeks | Milestone | Sessions | Client work |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | Manager identity and expectations | 2 x 60 min | Interview own manager on expectations |
| 3-5 | One-on-one system | 3 x 60 min | Run three one-on-ones; log notes |
| 6-8 | Feedback model | 3 x 60 min | Deliver two feedback conversations |
| 9-11 | Development plans | 2 x 60 min + review | Draft plans with each report |
| 12 | Review and next 90 days | 1 x 90 min | Self-assessment |

Support: templates for one-on-ones and feedback, messaging on weekdays with a one-business-day response window. Measurement: a manager-effectiveness self-assessment at weeks 1, 6, and 12, plus a count of one-on-ones completed.
:::

An offer is the full path from purchase to outcome. Hormozi's value equation highlights the dream outcome, perceived likelihood, time delay, and effort; coaches should apply it within ethical guardrails. Work backward from an observable outcome to milestones, sessions, client work, and measurement, and choose a format that balances depth with capacity.

# Chapter 4: Pricing and Unit Economics

:::objectives
- Calculate the revenue required to meet your income and business costs.
- Translate required revenue into client capacity and price.
- Compare hourly, package, and retainer pricing.
- Account for self-employment taxes and non-billable time.
:::

Many coaches set prices by copying competitors or by guessing what feels comfortable. A more reliable approach starts with what the business must earn and works forward to price.

## Step 1: Required Revenue

Required revenue = target owner pay + business expenses + taxes + a profit or reserve margin.

Self-employed people in the United States generally pay self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) on net earnings in addition to income tax; the IRS explains that self-employed individuals generally must file an annual return and pay estimated taxes quarterly [5]. The Taxpayer Advocate Service notes that self-employment tax applies when net earnings from self-employment are $400 or more and that one-half of the self-employment tax is deductible [6]. Work with a tax professional to estimate your effective rate; many sole proprietors set aside a fixed percentage of every payment.

## Step 2: Capacity

Not every working hour is billable. Marketing, sales calls, administration, preparation, and professional development consume substantial time. If you plan 40 working hours per week, a realistic share of client-facing hours for a solo coach is often well under half, especially in the first year.

## Step 3: Price

Price per client = required revenue / number of clients you can serve and will realistically enroll.

## Pricing Models

| Model | Description | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly or per session | Pay per session | Simple; low commitment | Rewards time, not outcomes; churn |
| Package | Fixed fee for a defined program | Aligns with outcome; predictable | Requires clear scope |
| Retainer | Monthly fee for ongoing access | Recurring revenue | Must keep delivering value |
| Corporate contract | Employer pays for multiple clients | Larger contracts | Longer sales cycles; procurement |

Packages generally fit outcome-based coaching best, because the client buys a result pathway rather than hours.

:::example Pricing From Unit Economics (illustrative)
Jordan wants $80,000 in annual owner pay. Business expenses (software, insurance, professional development, marketing, accounting) total $14,000. She estimates taxes and reserves at 30% of revenue. Required revenue R satisfies R = $80,000 + $14,000 + 0.30R, so 0.70R = $94,000 and R = about $134,300.

Capacity: she can deliver 15 client hours per week across 46 working weeks, or 690 hours. Her 12-week program requires about 14 hours per client including preparation, so her maximum is about 49 clients per year. She assumes she will realistically enroll 30 in year one.

Price = $134,300 / 30 = about $4,480. She prices the program at $4,500, with a three-payment plan of $1,550 each ($4,650 total). If she enrolls only 20 clients, revenue is $90,000, which covers expenses and taxes but leaves owner pay of about $49,000; she plans accordingly and does not assume best-case enrollment. These numbers are illustrative and not a prediction.
:::

# Chapter 5: Legal, Ethical, and Advertising Foundations

:::objectives
- Choose a business structure and complete basic registrations.
- Draft a client agreement covering scope, fees, cancellation, confidentiality, and referrals.
- Apply professional ethics standards to common situations.
- Comply with FTC rules on earnings claims and testimonials.
:::

## Business Setup

The SBA's planning guidance explains that a business plan can follow a traditional or lean format and should meet the owner's needs [7]. For a coaching practice, the minimum setup usually includes choosing a structure (sole proprietorship or LLC are common for solo coaches), registering a business name if required, obtaining any local business license, opening a separate bank account, and arranging professional liability insurance. Requirements vary by state and city; consult an attorney or your local Small Business Development Center.

## The Client Agreement

A written agreement prevents most disputes. Appendix D provides an outline. Essential terms:

- Description of coaching and what it is not (including the non-therapy statement).
- Program scope: sessions, duration, support elements, response windows.
- Fees, payment schedule, and refund or cancellation policy.
- Rescheduling and missed-session rules.
- Confidentiality and its limits.
- Client responsibilities and acknowledgment that results depend on the client's actions.
- Referral clause for issues outside scope.
- Recording consent, if sessions are recorded.
- Termination by either party.

## Professional Ethics

The ICF Code of Ethics sets ethical standards for ICF professionals in any coaching-related interaction and defines the coaching relationship as one established under an agreement or contract that defines responsibilities and expectations [1]. Even if you are not an ICF member, its structure (clear agreements, confidentiality, conflict-of-interest management, honest representation of qualifications) is a sound baseline.

## Earnings Claims and Testimonials

Business and "make money" coaching is an area of active FTC enforcement. In 2021 the FTC issued a Notice of Penalty Offenses concerning money-making opportunities, stating that it is an unfair or deceptive practice to make false, misleading, or deceptive representations about the profits or earnings that may be anticipated by participants [8]. Companies that receive such notices and violate them can face civil penalties.

:::case FTC Action Against Automators AI (2024)
In February 2024 the FTC announced that the owners of Automators AI, a money-making scheme that claimed to use artificial intelligence to boost earnings for consumers' e-commerce storefronts, agreed to surrender millions in assets to settle FTC charges involving bogus earnings promises. The businesses and two owners also faced a lifetime ban on selling business opportunities or coaching programs involving e-commerce [9].

Lesson for the reader: framing a program as "coaching" does not exempt it from consumer protection law. Earnings promises must be truthful and substantiated, and typical results must not be misrepresented.
:::

:::case FTC Action Against Click Profit (2025)
In August 2025 the FTC announced that operators of an e-commerce business opportunity scheme known as Click Profit would be permanently banned from the industry and required to turn over personal and business assets under a proposed settlement resolving FTC allegations [10].

Lesson for the reader: high-ticket programs that sell income dreams draw regulatory scrutiny. Coaches should describe their process and the client's role, not promise money.
:::

## Practical Rules for Coaches

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Describe your process and what clients do | Promise income, revenue, or specific results |
| Use real testimonials with permission | Invent, alter, or pay for undisclosed testimonials |
| Disclose any material connection with an endorser | Present exceptional results as typical |
| Keep evidence for any objective claim | Use "guaranteed" unless you actually guarantee a refund on stated terms |
| State that results vary and depend on the client | Pressure prospects with false scarcity |

:::example Rewriting a Risky Sales Page (illustrative)
Before: "My clients add $10K a month within 90 days. Guaranteed."

After: "In this 12-week program, we build your client-acquisition system together: a clear offer, a weekly outreach routine, and a sales conversation framework. You will do the outreach and conversations; I provide structure, feedback, and accountability. Results depend on your market and effort. If you complete all sessions and assignments and feel the program did not deliver the promised structure, you may request a refund within 14 days of the final session."

The revised version describes deliverables, the client's role, and a specific, honest guarantee about the program itself rather than income.
:::

- Referral list for out-of-scope issues.

:::

:::summary
Set up a legal entity or sole proprietorship with appropriate registrations, a separate account, and insurance. Use a written client agreement that defines scope, fees, confidentiality, and referrals. The FTC treats false earnings claims as unlawful and has banned operators of coaching-style money-making schemes; describe your process and the client's role, never promise income.

# Chapter 6: The Delivery System

:::objectives
- Use a consistent session structure.
- Conduct a thorough intake and baseline assessment.
- Track progress with leading and lagging indicators.
- Handle stalled clients and difficult moments professionally.
:::

## Intake and Baseline

Intake establishes goals, context, constraints, and baseline measures. A good intake form (Appendix E) asks about the client's goal in their own words, what success would look like in 90 days, past attempts, obstacles, support systems, preferred communication, and anything the coach should know about scope (for example, current therapy). Record a baseline for each outcome you will measure.

## A Repeatable Session Structure

| Segment | Time (60-minute session) | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Check-in | 5 minutes | Presence; what happened since last session |
| Review commitments | 10 minutes | What was done, learned, blocked |
| Focus topic | 30 minutes | Coaching on the most important issue |
| Commitments | 10 minutes | Specific actions, deadlines, and measures |
| Close | 5 minutes | Key insight; confirm next session |

Consistency helps clients prepare and makes your notes comparable over time.

## Coaching Techniques

Core techniques include powerful open questions ("What would make this week a success?"), reflective listening, reframing, goal clarification (for example, making goals specific and time-bound), accountability structures, and feedback with permission. Business coaches often add teaching moments; signal the switch ("May I share a framework that has helped others with this?").

## Measuring Progress

Track two kinds of indicators:

- **Leading indicators**: actions the client controls (outreach messages sent, one-on-ones held, workouts completed).
- **Lagging indicators**: outcomes (promotions, clients signed, energy ratings), which depend partly on factors outside the client's control.

Review both at milestones. Leading indicators tell you whether the plan is being executed; lagging indicators tell you whether the plan works.

## Stalled Clients

When a client repeatedly misses commitments, explore rather than scold: Is the goal still important? Is the commitment too large? Is something outside coaching interfering? Adjust the plan, shrink the next step, or, when appropriate, pause the engagement.

:::example Tracking a Client Over 12 Weeks (illustrative)
Client: invented new manager Sam. Leading indicator: weekly one-on-ones held (target 5 per week). Lagging indicator: team engagement self-rating from a short pulse survey (scale 1-10).

| Week | One-on-ones held | Team pulse average |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 5.8 |
| 4 | 3 | 6.1 |
| 8 | 5 | 6.7 |
| 12 | 5 | 7.0 |

The coach attributes nothing to herself directly; she reports that Sam's consistency rose from 1 to 5 sessions per week and that team pulse ratings rose during the same period, while noting other factors could contribute.
:::

# Chapter 7: Ethical Marketing and Positioning

:::objectives
- Build a marketing system around referrals, content, partnerships, and direct outreach.
- Write messaging using client language from validation calls.
- Measure marketing with a simple funnel.
- Avoid manipulative tactics.
:::

## Four Channels for Coaches

| Channel | How it works | Time to results | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Referrals and network | Past colleagues, clients, and professionals refer | Fast if network exists | Low |
| Content | Articles, videos, podcasts, social posts teaching your method | Slow, compounding | Time |
| Partnerships | Associations, employers, complementary professionals | Medium | Low to medium |
| Direct outreach | Personalized messages to ideal clients | Fast | Time |
| Paid ads | Targeted advertising | Fast but costly to learn | Medium to high |

Most new coaches should start with referrals, direct outreach, and one content channel before paying for ads.

## Messaging

Use the exact phrases you heard in validation calls. Describe the problem as the client experiences it, the outcome they want, how your process works, and what they do next. Avoid jargon and superlatives.

## The Coaching Funnel

Track a simple funnel monthly: people reached, conversations started, discovery calls booked, proposals made, clients enrolled. Conversion rates between stages tell you where to improve.

:::example Reading a Funnel (illustrative)
Jordan's month: 200 people reached through LinkedIn posts and messages, 40 conversations, 10 discovery calls, 6 proposals, 2 clients. Call-to-client conversion is 20%; reach-to-call is 5%. Her content attracts attention, but few conversations become calls. She adds a clear invitation to book a call at the end of conversations where the prospect describes the problem, and tracks whether reach-to-call improves next month.
:::

:::checklist Ethical Marketing Checklist
- Messaging uses real client language.
- No income or result promises.
- Testimonials are real, permitted, and representative.
- Scarcity statements (limited spots, deadlines) are true.
- Funnel numbers tracked monthly.
:::

:::summary
Coaches typically grow first through referrals, direct outreach, partnerships, and one content channel. Messaging built on client language outperforms clever copy, and a monthly funnel shows where to improve. Ethical marketing avoids false promises, fake scarcity, and misleading testimonials.
:::

# Chapter 8: The Enrollment Conversation

:::objectives
- Structure a discovery call that serves the prospect whether or not they buy.
- Ask diagnostic questions that clarify fit.
- Present an offer and price clearly.
- Handle objections honestly and decline poor-fit clients.
:::

The sales conversation in coaching is best understood as an enrollment conversation: a structured exploration of whether the prospect's goal, readiness, and resources fit your program. A good call is useful even when the answer is no.

## Discovery Call Structure (45 minutes)

| Stage | Time | Purpose | Sample question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frame | 3 min | Agenda and permission | "Shall we spend 40 minutes on where you are and where you want to be, then decide together whether working together makes sense?" |
| Current state | 10 min | Situation and problem | "What is happening right now that made you book this call?" |
| Desired state | 7 min | Outcome and why it matters | "If we were talking 12 weeks from now and things had gone well, what would be different?" |
| Gap and obstacles | 8 min | What has blocked progress | "What have you tried, and what got in the way?" |
| Fit check | 5 min | Readiness and resources | "How much time each week can you commit?" |
| Offer | 7 min | Program, price, terms | Describe milestones, sessions, investment |
| Decision | 5 min | Next step | "What questions do you have before deciding?" |

## Presenting the Offer

Summarize the prospect's goal in their words, connect each milestone of your program to it, state the price plainly, and stop talking. Offer a payment plan if you have one. Provide the agreement in writing.

## Handling Objections

Treat objections as questions to understand, not obstacles to overcome.

| Objection | Clarifying question | Honest response options |
|---|---|---|
| "It's too expensive." | "Compared with what?" | Explain the value; offer payment plan; suggest a lower-cost resource if truly unaffordable |
| "I need to think about it." | "What specifically would you like to think through?" | Address the specific concern; agree on a follow-up date |
| "I need to ask my spouse/manager." | "What will they want to know?" | Provide a summary they can share |
| "I'm not sure it will work for me." | "What would make you confident?" | Explain your process; share representative testimonials; describe your refund policy |

## Declining Poor-Fit Clients

Decline if the prospect needs therapy rather than coaching, cannot commit the necessary time, expects guaranteed results, or would be stretched financially in a way that creates hardship. Declining protects your reputation and the client.

:::example A Decision Point (illustrative)
A prospect on Jordan's call says, "I'd love to join, but I'd have to put it on a credit card and I'm already behind on rent." Jordan responds that she does not want coaching to add financial stress, suggests two free manager resources, and offers to reconnect in three months. The prospect later refers a colleague who enrolls. Whether or not such referrals occur, declining was the ethical choice.
:::

# Chapter 9: Client Results, Retention, and Testimonials

:::objectives
- Run milestone reviews and end-of-program reviews.
- Design renewal or alumni offers that serve continued client goals.
- Collect testimonials and case studies ethically.
- Track retention and satisfaction metrics.
:::

## Milestone Reviews

At each milestone, review leading and lagging indicators, ask what is working, and adjust. Milestone reviews make progress visible, which sustains motivation and reduces cancellations.

## End-of-Program Review

In the final session, compare baseline and current measures, identify the client's most important learning, and set a plan for the next 90 days. Ask whether the client wants continued support. Offer renewal only if there is a real next goal.

## Retention Options

| Option | Fit |
|---|---|
| Renewal of core program on a new goal | Clients with a clearly defined next outcome |
| Lighter monthly maintenance | Clients wanting accountability |
| Alumni group | Peer support at lower price |
| Graduation | Clients who have what they need |

## Ethical Testimonials and Case Studies

1. Ask for feedback first, testimonials second.
2. Request permission in writing, specifying where it will appear.
3. Use the client's own words; edit only for length with approval.
4. Avoid presenting atypical results as typical; add context such as "results vary."
5. Disclose any incentive (for example, a discount for a video testimonial).
6. Protect confidentiality: anonymize unless the client agrees to be named.

## Metrics

Track completion rate (clients who finish), renewal rate, referral rate, and satisfaction scores (for example, a short survey at the end of the program).

:::example Measuring Retention (illustrative)
In a year, Jordan enrolls 24 clients. 21 complete the program (88% completion). 8 renew for a second engagement (38% of completers). 6 refer at least one person (29%). Her end-of-program survey averages 8.9 out of 10. Seeing that completion is high but renewals moderate, she designs a lighter monthly accountability option for completers who want ongoing support, priced at $300 per month.
:::

:::checklist Testimonial Permission Checklist
- Client asked for feedback before testimonial.
- Written permission specifying where testimonial appears.
- Wording approved by client.
- Context added if result is unusual.
- Any incentive disclosed.
- Name or anonymity per client's choice.
:::

:::summary
Milestone and end-of-program reviews make progress visible and inform renewals. Offer continued support only for real next goals. Collect testimonials with written permission, accurate wording, context, and disclosure of incentives, and track completion, renewal, referral, and satisfaction.
:::

# Chapter 10: Protecting Your Time: Lessons From Dan Martell

:::objectives
- Explain Dan Martell's Buyback Principle, Buyback Rate, and Buyback Loop.
- Use the Camcorder Method to document repeatable tasks.
- Apply the 1-3-1 Rule to team problem-solving.
- Design a weekly schedule that protects client and growth time.
:::

Solo coaches often become the bottleneck in their own business: they deliver sessions, write content, answer email, invoice, schedule, and manage technology. Dan Martell, a software entrepreneur and coach to software founders, has published a set of frameworks for this problem in his book Buy Back Your Time and related interviews.

:::case Dan Martell's Buyback Framework
Martell describes launching SaaS Academy, a growth program for software founders, in 2015 [11]; Precision, a software company, states that it acquired SaaS Academy in February 2026 and describes it as the largest coaching program for B2B SaaS founders [12]. In a Genius Network interview with Joe Polish, Martell outlined a three-step "Buyback Loop": audit your calendar for tasks that drain time and energy; transfer low-value tasks by delegating or outsourcing them, using his "Camcorder Method" of recording yourself doing a task so others can create the process documentation; and fill your schedule with higher-value work [13]. The same interview discusses his "1-3-1 Rule" for problem-solving with a team [13].

In an Entrepreneur article, Martell explains his "buyback rate," which starts from the annual income a person currently earns [14]; summaries of his book describe the calculation as annual pay divided by about 2,000 working hours, then divided by four, so that tasks someone else could do for a quarter of your effective rate are candidates for delegation [15].

Lesson for the reader: these are one practitioner's frameworks and his results are his own. Their value for a coach is a disciplined way to decide what to stop doing personally.
:::

## The Buyback Loop Applied to a Coaching Practice

**Audit.** For two weeks, log your time in 30-minute blocks. Mark each activity as energizing or draining, and estimate what it would cost to pay someone else to do it.

**Transfer.** Delegate draining, low-cost tasks first: scheduling, invoicing, transcription, social media formatting, inbox triage. Use the Camcorder Method: record your screen while doing the task and narrating your decisions, then have the assistant write the step-by-step procedure.

**Fill.** Use reclaimed time for client delivery, relationship-building, content that teaches your method, and program improvement.

## The 1-3-1 Rule

When a team member brings a problem, the 1-3-1 approach asks them to bring one clearly defined problem, three possible options, and one recommendation. Even a solo coach with a part-time assistant benefits: it builds the assistant's judgment and reduces interruptions.

## Weekly Schedule Design

| Block | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Client days | Sessions grouped | Tuesday and Thursday |
| Growth block | Marketing, partnerships, content | Monday morning |
| Admin block | Finance, email, operations (shrinking via delegation) | Friday afternoon |
| Development block | Learning, supervision, program design | Wednesday morning |
| Recovery | Unscheduled time | Wednesday afternoon |

:::example Calculating a Buyback Rate and Delegation Plan (illustrative)
Jordan's current annual owner pay is $80,000. Effective hourly rate: $80,000 / 2,000 = $40. Buyback rate: $40 / 4 = $10 per hour. In practice, she finds a part-time virtual assistant at $18 per hour, above her buyback rate, so she applies the logic with judgment: she delegates only tasks that both drain her energy and free time for revenue-producing work.

Audit results: 6 hours per week on scheduling, invoicing, and formatting posts. Delegating these costs $108 per week. She uses 4 of the 6 reclaimed hours for outreach and partnerships. If that time produced even one additional client every two months at $4,500, the assistant's annual cost of about $5,000 would be covered; this is a decision rule, not a forecast.
:::

:::summary
Dan Martell's Buyback Loop (audit, transfer, fill), the Camcorder Method, and the 1-3-1 Rule offer a structured way to stop being the bottleneck. Coaches can audit their time, delegate draining low-value tasks, document processes by recording them, and fill reclaimed time with delivery and growth work, while applying the frameworks with judgment.

# Chapter 11: Scaling Beyond One-to-One

:::objectives
- Recognize the signals that a practice is ready to scale.
- Design a group program that preserves outcomes.
- Evaluate hiring associate coaches.
- Understand the trade-offs of courses and memberships.
:::

## Signals of Readiness

Consider scaling when your calendar is consistently full, your method produces consistent outcomes, clients ask similar questions, and you have documented your process. Scaling an unproven method multiplies problems.

## Group Program Design

1. Start with your proven one-to-one milestones.
2. Convert teaching moments into short recorded lessons.
3. Use live group sessions for coaching, hot seats, and peer accountability.
4. Cap group size to preserve attention (for example, 8 to 15 participants for live coaching).
5. Add limited one-to-one check-ins at critical milestones.
6. Measure outcomes the same way as in one-to-one.

## Economics of Formats

| Format | Price per client | Clients per coach hour | Typical trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| One-to-one | Highest | 1 | Depth vs. capacity |
| Small group (8-15) | Medium | 8-15 | Peer learning vs. personalization |
| Course plus community | Low | Many | Scale vs. completion |
| Associate coaches | Medium to high | Depends on team | Leverage vs. quality control |

## Associate Coaches

Hiring coaches requires documented methods, training, quality assurance (session reviews, client surveys), clear compensation, and attention to worker-classification law. Consult an employment attorney before engaging contractors.

:::example One-to-One vs. Group Economics (illustrative)
One-to-one: 12-week program at $4,500, about 14 coach hours per client, revenue per coach hour about $321.

Group: 12-week program at $1,800 per participant, 12 participants, 12 x 90-minute group calls plus 2 x 30-minute individual check-ins per participant. Coach hours: 18 group hours + 12 individual hours + 10 preparation hours = 40. Revenue: $21,600. Revenue per coach hour: $540.

The group format earns more per hour if enrollment reaches 12; at 6 participants, revenue is $10,800 for about 34 hours, or about $318 per hour. Jordan launches the group only after building a waitlist of at least 10 interested past prospects.
:::

:::checklist Scaling Readiness
- Calendar consistently full for three or more months.
- Method documented with milestones and materials.
- Outcome measures consistent across clients.
- Waitlist or demonstrated demand for new format.
- Quality controls defined for group or associate delivery.
:::

:::summary
Scale only a proven, documented method. Group programs can raise revenue per coach hour if enrollment is sufficient, but require design for attention and accountability. Associate coaches add leverage and quality-control obligations. Measure outcomes in every format.
:::

# Glossary

**Buyback Loop.** Dan Martell's three-step cycle of auditing, transferring, and filling time.

**Buyback rate.** In Martell's framework, a dollar threshold for delegation derived from a person's income.

**Camcorder Method.** Recording yourself doing a task so another person can document and take it over.

**Client agreement.** A written contract defining the coaching relationship.

**Coaching.** In the ICF's definition, partnering with clients in a thought-provoking and creative process that inspires them to maximize personal and professional potential.

**Completion rate.** Share of enrolled clients who finish a program.

**Discovery call.** A conversation to explore fit before enrollment.

**Earnings claim.** Any representation about income or profit a customer may achieve.

**Lagging indicator.** An outcome measure influenced by many factors.

**Leading indicator.** An action measure the client controls.

**Milestone.** A defined capability or result within a program.

**Niche.** A specific client, problem, and outcome a coach serves.

**1-3-1 Rule.** Bringing one problem, three options, and one recommendation.

**Package pricing.** A fixed fee for a defined program.

**Retainer.** A recurring fee for ongoing access.

**Scope boundary.** A limit on what a coach will address, with referral for issues outside it.

**Self-employment tax.** U.S. Social Security and Medicare tax on net self-employment earnings.

**Value equation.** Alex Hormozi's framework relating dream outcome, perceived likelihood, time delay, and effort.
