# Chapter 1: How Tax Sales Work

:::objectives
- Describe why counties sell property for unpaid taxes.
- Explain the basic life cycle from default to sale.
- Identify the documents that govern a specific sale.
:::

## Why counties sell property

Property taxes fund schools, roads and local services. When an owner does not pay, the county eventually has the power to collect by selling either the property itself (a tax deed or tax-defaulted property sale) or a lien on the property (a tax lien certificate sale). The goal of the county is to collect the tax, not to sell you a bargain.

## The life cycle, in general terms

1. **Taxes go unpaid.** Penalties and interest begin to accrue.
2. **A waiting period passes.** The length depends on state law and property type.
3. **Notices are sent and published.** Owners and lienholders must usually receive notice.
4. **The sale happens.** In person, by sealed bid, or increasingly online.
5. **Redemption, if any.** Some states let the former owner pay and take the property back after the sale.
6. **Deed and title clearing.** The buyer receives a deed and may need further legal steps to get insurable title.

## A California example

In California, property becomes tax-defaulted if taxes remain unpaid at 12:01 a.m. on July 1. After five years in default (or three years when the property is also subject to a nuisance abatement lien) it becomes subject to the county tax collector's power to sell, and the collector must attempt a sale within four years. Counties must notify the State Controller's Office 45 to 120 days before the sale and publish notice three times in a local newspaper [2]. Los Angeles County's 2026B online auction terms describe the power to sell as applying after five or more years of default for residential or agricultural property and three or more years for non-residential commercial property or vacant land [3].

## The documents that govern a sale

| Document | Where to find it | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| State statute | State legislature website | Sets redemption, notice and what title you receive |
| County terms and conditions | County tax collector or auction platform | Deposits, payment deadlines, fees, refunds, vesting |
| Property list | County or auction platform | Parcel number, minimum bid, basic tax data |
| Recorded documents | County recorder | Deeds, liens, easements, judgments |

:::summary
Counties sell property or liens to collect unpaid taxes. Every sale follows a life cycle set by state law and run under county terms. Before you research any parcel, collect the statute and the sale's terms.
:::

# Chapter 2: Deed States, Lien States and Hybrids

:::objectives
- Distinguish tax deed, tax lien and redeemable deed systems.
- Read a redemption rule and estimate its effect on your timeline.
- Avoid assuming one state's rules apply in another.
:::

## Three broad systems

| System | What you buy | What you hope for | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tax deed | The property, by deed | Ownership after sale | Defects in title, condition, occupancy |
| Tax lien certificate | A lien that earns interest or penalty | Repayment with interest, or a deed after a waiting period | Owner redeems; certificate expires; costs to foreclose |
| Redeemable deed | A deed the owner can undo by paying a premium | Premium if redeemed, property if not | Money tied up during redemption |

Industry summaries classify states differently, and many states mix features; always read the statute rather than relying on a label [4].

## Florida: certificates first, then deed sales

Florida sells tax certificates, and a certificate holder can later apply for a tax deed. At the resulting public auction, the high bidder must post a nonrefundable deposit of 5 percent of the bid or $200, whichever is greater, and pay the full bid plus documentary stamp tax and recording fees within 24 hours, excluding weekends and legal holidays, or the clerk cancels the bids and readvertises [5].

## Texas: deed sales with a right of redemption

In Texas, the former owner of a residence homestead, land designated for agricultural use, or a mineral interest may redeem within two years after the purchaser's deed is recorded, paying the bid and costs plus a premium of 25 percent in the first year or 50 percent in the second. For other property the window is 180 days and the premium may not exceed 25 percent [6].

:::example Illustrative: what a redemption period does to your plan
You win a Texas homestead parcel for $40,000 and spend $1,200 on recording, taxes and insurance. The owner redeems in month 10. You receive your costs plus a 25 percent premium on the aggregate total, but you could not safely renovate or resell during those 10 months. If you had budgeted a 4-month flip, your plan was wrong from the start. Numbers are made up; the premium rule comes from the statute [6].
:::

## California: deed sales, generally no post-sale redemption

California's tax-defaulted property sales convey a deed. The SCO explains the sale process and directs bidders to each county for current terms [2]. Redemption rights end before the sale under California's process, so researching before the sale is everything.

:::checklist Before you study a new state
- I found the state statute for tax sales.
- I know whether the state sells deeds, liens or redeemable deeds.
- I wrote down the redemption period and premium, if any.
- I found the county's terms for the specific sale.
- I logged these in the County Rules Log tab.
:::

:::summary
Deed, lien and redeemable-deed systems create very different risks and timelines. Florida, Texas and California each work differently. Read the statute and log the rules before you research parcels.
:::

# Chapter 3: What Survives a Tax Sale

:::objectives
- List the kinds of liens and rights that may survive a tax deed.
- Explain the federal tax lien redemption right.
- Understand why surplus proceeds belong to the former owner in many cases.
:::

## "Free and clear" has exceptions

A tax deed often wipes out many prior liens, but not all. In California, the deed conveys title free of prior encumbrances except, among others: future installments of taxes and assessments; rights of a taxing agency that did not consent to the sale; certain special assessments not included in the redemption amount; easements, separately held water rights and restrictions of record; unaccepted offers of dedication; certain unpaid 1915 Act improvement bond assessments and Mello-Roos special taxes; and federal IRS liens that federal law does not discharge, even if the tax collector gave proper notice [7].

| Item | May survive? | How to check |
|---|---|---|
| Easements and recorded restrictions | Often | Title search, recorded documents |
| Special assessments or special taxes | Sometimes | County terms, assessment district records |
| Federal tax liens | Sometimes | Title search; IRS notice rules |
| Code enforcement or nuisance liens | Depends on state and county | City and county code enforcement offices |
| HOA assessments | Depends on state law | HOA, title company |
| Future taxes | Yes, going forward | Tax collector |

## The federal tax lien right of redemption

When a nonjudicial sale discharges a federal tax lien after proper notice, the United States may still redeem the property within 120 days after the sale, or within the period allowed by local law if longer [8]. Los Angeles County's terms also warn that IRS liens may not be discharged by the sale [3]. Budget for this possibility on any parcel with a federal tax lien.

## Surplus proceeds and the former owner

In *Tyler v. Hennepin County* (2023), the U.S. Supreme Court held that a county that sold a home for unpaid taxes and kept the surplus beyond the tax debt took property without just compensation [9]. Many states changed their surplus procedures afterwards. For a buyer, the practical point is to read how the county now handles surplus and notice; for anyone tempted by "surplus funds recovery" programs, note that some states regulate finders and their fees.

:::warning Title insurance is not automatic
Many title insurers will not insure a tax deed until a waiting period passes or a quiet title action is completed. Ask a local title company before the sale what it requires for your county.
:::

:::summary
A tax deed is not automatically free and clear. Easements, some assessments and some federal tax liens may survive, and the IRS may have a 120-day redemption right. Check every exception for every parcel.
:::

# Chapter 4: Building Your Parcel List

:::objectives
- Turn a county sale list into a short list worth researching.
- Apply quick screens that remove obviously unsuitable parcels.
- Record every parcel in the Parcel Pipeline tab.
:::

## Start with the official list

Download the property list from the county or the auction platform named in the terms. Copy only the fields you need into the **Parcel Pipeline** tab: parcel number, address or legal description, minimum bid, property type, and status. Lists change; parcels are redeemed or withdrawn up to the sale. Los Angeles County's terms, for example, note that a parcel not activated for bidding may have been redeemed, withdrawn, or reached the end of its bidding period [3].

## Quick screens

| Screen | Remove the parcel if... |
|---|---|
| Access | It is landlocked or has no legal access |
| Use | It is a strip, alley, drainage channel, or common area |
| Size and zoning | You cannot build or use it for your purpose |
| Location | It is in a flood channel or similar restricted area |
| Budget | Minimum bid plus known costs exceeds your budget |
| Distance | You cannot inspect it from the street before the sale |

Los Angeles County warns that parcels may turn out to be part of a street or alley, in a flood control channel, or landlocked, and that listed improvements may no longer exist [3].

:::example Illustrative: from 400 parcels to 12
A county list has 400 parcels. 180 are vacant slivers or common areas; 90 are outside the area you can visit; 70 have minimum bids above your budget; 48 fail access or zoning checks. Twelve remain for full research. The numbers are made up, but the shape is typical of a careful process: most parcels are not worth your time.
:::

# Chapter 5: Title and Record Research

:::objectives
- Pull the recorded documents for a parcel.
- Identify liens that may survive the sale.
- Know when to pay a professional for a title report.
:::

## What to pull

For each short-listed parcel, look up the recorded documents at the county recorder (many counties offer online indexes): the vesting deed, deeds of trust or mortgages, judgments, federal and state tax liens, mechanic's liens, code enforcement liens, easements, covenants and any lis pendens.

| Document | What it tells you | Survives? (check) |
|---|---|---|
| Vesting deed | Who owns it and how | Not applicable |
| Mortgage or deed of trust | Lender's lien | Usually eliminated by a valid tax deed; confirm |
| Federal tax lien | IRS claim | Possibly; 120-day redemption risk [8] |
| Easement | Someone else's right to use part of the land | Often survives [7] |
| Code enforcement lien | City fines or abatement costs | Depends on state and county |
| Special assessment | District charges | Sometimes survives [7] |

## When to pay for a title report

A title report or limited search from a title company or abstractor usually costs far less than a mistake. Order one for every parcel you are serious about, and ask the title company in advance what it requires to insure a tax deed in that county.

# Chapter 6: Condition, Occupancy and Neighbourhood

:::objectives
- Inspect lawfully from public places.
- Estimate repairs when you cannot go inside.
- Plan for occupants respectfully and lawfully.
:::

## Look, do not trespass

Tax-defaulted property is usually sold as is, and bidders may not enter private property. Los Angeles County's terms say bidders should inspect before bidding but may not trespass [3]. Drive by, walk the street, take notes and photos from public places, and talk to neighbours politely if they are willing.

## Estimating repairs you cannot see

| Sign from the street | What it may mean | Allowance approach |
|---|---|---|
| Roof sagging or tarps | Roof replacement, water damage | Use a full roof budget |
| Boarded windows | Vacancy, vandalism | Add for interior damage and cleanup |
| Overgrown lot, debris | Long vacancy | Add trash-out and yard costs |
| Utility meters removed | Utilities off for a long time | Add for systems and permits |
| Fire marks | Possible total loss | Treat as land value only unless proven otherwise |

## Occupants

A property may be occupied by a former owner, family members or tenants. Removing occupants requires lawful process under state and local law. Never use self-help. Budget time and legal fees, and treat people with respect.

:::checklist Drive-by
- I viewed the property from public places only.
- I photographed the exterior and street.
- I noted signs of occupancy.
- I recorded repair allowances in the calculator.
- I checked nearby sales for value, not just an automated estimate.
:::

:::summary
Inspect only from public places, assume the interior is worse than the exterior, and plan for occupants through lawful process.

# Chapter 7: The Money: Your Walk-Away Price

## How the calculator works

The **Max-Bid Calculator** tab starts with your estimated as-is value and subtracts every cost besides the bid: repairs, title search, title clearing, surviving liens, fees, holding costs, selling costs, a contingency and your minimum profit. What remains is the most you can bid and still meet your own rules.

| Input | Example (made up) | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Estimated as-is value | $95,000 | Your own comparable sales |
| Repairs, cleanup | $18,000 | Conservative allowance |
| Title search | $400 | Title company quote |
| Quiet title or clearing | $3,500 | Attorney quote |
| Fees | $300 | County terms |
| Holding (6 months × $450) | $2,700 | Your estimate including redemption time |
| Selling costs (8%) | $7,600 | Commission and closing |
| Contingency (10%) | $9,500 | Unknowns |
| Minimum profit (15%) | $14,250 | Your rule |

:::example Illustrative: the calculator's example parcel
With the example inputs, known costs besides the bid total $42,000. The calculator's maximum bid is $95,000 − $42,000 − $14,250 = $38,750. The opening bid is $8,500, so there is $30,250 of room. Stress tests: if the value is 10% lower, the maximum bid falls to $32,385; if repairs run 25% higher, to $34,250; if holding takes twice as long, to $36,050. If you win at $38,750 you need about $63,650 in cash before any resale money returns. Every number is made up.
:::

## Cash and deposits

Read the deposit and payment rules before you register. Los Angeles County's 2026B online auction required a bid deposit of $5,000 or 10 percent of the bidder's spend limit, whichever is greater, paid in advance by approved methods [3]. Florida requires a deposit at the time of sale and full payment within 24 hours [5]. Missing a deadline can cost you the deposit.

## Prepare a bid sheet

List each parcel you may bid on, its walk-away price and the reason. Print it or keep it beside you. Bid only on parcels you researched.

| Parcel | Opening bid | Walk-away price | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Example A | $8,500 | $38,750 | Title clear except easement |
| Example B | $3,200 | $11,000 | Vacant lot, access confirmed |

## Common mistakes

- Bidding above your walk-away price because others are bidding.
- Bidding on a parcel added late that you did not research.
- Misreading online bidding rules, time extensions or proxy bids.
- Forgetting fees, transfer taxes or a buyer's premium.
- Missing the payment deadline.

# Chapter 9: After the Sale

:::objectives
- Complete payment and recording steps.
- Plan for redemption, title clearing and insurance.
- Decide what to do with the property.
:::

## The first weeks

1. Pay in full by the deadline and keep every receipt.
2. Confirm when and how the deed will be recorded.
3. Insure the property as soon as an insurer will cover it.
4. Secure the property lawfully.
5. Diary any redemption period, including the federal 120-day period where relevant [8].

## Title clearing

Ask a local real estate attorney whether a quiet title action or other step is needed for insurable title, how long it takes, and what it costs. Put the real numbers back into the calculator so your next walk-away price is better informed.

# Glossary

- **Tax deed:** A deed issued after a sale for unpaid property taxes.
- **Tax lien certificate:** A certificate representing a lien for unpaid taxes, sold to an investor.
- **Redemption:** The former owner's right in some states to pay and recover the property.
- **Minimum bid:** The lowest acceptable bid, often the taxes, penalties and costs owed.
- **Quiet title action:** A court action to settle ownership and clear clouds on title.
- **Encumbrance:** A claim or right affecting property, such as a lien or easement.
- **Easement:** A right for someone else to use part of the property for a specific purpose.
- **Vesting:** How the buyer takes title (for example, individually or as a company).
- **Surplus proceeds:** Money from a sale above the amount owed.
- **Walk-away price:** The highest bid you will make, set before the auction.
