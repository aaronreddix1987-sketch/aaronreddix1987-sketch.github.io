# How to Use This Book

This book is a working manual for one specific job: understanding your consumer credit file well enough to correct what is wrong, protect what is right, and build a stronger record over time. It is written for adults in the United States who want to do that work themselves, for small-business owners whose personal credit still affects their access to financing, and for counselors, coaches, and family members who help others through the process.

The phrase "credit repair" carries a great deal of marketing baggage. Advertisements promise to "erase" collections, "add 100 points," or "guarantee" approvals. This book takes a different approach, grounded in the law and in what federal regulators have actually said. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) states plainly that no one can legally remove accurate and timely negative information from a credit report, and that anything a credit repair company can do legally, you can do for yourself at little or no cost [1]. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) gives the same message [2]. Everything that follows is built on that foundation.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

By the end of this book you should be able to:

- Obtain all three of your nationwide credit reports safely and at no cost, and know what each section means.
- Distinguish a genuine error from accurate-but-unwelcome information, and decide which items are worth disputing.
- Write and send disputes that a credit reporting company and a furnisher must take seriously, with proper documentation.
- Track every dispute, follow up on deadlines, and escalate through the CFPB complaint system or a qualified attorney when a dispute is mishandled.
- Recognize illegal credit repair practices, protect yourself from identity theft, and use credit freezes and fraud alerts correctly.
- Understand the main factors in common scoring models and adopt habits that build positive history month by month.
- Run a disciplined 90-day plan and an annual credit review.

## How Each Chapter Is Built

Every chapter follows the same structure so that you can study it like a course module. It opens with learning objectives. It then explains the concepts in plain language and defines technical terms the first time they appear. A step-by-step framework follows, and at least one worked example with numbers shows the framework in use. The people in worked examples are invented and labelled "illustrative"; they show how a step works, not what will happen to you. Where a real, publicly documented case teaches something useful, it appears in a case-study box with a citation to the original source. Each chapter closes with a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions.

The appendices contain tools you can use immediately: dispute letter templates, a dispute log, a budget for paying down revolving balances, an identity-theft recovery checklist, and a 90-day action calendar. Purchasers may copy the worksheets for their own use.

Scoring factors are drawn from FICO's published consumer education. Every source appears with a full web address in the References chapter, and bracketed numbers in the text, such as [1], point to that list. Laws and agency positions change; verify any rule that matters to your decision against the current official source.

# Chapter 1: The Credit Reporting System Explained

:::objectives
- Define a consumer report, a credit score, a consumer reporting agency, and a furnisher.
- Describe how information moves from a lender to a credit file and from a credit file to a decision-maker.
- Explain the core rights the Fair Credit Reporting Act gives consumers.
- Identify how long different types of negative information may be reported.
:::

Before anyone can correct a credit report, they need a clear mental model of the system that produces it. Most frustration in credit repair comes from treating the credit report as a single document controlled by a single company. In reality it is the output of a network of independent businesses, each with its own records and legal duties.

## The Four Parties

There are four main parties in the credit reporting system.

**The consumer** is you: the person the information is about.

**Furnishers** are businesses that send information about consumers to credit reporting companies. Banks, credit card issuers, auto lenders, student loan servicers, mortgage servicers, some landlords, utilities, and debt collectors can all be furnishers. The CFPB describes furnishers as the companies that provide information about you to the credit reporting companies, and lists examples such as your bank, your landlord, and your credit card company [2].

**Consumer reporting agencies (CRAs)**, commonly called credit bureaus, collect furnisher data, organize it into files, and sell reports to authorized users. The three nationwide CRAs are Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. There are also many specialty reporting companies that track things like checking-account history, tenant screening, and employment background; this book focuses on the three nationwide files because they drive most lending decisions.

**Users** are lenders, landlords, insurers, employers (with your permission), and others who obtain your report for a purpose the law allows.

## How Data Flows

A typical cycle works like this. You open a credit card. Each month, the issuer sends a data file to one, two, or all three nationwide CRAs listing your balance, credit limit, payment status, and other fields. Each CRA matches that record to the file it believes is yours, using identifiers such as name, address, date of birth, and Social Security number. When you later apply for a car loan, the lender requests your report from one or more CRAs and may also request a credit score calculated from that report.

Three features of this flow explain most errors:

1. **Furnishers are not required to report to every bureau.** A missing account on one report and its presence on another is not necessarily an error.
2. **Matching is probabilistic.** When identifiers are similar, such as a parent and child with the same name, or a typo in a Social Security number, records can land in the wrong file. The CFPB lists mixed files and accounts belonging to someone else among common errors to look for [3].
3. **Each CRA keeps its own file.** Correcting an item with one bureau does not automatically correct it with the others.

## Reports Versus Scores

A **credit report** is the record. A **credit score** is a number calculated from the record by a scoring model at a particular moment. There is no single "credit score." Different companies sell different models, and the same model can have versions tailored to auto lending, card lending, or mortgages. FICO, one of the best-known model developers, states that its scores are calculated from the data in your credit report and that personal details such as income, age, and where you live are not factors [4].

Because scores are derived from reports, the only durable way to change a score is to change the underlying report: remove inaccurate data through the dispute process, and add positive data through on-time payment and responsible use over time.

## The Legal Framework

The **Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)** is the main federal law governing consumer reports. Among other things, it requires CRAs to follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy, limits who may obtain reports, gives consumers the right to see their files, and creates a dispute process that obliges both the CRA and the furnisher to investigate. The FTC's consumer booklet summarizes that both the credit reporting company and the information provider are responsible for correcting inaccurate or incomplete information, and that disputing mistakes costs nothing [1].

The **Credit Repair Organizations Act (CROA)** governs companies that sell credit repair services. According to the FTC, it prohibits untrue or misleading representations, bars such companies from demanding advance payment, requires written contracts, and gives consumers cancellation rights [5]. Chapter 8 returns to CROA in detail.

The **Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)** and the CFPB's debt collection rule (Regulation F) govern third-party debt collectors. They matter here because collection accounts are often the most damaging items on a report, and collectors must provide validation information that you can use to dispute a debt [6].

## How Long Information Stays

Time is the most powerful "repair" tool, and it is entirely legal. The CFPB explains that credit reporting companies can generally report most negative information for seven years, while positive information may be reported longer [7]. The Federal Reserve notes that a personal bankruptcy may stay for ten years, and that information about a lawsuit or unpaid judgment can be reported for seven years or until the statute of limitations runs out, whichever is longer [8].

| Type of information | General reporting period | Practical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Late payments, charge-offs, most collections | About seven years [7] | Accurate items fade with time; no lawful shortcut exists |
| Chapter 7 bankruptcy | Up to ten years [8] | Plan around it; focus on new positive history |
| Lawsuits and unpaid judgments | Seven years or the statute of limitations, whichever is longer [8] | Confirm dates carefully; errors in dates are disputable |
| Positive, open accounts in good standing | May be reported longer [7] | Keeping old, well-managed accounts open can help |
| Inquiries | Typically shorter; check each bureau's policy | Minor factor for most people |

:::example Mapping One Account Through the System (illustrative)
Dana, an invented consumer, opened a store card in March 2021 with a $1,200 limit. She paid 60 days late once in August 2022 and has paid on time since. The issuer reports to Experian and TransUnion, but not Equifax. When Dana pulls her three reports, she sees the card on two of them with a "60 days late" notation for August 2022. Her Equifax report does not show the card at all.

Applying the concepts: the missing Equifax entry is not an error, because furnishers choose which bureaus to report to. The late payment is accurate, so it is not disputable as inaccurate; under the general seven-year rule it would be expected to stop being reported around 2029. Dana's productive actions are therefore to keep paying on time, keep the balance low relative to the $1,200 limit, and check that the late-payment date and amount are reported identically on both files. If one bureau showed the late payment in a different month, that discrepancy would be worth disputing.
:::

:::summary
The credit report is the product of a network: furnishers send data, credit reporting companies assemble files, and users buy reports and scores. Most errors come from matching problems, furnisher mistakes, or outdated data. Federal law, especially the FCRA, gives you the right to see your files and to dispute inaccurate or incomplete information at no cost. Accurate negative information generally remains for about seven years, and bankruptcy for up to ten. Durable credit improvement therefore has two sources only: correcting genuine errors and building new positive history.
:::

# Chapter 2: Getting Your Reports Safely and Organizing Your File

:::objectives
- Obtain free reports from all three nationwide credit reporting companies through the official channel.
- Recognize imposter sites and paid "free report" offers.
- Know when you are entitled to additional free reports.
- Set up a secure filing system that will support disputes and follow-up.
:::

Every credit project starts with complete, current copies of all three reports. Working from a single report, or from a score summary in a banking app, is the most common reason people miss errors. A score app may show only one bureau's data, may summarize rather than display account details, and may not show the dispute instructions you will need.

## The Official Source

AnnualCreditReport.com describes itself as created by the three major credit reporting agencies as the central source for the credit reports you are entitled to under federal law, and states that you can check your credit for free every week [9]. The CFPB confirms that you can get a free copy of your report from each of the three companies once a week at AnnualCreditReport.com, in addition to your legal right to one free report from each company every 12 months, and lists the phone number (877) 322-8228 for requests by phone [10]. Requests can also be made by mail [11].

Checking your own report is a "soft" inquiry and does not affect your scores.

## Other Free-Report Rights

The FTC notes that you are entitled to a free report if a company takes "adverse action" against you, such as denying credit, insurance, or employment, provided you ask within 60 days of receiving notice of the action [1]. Adverse action notices also name the reporting company that supplied the report, which tells you exactly which file to examine.

## Avoiding Imposters

Many websites use names similar to the official site or advertise "free reports" that convert into paid monitoring subscriptions. The safest practice is to type AnnualCreditReport.com directly into your browser rather than clicking ads or email links. The official site will never call or email you asking for personal information.

## Step-by-Step: The Report Pull

1. **Prepare.** Set aside 45 minutes, a private internet connection, and your previous addresses for the last two years.
2. **Go to the official site directly.** Type the address yourself.
3. **Request all three reports.** Select Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.
4. **Answer verification questions carefully.** These may ask about old loans or addresses. If you fail verification, the site provides instructions for requesting by mail.
5. **Save each report as a PDF immediately.** Name files consistently, for example `2026-11-22_Experian.pdf`.
6. **Note the report or confirmation number** on each, which you will cite in disputes.
7. **Print or annotate.** Mark-up is easier on paper or in a PDF editor.

## Building Your Credit File

A dispute is only as strong as the records behind it. Before you read a single line, set up the system you will use for the next several months.

| Folder | What goes in it | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 01 Reports | Each report, dated, by bureau | Baseline and later comparison |
| 02 Evidence | Statements, payoff letters, court records, ID copies | Supports each dispute |
| 03 Disputes sent | Copies of every letter and online submission | Proves what you said and when |
| 04 Responses | Every bureau and furnisher reply, with envelopes | Proves deadlines and outcomes |
| 05 Mail receipts | Certified mail and return-receipt cards | Proves delivery dates |
| 06 Complaints | CFPB or state complaint numbers and replies | Supports escalation |
| 07 Plan | Budget, payoff plan, 90-day calendar | Tracks positive-history work |

Store digital files in an encrypted, password-protected location. Redact all but the last four digits of account and Social Security numbers on any copy you share with anyone other than the bureau or furnisher.

Marcus, an invented 41-year-old electrician, pulls all three reports on a Sunday morning. He saves them as `2026-03-01_Equifax.pdf`, `2026-03-01_Experian.pdf`, and `2026-03-01_TransUnion.pdf`. That sheet immediately shows two accounts present on only one report and one collection account present on all three. The count differences are not errors in themselves, but the summary sheet gives Marcus a disciplined list of 15 unique accounts to review line by line in Chapter 3, rather than three disorganized documents.

# Chapter 3: Reading a Credit Report Line by Line

:::objectives
- Identify the standard sections of a nationwide credit report.
- Interpret account fields such as status, payment history grid, date of first delinquency, and balance.
- Apply a systematic review method that catches identity, account, and data-management errors.
- Classify each item as accurate, inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable.
:::

Reading a credit report is a skill, and like any professional review it benefits from a fixed method. Accountants call this "ticking and tying": checking every figure against a source and every total against its parts. You will do the same with your own file.

## Sections of a Report

Each bureau formats its report differently, but nearly all contain these sections:

1. **Personal information:** names, addresses, Social Security number (partially masked), date of birth, and sometimes employers.
2. **Accounts (tradelines):** each credit account with its creditor, account type, dates, limit or original amount, balance, payment status, and monthly payment history.
3. **Collections:** accounts transferred or sold to collectors.
4. **Public records:** in current practice usually limited to bankruptcies.
5. **Inquiries:** "hard" inquiries from applications and "soft" inquiries from account reviews and your own checks.
6. **Consumer statements and dispute notations.**
7. **How to dispute:** instructions and contact information.

## Key Account Fields

| Field | What it means | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Date opened | When the account began | Matches your records; not opened by someone else |
| Account type and responsibility | Revolving, installment, mortgage; individual, joint, authorized user | You are not listed as joint owner on an account where you were only an authorized user |
| Credit limit or original amount | Ceiling or loan principal | Correct limit; a missing limit can distort utilization |
| Balance and date reported | Amount owed as of the reporting date | Paid-off accounts show $0 |
| Payment status | Current, 30/60/90+ days late, charged off, collection | Status matches the reality for each month |
| Payment history grid | Month-by-month status | No late marks for months you paid on time |
| Date of first delinquency | When the account first went delinquent and was never brought current | Drives the seven-year reporting clock; must not be "re-aged" |
| Date closed | When the account closed | Closed accounts not shown open, and vice versa |

## Categories of Error

The CFPB groups common errors into identity errors (wrong name, address, or phone; accounts belonging to someone with a similar name; accounts resulting from identity theft), incorrect reporting of account status (closed accounts reported as open, accounts incorrectly reported as late or delinquent, incorrect dates), data-management errors (accurate information repeated, a mixed file, or corrected information that reappears), and balance errors (wrong current balance or credit limit) [3].

Using those categories as a checklist keeps your review complete and keeps your dispute language precise.

## The Four-Pass Review Method

Read each report four times, each time looking for one thing only.

**Pass 1, identity.** Highlight any name, address, employer, or birth date you do not recognize. Unknown addresses can signal mixed files or identity theft.

**Pass 2, ownership.** For every account and collection, ask: is this mine? Did I open it? Am I the right type of owner?

**Pass 3, accuracy.** For accounts you own, compare each field to your statements. Pay particular attention to payment grids and the date of first delinquency.

**Pass 4, duplication and age.** Look for the same debt listed twice (for example, the original creditor and a collector both showing a balance owed), and for negative items older than the general reporting period.

Mark each item with one of four codes:

- **A, accurate:** keep; build around it.
- **I, inaccurate:** a specific fact is wrong; dispute.
- **C, incomplete:** information is missing that makes the record misleading, such as a paid collection showing a balance; dispute.
- **U, unverified:** you are unsure; gather records before deciding.

:::example A Four-Pass Review in Practice (illustrative)
Priya, an invented 29-year-old teacher, reviews her TransUnion report.

Pass 1 shows an address in a city she has never lived in. Pass 2 finds a $412 medical collection she does not recognize and an auto loan correctly listed. Pass 3 shows that her student loan payment grid has a "30" late mark for June 2024; her servicer statements show the June payment posted on June 3, before the due date. Pass 4 shows the same $1,950 credit card debt listed both by the original issuer (balance $1,950, status charged off) and by a collector (balance $1,950), so the debt appears twice as owed.

Priya codes the address as I, the medical collection as U (she will request validation from the collector and check her insurance explanations of benefits), the student loan late mark as I with the servicer statement as evidence, and the duplicate balance as C because the original creditor should show a zero balance after selling the debt. She now has three disputes ready and one item to research, all tied to documents.
:::

:::case The Scale of Credit Report Errors: The FTC Accuracy Study
In 2013 the Federal Trade Commission released a congressionally mandated study of credit report accuracy. It found that one in five consumers had an error on at least one of their three credit reports, and that five percent had errors that could lead to less favorable terms on products such as auto loans and insurance. Four out of five consumers who filed disputes saw some modification to their report; slightly more than one in ten saw a score change after errors were corrected; about one in 20 had a maximum score change of more than 25 points; and only one in 250 had a maximum change of more than 100 points [12].

Lesson for the reader: errors are common enough that every consumer should review all three reports, but the study also undercuts the "100-point" marketing of credit repair firms. Most corrections produce modest score changes. The value lies in accuracy itself, which can make the difference at pricing thresholds.
:::

# Chapter 4: Deciding What to Dispute and Building Evidence

:::objectives
- Apply a decision rule for whether an item should be disputed.
- Understand why mass or template disputes often fail and can be treated as frivolous.
- Assemble evidence packages that make the dispute easy to verify.
- Prioritize disputes by likely impact on your file.
:::

Once your review grid is complete, the temptation is to dispute everything negative. That approach is ineffective and, when it involves claiming that accurate information is false, dishonest. The law gives you a right to dispute information you believe is inaccurate or incomplete. It does not give anyone a right to remove accurate records by overwhelming a bureau with challenges.

## The Dispute Decision Rule

Dispute an item only if you can complete this sentence truthfully: "This item is inaccurate (or incomplete) because ____, and I can show that with ____." If you cannot fill the first blank with a specific fact, the item is not disputable as an error. If you cannot fill the second blank, gather evidence first or explain clearly why evidence is unavailable (for example, an account you never opened).

## Why "Dispute Everything" Backfires

The CFPB explains that a credit reporting company is not required to investigate disputes it reasonably determines are frivolous or irrelevant, such as disputes that do not contain enough information to investigate or that do not specify what is disputed, though it must notify you of that decision within five business days [2]. Vague, form-letter disputes that challenge every negative item with the same wording invite exactly that response. They also bury your strongest disputes among weak ones.

## Evidence Hierarchy

Not all evidence carries equal weight. In descending order of persuasive value:

| Strength | Evidence type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Strongest | Documents from the furnisher itself | Statement showing payment posted before due date; payoff or "paid in full" letter |
| Strong | Official records | Bankruptcy discharge order; court dismissal; identity-theft report from IdentityTheft.gov |
| Strong | Third-party financial records | Bank statement showing the payment leaving your account |
| Moderate | Your own contemporaneous records | Email confirmations, screenshots with dates |
| Supporting | Proof of identity and address | Driver's license copy, utility bill |
| Weak | Your recollection alone | "I'm sure I paid on time" |

## Prioritizing

Score each disputable item from 1 to 3 on two dimensions: impact (how much the error likely affects your file) and evidence (how strong your proof is). Dispute the highest combined scores first. As a rule of thumb, high-impact items include accounts that are not yours, incorrect late marks on otherwise clean accounts, collections reported with a balance after payment, duplicate debts, and negative items past their reporting period. Low-impact items include a misspelled former employer or an outdated phone number; these are still worth correcting in identity-error disputes because they can contribute to mixed files.

## Step-by-Step: Building an Evidence Package

1. Create a subfolder for each disputed item named with bureau, creditor, and last four digits.
2. Copy the relevant page of the credit report and circle or highlight the item.
3. Gather the strongest available evidence from the hierarchy above.
4. Write a one-paragraph fact statement: what is reported, what is true, and which document proves it.
5. Redact unnecessary sensitive information from copies; never send originals.
6. Add proof of identity and current address if sending by mail.
7. List every enclosure so you can reference it in your letter.

:::example Prioritizing Five Items (illustrative)
Luis, an invented small-business owner, has five candidate items.

| Item | Impact (1-3) | Evidence (1-3) | Total | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Credit card he never opened | 3 | 2 (identity-theft report filed) | 5 | Dispute first |
| 30-day late on auto loan, statement shows on-time | 3 | 3 | 6 | Dispute first |
| Paid collection still showing $640 balance | 2 | 3 (paid-in-full letter) | 5 | Dispute first |
| Old address misspelled | 1 | 2 | 3 | Include in identity correction |
| Accurate 90-day late from 2022 | n/a | n/a | n/a | Do not dispute; let age |

Luis sends three focused disputes rather than five scattered ones, and does not challenge the accurate 2022 late payment. His file is cleaner and his credibility with the bureaus intact.
:::

:::checklist Ready-to-Dispute Test
- I can state the specific fact that is wrong.
- I have at least one document from the strength hierarchy, or a clear reason none exists.
- The item is coded I or C on my review grid, not A.
- I have checked whether the same error appears on the other two bureaus.
- My copies are redacted appropriately and I am keeping originals.
- I have recorded the item in my dispute log (Appendix B).
:::

:::summary
Dispute only what you can describe as specifically inaccurate or incomplete and, wherever possible, prove. Bureaus may decline disputes that are frivolous or too vague, so mass template challenges weaken your position. Organize evidence by strength, prioritize by impact and proof, and build a labelled package for each item before writing a single letter.
:::

# Chapter 5: Disputing Errors With the Credit Reporting Companies

:::objectives
- Choose between online, mail, and phone disputes and understand the trade-offs.
- Write a dispute letter containing every element the CFPB recommends.
- Understand what the credit reporting company must do after receiving a dispute.
- Interpret the results letter and plan the next step.
:::

The credit reporting company is usually the first stop. When it receives your dispute, the CFPB explains that it must investigate, forward the dispute and all relevant information you provided to the furnisher, and report the results back to you [2].

## Choosing a Channel

Each nationwide bureau accepts disputes online, by mail, and by phone. The CFPB lists current online portals and phone numbers for Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion [2]. Each channel has trade-offs.

| Channel | Advantages | Disadvantages | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Online portal | Fast; immediate confirmation; easy tracking | Character limits; dropdown reasons may oversimplify; uploading evidence can be clumsy | Simple, single-fact errors with clear documents |
| Certified mail | Full narrative; complete enclosures; proof of delivery | Slower; postage cost | Complex errors, identity issues, anything you may escalate |
| Phone | Quick for very simple corrections | Weak record of what you said | Address or name corrections only |

For any dispute you consider important, certified mail with return receipt creates the clearest record. The CFPB suggests this option so that you have a record your letter was received [2].

## Required Elements of a Dispute Letter

The CFPB recommends that a mailed dispute include [2]:

- your complete name, address, and telephone number;
- the credit report confirmation number, if available;
- each error you want fixed, including the account number for any disputed account;
- a clear explanation of why you are disputing the information;
- a request that the information be removed or corrected;
- a copy of the report section with the disputed items circled or highlighted; and
- copies, not originals, of supporting documents.

The FTC publishes a sample letter built on the same elements [13]. Appendix A adapts it into templates for several common situations.

## Writing Principles

1. **One item per numbered paragraph.** Make each dispute independently understandable.
2. **Fact, then proof, then request.** "The account shows a 30-day late payment for June 2024. My servicer statement (Enclosure 2) shows the payment posted June 3, 2024, before the June 15 due date. Please correct the June 2024 status to 'current.'"
3. **Neutral tone.** Anger does not speed investigations; clarity does.
4. **No legal theatrics.** Letters packed with statutory citations copied from the internet add nothing; the bureau knows the law. A simple reference to your right to dispute under the FCRA is sufficient.
5. **Keep it short.** One to two pages plus enclosures.

## What Happens Next

After investigation, the bureau sends results. Typical outcomes are:

- **Deleted:** the item is removed.
- **Updated or modified:** one or more fields changed.
- **Verified as accurate:** the furnisher confirmed the information and it remains.
- **Frivolous or irrelevant:** the bureau declined to investigate, with an explanation.

If information is changed or deleted, you can generally ask the bureau to send notices of the correction to anyone who received your report recently; check each bureau's results letter for instructions. Compare the updated report against your original line by line. Corrections sometimes fix one field while leaving another wrong.

:::example Converting a Messy Dispute Into a Clean One (illustrative)
Before: "I am disputing all negative accounts under the FCRA. These are not mine and must be deleted within 30 days or I will sue."

After:
"1. Capital Example Bank, account ending 4471. Your report shows a 30-day late payment for June 2024. Enclosure 2, my June 2024 statement, shows the payment of $187.00 posted on June 3, 2024, before the June 15 due date. Please correct the June 2024 status to current.

2. Address: 1200 Pine Street, Fresno, CA. I have never lived at this address. Enclosure 3 is my driver's license and Enclosure 4 a utility bill showing my current address. Please remove the Fresno address from my file."

The revised version identifies each item, states a specific fact, cites proof, and requests a specific correction. It is far harder to dismiss as frivolous.
:::

Online portals suit simple errors; certified mail suits anything complex or likely to be escalated. A strong letter follows the CFPB's recommended elements, addresses one item per paragraph, and moves from fact to proof to request in a neutral tone. # Chapter 6: Disputing With Furnishers and Debt Collectors

:::objectives
- Explain when and why to dispute directly with the furnisher.
- Use the debt collection validation process to test collection accounts.
- Understand the basics of time-barred debt and why paying or acknowledging old debt deserves caution.
- Draft furnisher and collector letters that are specific and documented.
:::

The bureau is the messenger; the furnisher is the source. If the source keeps sending bad data, corrections at the bureau level may not hold. The CFPB recommends that after disputing with the credit reporting company, you also dispute the information with the company that provided it [2].

## Direct Disputes With Furnishers

A direct dispute goes to the creditor, servicer, or collector that reports the item. Use the dispute address listed on your statements or on the credit report. Include the same core elements as a bureau dispute: identification, the specific inaccuracy, supporting documents, and the correction you request. Ask the furnisher to notify every bureau to which it reports.

Direct disputes are especially valuable when:

- the furnisher's own records will prove the error (for example, a payment it received on time);
- the bureau "verified" an item you can document as wrong;
- you need the furnisher to correct its internal records, not just the credit report.

## Debt Collectors and Validation

Collection accounts deserve particular care. Under the CFPB's debt collection rule, collectors must provide certain validation information, generally in the initial communication or within five days of first communicating with you, including an end date for a 30-day period during which you can dispute the debt [6]. If you believe you already paid the debt, do not owe it, the amount is wrong, or it is not yours, the CFPB says you may send a written request to dispute the debt or to receive more information [14].

A written dispute within the validation period is powerful because it generally requires the collector to provide verification before continuing collection. Even outside that window, you can still dispute inaccurate reporting with the bureaus and with the collector as a furnisher.

## Time-Barred Debt

Most states have statutes of limitations limiting how long creditors or collectors can sue over a debt. The CFPB notes most are between three and six years, that some debts such as federal student loans have no statute of limitations, and that a debt does not generally disappear just because the limitation period has run [15]. Under the debt collection rule, a collector must not sue or threaten to sue to collect a time-barred debt [16].

Two cautions follow. First, the statute of limitations for lawsuits is separate from the credit reporting period; a debt can be too old to sue on but still reportable, or vice versa. Second, in some states a payment or written acknowledgment can affect the limitation period. Before paying or promising to pay a very old debt, check your state's rules or consult a nonprofit credit counselor or attorney.

| Question | Where to find the answer | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Is this debt mine and is the amount right? | Validation notice; your records | Basis for dispute |
| Did I dispute within the validation period? | Date on notice; your mail receipt | Determines collector's obligations |
| What is the date of first delinquency? | Credit report; original creditor statements | Sets the reporting clock |
| What is my state's statute of limitations? | State attorney general or legal aid | Affects lawsuit risk |
| Will paying restart any clock in my state? | State law; attorney | Avoids unintended consequences |

:::example Testing a Collection Account (illustrative)
Renee, an invented 52-year-old, receives a letter from Example Recovery LLC claiming she owes $1,284 on an old phone account. The validation notice lists a dispute deadline 30 days out. Renee does not recognize the account number, and her records show she closed her phone service in 2019 with a $0 final bill.

Within the window, she mails a dispute by certified mail stating that she does not believe she owes the debt, that her final statement (enclosed) shows a $0 balance, and requesting verification including the name of the original creditor and an itemization. She simultaneously disputes the collection with all three bureaus, enclosing the same final statement. She records every date in her dispute log. She does not make a "good faith" payment, because she believes she owes nothing.
:::

# Chapter 7: Tracking, Following Up, and Escalating

:::objectives
- Maintain a dispute log that captures every date and outcome.
- Decide the right next step after each type of result.
- File an effective complaint with the CFPB.
- Recognize when to involve a consumer attorney or legal aid.
:::

Disputes are rarely one-and-done. A disciplined tracking system turns a confusing exchange of letters into a manageable project with clear next actions.

## The Dispute Log

Appendix B contains a complete dispute log. At minimum, record for each item: bureau or furnisher, item, date sent, method and tracking number, date received (from return receipt), date of response, outcome, and next action with a due date. Set calendar reminders 35 and 45 days after each mailing so that nothing slips.

## Decision Tree After a Result

| Result | Next step |
|---|---|
| Deleted or corrected | Pull an updated report; confirm the change on all bureaus where the item appeared; file the results letter |
| Partially corrected | Send a follow-up dispute addressing the remaining field with the same evidence |
| Verified, but you have strong proof | Dispute directly with the furnisher; then re-dispute with the bureau including any new evidence; consider a CFPB complaint |
| Frivolous determination | Read the reason; supply what was missing; resubmit with specificity |
| No response | Confirm delivery; send a short follow-up with copy of original; consider a CFPB complaint |
| Item reappears after deletion | Dispute again, enclosing the earlier deletion letter; this is a data-management error the CFPB specifically identifies [3] |

## Filing a CFPB Complaint

The CFPB accepts complaints about credit reporting, debt collection, and credit repair. A strong complaint is factual and chronological: what you disputed, when, what evidence you provided, what the company replied, and what outcome you seek. Attach your dispute letter, proof of delivery, and the company's response. Companies typically respond through the CFPB portal, and you can review and comment on their response.

## Consumer Statements

If a dispute does not resolve an item you still believe is wrong, you can generally add a brief statement to your file explaining your position. Statements do not change scores, but a human reviewer, such as a mortgage underwriter, may read them. Keep statements short, factual, and free of personal details you would not want strangers to read.

## When to Seek Legal Help

The FCRA allows consumers to sue for certain violations. You do not need a lawyer for ordinary disputes, but consider consulting a consumer attorney or legal aid office if:

- a bureau keeps verifying an item you can clearly prove is not yours;
- a mixed file persists after repeated disputes;
- you are being sued on a debt, or a judgment appears that you never knew about;
- the error caused a specific denial or higher price that you can document.

Many consumer attorneys offer free consultations for FCRA matters. Avoid anyone who demands large fees before reviewing your documents, and be wary of non-lawyers who claim they can "sue the bureaus" for you.

Day 4: Return receipt shows delivery.
Day 31: Experian's results letter says "verified."
Day 33: Kofi mails a direct dispute to the lender with the same bank statement plus a cover note citing the Experian result.
Day 50: The lender replies that a processing error occurred and it has instructed all bureaus to correct the month.
Day 58: Kofi pulls fresh reports. Experian and TransUnion are corrected; Equifax is not yet updated.

# Chapter 8: Credit Repair Companies, the Law, and Common Scams

:::objectives
- Summarize the consumer protections in the Credit Repair Organizations Act and the Telemarketing Sales Rule.
- Identify the warning signs of illegal or deceptive credit repair offers.
- Learn from documented federal enforcement actions.
- Evaluate a credit repair offer using a structured screen.
:::

Paying someone to dispute errors is legal, and some people prefer to delegate the paperwork. But the industry has a long record of enforcement actions, and the law imposes specific limits that every consumer should know before signing anything.

## What the Law Requires

According to the FTC, the Credit Repair Organizations Act prohibits untrue or misleading representations, bars credit repair companies from demanding advance payment, requires contracts to be in writing, and gives consumers contract cancellation rights [5]. In practice, that means a legitimate company cannot charge you before it has performed the promised services, must give you a written contract describing those services, and must honor your right to cancel within the period the law provides.

The Telemarketing Sales Rule adds a related prohibition on collecting fees for credit repair sold over the phone until the promised results have been achieved and documented. That provision was central to the CFPB's case described below.

## Warning Signs

The FTC's consumer booklet notes that its attorneys have never seen a legitimate credit repair operation making claims of quick fixes, and that there is no quick fix for creditworthiness [1]. Treat any of the following as a red flag:

| Red flag | Why it is a problem |
|---|---|
| Payment required before any work is done | Generally prohibited by CROA [5] |
| Promise to remove accurate negative items | No one can legally remove accurate, timely information [1] |
| Guaranteed score increases | Outcomes depend on what is actually inaccurate |
| Advice to dispute everything as "not mine" | Encourages false statements |
| Suggestion to create a "new credit identity" with an EIN or CPN | Misrepresenting identity on credit applications can be illegal |
| Refusal to explain your legal rights or provide a written contract | Required disclosures and written contract are mandated [5] |
| Pressure to act immediately or keep you from contacting bureaus yourself | Classic high-pressure tactic |

:::case CFPB v. Lexington Law and CreditRepair.com
The CFPB sued companies doing business as Lexington Law and CreditRepair.com, which it described as two of the country's largest credit repair companies, alleging that they illegally charged upfront fees and used deceptive, bait-and-switch advertising, in violation of the Consumer Financial Protection Act and the Telemarketing Sales Rule [17]. The CFPB's consumer advisory states that the court concluded the companies broke the law and that, as part of the settlement, the companies are banned from using telemarketing to sell credit repair services for ten years [18]. The stipulated judgment describes Lexington Law, CreditRepair.com, and their parent as the largest credit repair organizations in the United States and records their agreement not to violate the Telemarketing Sales Rule's advance fee provision [19].

Lesson for the reader: size and advertising budgets are not evidence of legality. The questions that matter are whether fees are charged before results, and whether the marketing promises something the law does not allow.
:::

:::case FTC Action Against the Credit Glory Network (2026)
In August 2026 the FTC announced that a federal court had temporarily halted a credit repair scheme run by a network of related companies doing business as Credit Glory. The FTC alleged that since at least 2016 the defendants made false and misleading promises about credit repair, impersonated debt collectors and creditors, collected illegal upfront fees, and used unlawful subscription enrollment practices, taking nearly $200 million from consumers. The complaint said the operation used paid search ads, in some instances targeting military servicemembers [20]. These are allegations in a case announced by the FTC; readers should consult the FTC's page for the current status.

Lesson for the reader: search ads are not endorsements. Before clicking "fix my credit now," check the company against the red-flag table and remember that disputes cost nothing to file yourself.
:::

## The Offer Screen

If you still want help, use this five-question screen before signing:

1. Does the company charge only after completing specific, documented services?
2. Does it provide a written contract and the disclosures CROA requires?
3. Does it promise only to dispute items that are inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable?
4. Will it share copies of every letter it sends in your name?
5. Does it have a physical address, and are there regulatory actions or complaints against it? (Check the CFPB complaint database and your state attorney general.)

A "no" to any of the first four is disqualifying.

:::example Evaluating Two Offers (illustrative)
Offer A, found through a search ad: "$129 setup fee plus $99 per month. We remove collections and late payments. Average increase 112 points. Start today." Offer B, a nonprofit credit counseling agency: free initial session, a review of reports, help writing disputes for documented errors, and an optional debt management plan with fees disclosed in writing.

Screening Offer A: it charges a setup fee before services (question 1 fails), promises removal regardless of accuracy (question 3 fails), and advertises an average score increase with no basis. Offer B passes the screen. Over a year, Offer A would cost $129 + 12 x $99 = $1,317, all for services the consumer could perform free.
:::

:::checklist Credit Repair Contract Review
- No payment demanded before services are completed.
- Written contract lists specific services, total cost, and timeline.
- Cancellation rights stated clearly.
- No promise to remove accurate information.
- No suggestion to use a CPN, EIN, or new identity.
- Company searched in the CFPB complaint database and state attorney general records.
:::

:::summary
The Credit Repair Organizations Act bars advance fees, misleading claims, and unwritten contracts, and gives cancellation rights; the Telemarketing Sales Rule restricts fees for telemarketed credit repair until results are achieved. Federal enforcement against large, heavily advertised firms shows that marketing reach is no proof of legality. Anything a legitimate company can do, you can do yourself at little or no cost.
:::

# Chapter 9: Identity Theft, Credit Freezes, and Fraud Alerts

:::objectives
- Distinguish a credit freeze from a fraud alert and choose the right tool.
- Follow the federal recovery process for identity theft.
- Dispute fraudulent accounts using an identity-theft report.
- Build long-term habits that reduce identity risk.
:::

Some of the most damaging credit report errors are not errors at all in the clerical sense; they are accounts opened by someone else. Identity theft requires its own process, because the law gives victims additional tools.

## Credit Freeze

The FTC explains that when a credit freeze is in place, nobody can open a new credit account in your name, that there is no cost to place or lift a freeze, that it does not affect your credit score, and that anyone can place one at any time without waiting for a breach [21]. Freezes have been free nationwide since September 21, 2018 [22]. You must contact each of the three bureaus separately, and you will temporarily lift the freeze when you apply for new credit.

## Fraud Alert

A fraud alert tells businesses that check your report to take extra steps to verify your identity before extending credit. Placing an alert with one bureau generally causes it to notify the other two. Alerts are useful when you suspect risk but want to keep credit access smooth; freezes offer stronger protection.

| Feature | Credit freeze | Fraud alert |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Blocks access to your report for new credit | Requires businesses to verify identity |
| Cost | Free [21] | Free |
| Effect on score | None [21] | None |
| How to place | Contact each bureau separately | Contact one bureau; it notifies the others |
| Best for | Strongest prevention; anyone not applying for credit soon | Suspected risk while still applying for credit |

## Recovery Steps

The CFPB directs suspected identity-theft victims to IdentityTheft.gov, the federal government's one-stop resource to report and recover from identity theft [2]. The typical sequence is:

1. Call the fraud departments of companies where fraud occurred; close or freeze affected accounts.
2. Place a fraud alert or freeze with the bureaus.
3. Report the theft at IdentityTheft.gov to create an identity-theft report and personalized recovery plan.
4. Dispute fraudulent accounts and inquiries with each bureau, enclosing the identity-theft report and proof of identity.
5. Dispute directly with each business where an account was opened.
6. Review all three reports again after 30 to 60 days.

## Prevention Habits

Shred financial documents; use unique passwords and a password manager; enable multi-factor authentication on financial accounts; never give your Social Security number to unsolicited callers; review reports regularly. These habits do not guarantee safety, but they reduce exposure.

:::example A Data-Breach Response (illustrative)
Tanya, an invented nurse, receives notice that a health-care vendor exposed her name, birth date, and Social Security number. She is not planning to apply for credit for at least a year. Within an hour she places freezes with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, storing each PIN or account login in her password manager. She pulls all three reports to confirm there are no unfamiliar accounts. Six months later, when renting an apartment, she temporarily lifts the freeze only at the bureau the landlord's screening company uses, for a two-week window.
:::

A credit freeze, free since 2018, is the strongest preventive tool and does not affect your score; a fraud alert adds verification while keeping access easier. # Chapter 10: How Credit Scores Work

:::objectives
- Describe the five factor categories in FICO's published model.
- Calculate revolving credit utilization overall and per card.
- Explain why scores differ across bureaus and models.
- Identify behaviors with the greatest long-term effect on scores.
:::

Understanding scoring helps you focus on what matters and ignore myths. Scoring formulas are proprietary, but FICO publishes the broad categories it uses.

## FICO's Five Categories

FICO groups the data it considers into five categories: payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of credit history (15%), new credit (10%), and credit mix (10%) [4]. FICO notes that the importance of each category varies from person to person, so these percentages describe the general model, not a fixed formula for every file.

| Category | Approximate weight [4] | Practical levers |
|---|---|---|
| Payment history | 35% | Pay every account on time; automate minimums |
| Amounts owed | 30% | Lower revolving balances relative to limits |
| Length of credit history | 15% | Keep older accounts open when sensible |
| New credit | 10% | Apply only when needed; space applications |
| Credit mix | 10% | Do not open accounts just for "mix" |

## Utilization

Revolving utilization is your total revolving balances divided by your total revolving limits. Lenders and educators widely recommend keeping it low; Capital One's education page, for example, reports that the CFPB recommends keeping utilization below 30% [23]. Lower is generally better, and per-card utilization can also matter. Because utilization uses the balance reported on your statement date, paying down a balance before the statement closes can lower reported utilization even if you always pay in full.

## Why Scores Differ

Your scores can differ because each bureau holds slightly different data, because there are many models and versions, and because scores are calculated at different moments. A difference of a few dozen points between sources is normal and not, by itself, evidence of error.

## Myths to Discard

- **Checking your own report hurts your score.** It does not; self-checks are soft inquiries.
- **Carrying a balance builds credit.** Paying in full avoids interest; utilization is measured on the reported balance, not on whether you pay interest.
- **Closing old cards always helps.** Closing can raise utilization and, over time, shorten average age.
- **Income affects your score.** FICO states that income is not a factor [4].

:::example Calculating and Lowering Utilization (illustrative)
Jamal, an invented consumer, has three cards:

| Card | Limit | Balance | Per-card utilization |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $2,000 | $1,700 | 85% |
| B | $5,000 | $900 | 18% |
| C | $3,000 | $0 | 0% |
| Total | $10,000 | $2,600 | 26% |

Overall utilization is 26%, but card A is at 85%. Jamal has $900 available this month for debt reduction. Paying $900 toward card A reduces its balance to $800 (40%) and overall utilization to 17% ($1,700 / $10,000). He also sets a reminder to pay card A three days before its statement closing date.
:::

:::checklist Score-Supporting Habits
- Automatic minimum payment set on every account.
- Calendar note for each card's statement closing date.
- Revolving balances tracked monthly against limits.
- New applications limited to genuine needs.
- Oldest accounts kept open and used lightly where fees allow.
:::

:::summary
FICO's published model weighs payment history and amounts owed most heavily, followed by length of history, new credit, and mix. Utilization, the ratio of revolving balances to limits, is one of the most controllable factors. Scores vary across bureaus and models, so focus on report accuracy and behavior rather than chasing a single number.
:::

# Chapter 11: Collections, Medical Debt, and Old Accounts

:::objectives
- Choose among disputing, paying, settling, or waiting for each collection account.
- Understand recent changes in the reporting of medical collections and their limits.
- Negotiate in writing and document every agreement.
- Avoid common mistakes with old debts.
:::

Collections are often the heaviest items on a damaged report. Handling them well requires separating three questions: Is it accurate? Do I owe it? What is the best financial decision?

## The Collection Decision Matrix

| Situation | Typical approach |
|---|---|
| Not yours or amount wrong | Dispute with collector (validation) and bureaus (Chapters 5 and 6) |
| Yours, accurate, recent, collector may sue | Consider payment plan or settlement; get terms in writing; seek advice if sued |
| Yours, accurate, old, near end of reporting period | Weigh paying against legal risk and state law on limitation periods; get advice |
| Paid but still showing balance | Dispute as incomplete with payment proof |
| Duplicate listing (original creditor and collector both show balance owed) | Dispute so only one shows a balance |

## Medical Debt

Medical collections changed significantly in recent years. A CFPB data report notes that in July 2022 paid medical collections were removed from credit reports and the delay before medical collections are reported was extended to one year after first delinquency [24]. The CFPB has also stated that the three nationwide companies removed paid medical debts and those less than a year old, and took steps to remove medical collections under $500 [25]. A Congressional Research Service overview adds that the CFPB finalized a rule in 2024 to remove most medical debt from credit reports, but that a federal judge overturned the rule in July 2025 [26].

Practical implications: a paid medical collection, a medical collection less than a year old, or one under $500 that still appears is worth examining and, where it contradicts these voluntary bureau policies, disputing. Larger, unpaid medical collections may still be reported. Always check insurance explanations of benefits; billing errors and unprocessed claims are common sources of medical collections.

## Negotiating in Writing

If you decide to pay or settle an accurate debt:

1. Confirm the collector owns or is authorized to collect it.
2. Decide your maximum amount based on your budget, not on pressure.
3. Make an offer in writing; ask for any agreement in writing before paying.
4. Ask how the account will be reported after payment; some collectors agree to report "paid" or to request deletion, but they are not required to.
5. Pay by traceable method, never by giving bank access to the collector.
6. Keep the agreement and proof of payment permanently.

:::example Comparing Options for a Collection (illustrative)
Andrea, an invented consumer, owes an accurate $2,400 collection, first delinquent 18 months ago. Her budget can free $150 per month. Option 1: a 16-month payment plan for the full balance. Option 2: a lump-sum settlement offer of 50%, $1,200, which she could save for in 8 months. Option 3: do nothing.

Option 3 leaves the risk of a lawsuit while the debt is well within typical limitation periods and keeps a balance-owed collection on her report. Option 2 resolves the debt for half the cost in half the time but requires disciplined saving and a written agreement first. Andrea opens a separate savings account, saves $150 a month, and after 8 months requests the settlement in writing before paying. These numbers are illustrative; actual settlement percentages vary widely and are not guaranteed.
:::

# Chapter 12: Building Positive History and Your 90-Day Plan

:::objectives
- Choose appropriate credit-building products for a thin or damaged file.
- Design a monthly routine that compounds positive history.
- Execute a 90-day plan and an annual review.
- Measure progress honestly without relying on a single score.
:::

Removing errors stops the damage; positive history drives recovery. The CFPB notes that paying on time, every time, helps build a strong credit history and lowers future borrowing costs [27].

## The Credit-Invisible and Thin-File Problem

The CFPB reported in 2015 that 26 million Americans, roughly one in ten adults, were "credit invisible," with no history at a nationwide credit reporting company [28]. If your file is thin, the challenge is to add accounts that report positive history at a cost you can manage.

## Building Tools

| Tool | How it works | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Secured credit card | You deposit an amount equal to the limit; on-time use builds history; deposit may be refunded and limit raised over time [29] | Fees and interest can be high [29] |
| Credit builder loan | Lender holds the loan amount in savings while you repay over about 6 to 24 months; you receive the funds at the end [27] | Confirm it reports to all three bureaus |
| Authorized user | A trusted person adds you to a well-managed card | Their mistakes can affect you; agree on rules |
| Rent or utility reporting | Some services report on-time rent | Check fees and which bureaus receive data |

## The Monthly Routine

1. Pay every bill on or before the due date; automate minimums.
2. Use a secured or starter card for one small recurring bill and pay it in full.
3. Track utilization before statement closing dates.
4. Add to an emergency fund, even modestly, to avoid new late payments when surprises happen.
5. Review one bureau report each month on a rotating basis.

## The 90-Day Plan

| Weeks | Focus | Key outputs |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | Reports and file setup | Three reports saved; seven folders; review grids complete |
| 3-4 | First disputes | Top three disputes mailed; dispute log started; freezes placed if needed |
| 5-6 | Budget and automation | Autopay on all accounts; utilization plan; collection decisions recorded |
| 7-8 | Follow-up | Results reviewed; furnisher disputes sent where needed |
| 9-10 | Build | Secured card or builder loan chosen if file is thin |
| 11-12 | Review | Fresh reports compared to baseline; next 90-day plan written |

## Measuring Progress Honestly

Track leading indicators you control: number of verified errors corrected, months of on-time payments, utilization percentage, emergency-fund balance. Scores are lagging indicators influenced by many factors; watch the trend over quarters rather than reacting to weekly changes.

:::example A Thin-File Build Over 12 Months (illustrative)
Sam, an invented 23-year-old with no credit file, opens a credit builder loan of $600 repayable at $52 per month for 12 months, and a secured card with a $300 deposit. He puts a $15 streaming subscription on the card and pays it in full each month by autopay. After 12 months, Sam has two accounts with 12 months of on-time history each, utilization of about 5% on the card at statement time, and receives the $600 held by the lender (less any interest and fees disclosed in his agreement). No particular score is promised, but Sam's file now contains the positive data that scoring models need.
:::

:::checklist Annual Credit Review
- Pull all three reports and compare with last year's baseline.
- Confirm every dispute from the year is closed and filed.
- Check that freezes remain in place where wanted.
- Recalculate utilization and review interest rates.
- Identify negative items reaching the end of their reporting period.
- Write next year's three credit goals.
:::

:::summary
Positive history comes from on-time payment and responsible, low-utilization use of reporting accounts. Secured cards, credit builder loans, and carefully chosen authorized-user arrangements can help thin or damaged files. A 90-day plan turns this book into action, and an annual review keeps your file accurate and your habits steady. Measure what you control.
:::

# Appendix C: Revolving Debt Paydown Planner

Two well-known methods exist for ordering debt payments. The avalanche method pays extra toward the highest interest rate first, minimizing total interest. The snowball method pays extra toward the smallest balance first, producing quick wins that help some people stay motivated. Either works if you stick with it; the planner below supports both.

| Debt | Balance | Limit | APR | Minimum | Order (avalanche) | Order (snowball) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example store card | $1,100 | $1,500 | 31% | $40 | 1 | 2 |
| Example card A | $1,700 | $2,000 | 27% | $55 | 2 | 3 |
| Example card B | $900 | $5,000 | 19% | $30 | 3 | 1 |

In this illustrative set, the avalanche method targets the 31% store card first, while the snowball method targets the $900 balance on card B first. In both methods, every other debt continues to receive its minimum payment.

# Appendix F: Questions to Ask a Credit Counselor or Attorney

Use these questions to make a short consultation productive.

1. Based on my documents, which items appear to be genuine errors, and which are accurate?
2. What is the statute of limitations for this type of debt in my state, and could a payment affect it?
3. Am I at risk of being sued on any debt, and what should I do if I am served?
4. Does my situation suggest a possible FCRA violation, such as repeated verification of an item that is clearly not mine?
5. What do your services cost, when are fees charged, and what is in writing?
6. If you are a nonprofit counselor, how is your agency funded and what would a debt management plan cost me each month?

# Glossary

**Adverse action.** A denial or less favorable terms based in whole or part on a consumer report; triggers a right to a free report if requested within 60 days.

**Authorized user.** A person permitted to use another person's credit card account; the account may appear on the user's report.

**Charge-off.** An accounting action by a creditor treating a debt as a loss; the debt generally remains owed.

**Collection account.** A debt referred or sold to a third-party collector.

**Consumer reporting agency (CRA).** A company that assembles consumer information into reports; Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion are the nationwide CRAs.

**Credit builder loan.** A loan whose proceeds are held in savings while the borrower repays, designed to build payment history.

**Credit freeze.** A restriction that prevents new creditors from accessing your report; free to place and lift.

**Credit Repair Organizations Act (CROA).** Federal law regulating credit repair companies, including a ban on advance fees.

**Date of first delinquency.** The date an account first became delinquent and was not brought current; governs how long negative information may be reported.

**Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA).** Federal law governing consumer reports, accuracy, access, and disputes.

**Fraud alert.** A notice on your file requiring businesses to verify identity before extending credit.

**Frivolous dispute.** A dispute a CRA reasonably determines lacks enough information to investigate; the CRA must notify you of that determination.

**Furnisher.** A business that supplies consumer information to a CRA.

**Hard inquiry.** A request for your report connected with an application for credit.

**Mixed file.** A credit file containing another person's information.

**Secured credit card.** A card backed by a cash deposit, usually equal to the credit limit.

**Soft inquiry.** A request for your report not tied to an application, including your own checks; does not affect scores.

**Statute of limitations.** The period during which a creditor may sue to collect a debt; varies by state and debt type.

**Time-barred debt.** A debt for which the statute of limitations has expired; collectors may not sue or threaten to sue on it.

**Utilization.** Revolving balances divided by revolving credit limits.

**Validation information.** Details a debt collector must provide about a debt, including the dispute period.
