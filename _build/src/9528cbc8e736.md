# How to Use This Book

Most people who run a business do not lack goals. They lack a reliable way to convert a goal into the specific actions that must happen on Tuesday afternoon when the phone is ringing and a customer is waiting. Psychologists call this the intention-behavior gap: the distance between deciding to do something and actually doing it. This workbook is a structured, 30-day program for closing that gap on one important goal.

The program rests on four bodies of research that have been studied for decades. Goal-setting research by Edwin Locke and Gary Latham found that specific, difficult goals consistently lead to higher performance than vague exhortations to "do your best" ([Locke and Latham, 2002](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgPsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20(2002).pdf)). Research on implementation intentions, summarized in a meta-analysis by Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran, found that if-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment across 94 studies ([Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006](http://ereserve.library.utah.edu/Annual/PSY/7963/Werner/Gollwitzer2006.pdf)). A meta-analysis of 138 studies by Benjamin Harkin and colleagues found that monitoring progress promoted goal attainment, with larger effects when progress was physically recorded or reported to others ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)). And diary research by Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer found that making progress in meaningful work was the most prominent feature of people's best workdays ([Harvard Business School Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity)).

## Who This Book Is For

- Business owners and managers with an important goal that keeps getting pushed aside.
- Solo professionals who need structure without a boss.
- Teams that want a shared 30-day sprint.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Choose one 30-day target that is specific, challenging, and within your control.
- Break it into four weekly milestones and daily actions.
- Protect a daily execution block with a 15-minute minimum.
- Anticipate obstacles with a pre-mortem and write if-then plans.
- Track, record, and report progress in ways research supports.
- Run a weekly sprint review and recover from missed days.
- Close the cycle with a structured review and plan the next 30 days.

## How the Book Is Organized

Chapters 1 through 11 teach the method. Each chapter includes learning objectives, definitions, a step-by-step framework, a worked illustrative example (a hypothetical person or business used to show the method), a worksheet or checklist, a summary, and review questions. Chapter 12 contains the 30-day logs: a setup page and four weekly sprint logs, which feed the Day 30 Review in Chapter 10. Appendices provide templates and scripts.

Research findings describe average effects across groups of study participants; individual results vary and are not guaranteed. Case references to named authors and researchers describe their published work; their findings and experiences are their own, not TTI's.
:::

# Chapter 1: Why Good Intentions Fail

:::objectives
- Describe the intention-behavior gap.
- Identify the three common points where goal pursuit breaks down.
- Explain why vague goals underperform specific ones.
- Recognize the structure of the 30-day program.
:::

## The Intention-Behavior Gap

Deciding to do something is a necessary first step, but it is not sufficient. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's review organizes the problems people face when acting on goals into a few categories: failing to get started (forgetting, missing opportunities, or hesitating), getting derailed once started, and failing to disengage from an unproductive course ([National Cancer Institute summary of Gollwitzer and Sheeran](https://cancercontrol.cancer.gov/brp/research/constructs/implementation-intentions)). Each of these failures is a design problem, not a character flaw, and each has a known countermeasure.

| Breakdown point | What it looks like | Countermeasure in this book |
|---|---|---|
| Not starting | "I'll do it when things calm down." | Daily block with a fixed time and place (Chapter 4) |
| Missing opportunities | The block is consumed by email and calls | If-then plans for interruptions (Chapter 5) |
| Derailment | A bad day turns into a lost week | Restart rule and 15-minute floor (Chapter 9) |
| Losing sight | The goal fades by week three | Daily recording and weekly review (Chapters 6 and 8) |
| Not adjusting | Persisting with a plan that is not working | Weekly sprint review with adjust decisions (Chapter 8) |

## Vague Goals Underperform

Locke and Latham report that specific, difficult goals consistently led to higher performance than urging people to do their best, with effect sizes in meta-analyses ranging from .42 to .80. Their explanation is that "do-your-best" goals have no external referent, so people define them however they like ([Locke and Latham, 2002](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgPsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20(2002).pdf)). "Grow the business" allows almost any level of effort to feel acceptable. "Book 12 new recurring clients by March 31" does not.

## Why 30 Days

Thirty days is long enough to produce visible results on a meaningful goal and short enough to maintain focus. It divides cleanly into four weekly sprints with a buffer of two days for setup and review. It also creates a natural cadence for repeating the cycle: twelve cycles in a year, each building on the last.

## The Structure of the Program

1. **Setup (Day 0):** choose the target, define milestones, design the daily block, run a pre-mortem, write if-then plans, and recruit an accountability partner.
2. **Weeks 1 to 4:** execute daily, record progress, and hold a weekly sprint review.
3. **Day 30 review:** evaluate results, extract lessons, and decide the next target.

:::example Illustrative: Maria's Stalled Goal
Maria owns a bookkeeping practice. For a year she has intended to "do more marketing." She has started three times: a newsletter that ran two issues, a LinkedIn plan that lasted a week, and a referral idea she never launched. Each attempt failed at a different breakdown point. The newsletter stopped when tax season derailed her schedule. LinkedIn was never assigned a time. The referral program was never specified. The 30-day program will ask her to choose one specific target, protect a daily block, plan for tax-season interruptions, and record progress where her accountability partner can see it.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choosing One 30-Day Target

:::objectives
- Select one target from several candidate goals.
- Write a target that is specific, challenging, and controllable.
- Distinguish outcome measures from activity measures.
- Confirm the target is worth 30 days of focus.
:::

## Why Only One

Running several goals at once divides attention and makes it hard to tell which effort produced which result. Locke and Latham note that goal effects depend on commitment, ability, and the absence of conflicting goals ([Locke and Latham, 2006](https://www.nj.gov/highereducation/documents/pdf/NewAdvancesinGoalSettingTheory-Locke_Latham2006.pdf)). One target per 30 days reduces conflict and concentrates effort. You will still run your business; the target is the one thing that gets protected time.

## Selecting the Target

List every goal you are considering. For each, score three questions from 1 (low) to 5 (high):

1. **Impact:** If achieved, how much would it improve the business or your life?
2. **Urgency:** How costly is another month of delay?
3. **Controllability:** How much of the result depends on your own actions?

Multiply the three scores. The highest product is usually the right target, but use judgment: a goal with low controllability may need to be reframed.

## Writing the Target

A strong 30-day target has five features:

| Feature | Question | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Specific | Exactly what? | More clients | 8 new monthly bookkeeping clients |
| Measurable | How will I count it? | Better marketing | 20 referral conversations logged |
| Challenging | Does it stretch me? | Send a few emails | Send 60 personalized outreach emails |
| Controllable | Is it mostly within my actions? | Get featured in the news | Pitch 10 local publications |
| Dated | By when? | Soon | By March 31 |

## Outcome and Activity Measures

An **outcome measure** describes the result you want (clients signed, revenue collected, product launched). An **activity measure** describes the actions you control that produce the outcome (calls made, proposals sent, chapters drafted). Outcomes motivate; activities are what you can do today. A good 30-day plan names both: one outcome target and one or two activity targets that drive it.

When the outcome depends heavily on others (for example, customer purchasing decisions), the activity target becomes the primary commitment, and the outcome becomes the measure of whether the activity is working.

## Is It Worth 30 Days?

Before committing, answer in writing: Why does this matter now? What will be different on day 31 if I succeed? What will it cost me if I do not? Amabile and Kramer's research emphasizes progress in meaningful work, noting that the work need only matter to the person doing it ([Amabile and Kramer, HBR](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=40244)). A target you care about sustains effort through difficult days.

:::example Illustrative: Maria's Target
Candidate goals and scores (impact x urgency x controllability): newsletter (3 x 2 x 5 = 30); referral program (5 x 4 x 4 = 80); new website (4 x 2 x 3 = 24); hire an assistant (5 x 3 x 3 = 45).
Chosen: referral program.
Target: By March 31, hold 20 referral conversations with existing clients and professional contacts (activity), resulting in at least 6 qualified introductions (outcome).
Why now: Tax season brings her into contact with every client; referrals convert better than cold leads; she wants to replace two clients retiring this year.
:::

# Chapter 3: Four Weekly Milestones

:::objectives
- Break a 30-day target into four weekly milestones.
- Sequence milestones from preparation to completion.
- Define daily actions that move each milestone forward.
- Use small wins to sustain motivation.
:::

## Why Weekly Milestones

A 30-day target can feel distant on day 3. Weekly milestones bring the finish line closer and provide four natural checkpoints. Amabile and Kramer found that "small wins" were nearly as effective as major breakthroughs in boosting people's inner work lives, and that 28 percent of small events had a major impact on how people felt about their work ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity)). Weekly milestones are designed to produce regular, visible wins.

## The Four-Week Arc

| Week | Typical focus | Milestone question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prepare and start | What must be built or gathered so the main work can flow? |
| 2 | Build momentum | What volume of core activity proves the method works? |
| 3 | Push through the middle | What is the hardest part, and how much of it can be done this week? |
| 4 | Finish and consolidate | What completes the target and makes the gains durable? |

Week three is often the hardest. Novelty has worn off and the finish is not yet close. Plan for it by making week three's milestone concrete and slightly smaller than week two's in scope, while still meaningful.

## Writing Milestones

Each milestone should be a finished, checkable result, not an activity. "Work on the outreach list" is an activity. "Outreach list of 40 names with contact details and relationship notes complete" is a milestone.

## From Milestones to Daily Actions

For each week, list the daily actions that produce the milestone. Make each action:

- **Small enough** to complete in one daily block.
- **Concrete enough** that you know exactly what to do when you sit down.
- **Sequenced** so that each day builds on the last.

A useful test: if you read the action at the start of your block, could you begin within 60 seconds?

:::example Illustrative: Maria's Milestones
Week 1 milestone: List of 40 referral prospects with notes; referral conversation script and one-page "who I help" sheet finished.
Daily actions: Day 1 list current clients; Day 2 list professional contacts; Day 3 add notes; Day 4 draft script; Day 5 draft one-pager; Day 6 practice script with partner.
Week 2 milestone: 8 referral conversations completed and logged.
Week 3 milestone: 7 more conversations (15 total); follow-ups sent to all.
Week 4 milestone: 5 more conversations (20 total); introductions tracked; thank-you notes sent; referral process written as a repeatable checklist.
:::

:::worksheet My Milestones

Daily actions: ___

Daily actions: ___
Week 3 milestone: ___
Daily actions: ___
Week 4 milestone: ___
Daily actions: ___
:::

:::summary
Four weekly milestones bring the finish line closer and create regular small wins, which Amabile and Kramer found strongly influence motivation. Sequence weeks from preparation through momentum, the difficult middle, and completion. Write milestones as finished results, and break them into small, concrete daily actions you could start within a minute.

# Chapter 4: The Daily Block and the 15-Minute Floor

:::objectives
- Design a protected daily execution block.
- Apply implementation intentions to when and where you work.
- Set a 15-minute floor for difficult days.
- Shape your environment to reduce friction.
:::

## The Daily Block

A **daily block** is a fixed, recurring period reserved for work on your 30-day target. It is the single most important structural element of the program. Without it, the target competes with every urgent task and usually loses.

Dan Martell's Buy Back Your Time describes a "Perfect Week," a templated calendar designed in advance so that important work has a home. A summary of the book describes Martell's own template: mornings blocked for creative work, a workout at lunch, and meetings batched into the afternoons. Martell writes that when you design your week ahead of time, "you'll understand exactly what you must say no to in order to say yes to something that comes up sporadically" ([Deven Davis summary of Buy Back Your Time](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). The daily block applies the same idea to one target.

## Choosing Time, Place, and Length

| Decision | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Time | Your highest-energy period, before reactive work begins if possible |
| Place | The same location daily; somewhere with minimal interruptions |
| Length | 45 to 90 minutes for most targets |
| Days | Five or six days per week; decide in advance which days are off |
| Start cue | A specific trigger, such as "after I pour my first coffee" |

## Implementation Intentions

An **implementation intention** is an if-then plan that links a situation to a response: "When situation X arises, I will do Y." Gollwitzer and Sheeran's meta-analysis of 94 studies with more than 8,000 participants found an overall effect of d = .65 on goal attainment, which they describe as medium-to-large, beyond the effect of simply forming a goal intention ([Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006](http://ereserve.library.utah.edu/Annual/PSY/7963/Werner/Gollwitzer2006.pdf)). For getting started specifically, the National Cancer Institute's summary reports an effect of d = .61 ([NCI](https://cancercontrol.cancer.gov/brp/research/constructs/implementation-intentions)).

Write your daily block as an implementation intention: "When it is 7:30 a.m. on a weekday and I have poured my coffee, I will sit at my desk with my phone in the drawer and work on today's action for 60 minutes."

## The 15-Minute Floor

Some days will go wrong: a sick child, an emergency job, a system outage. On those days, the full block may be impossible. The **15-minute floor** is the minimum commitment you will honor no matter what. Fifteen minutes is small enough to fit almost any day and large enough to keep momentum and preserve the identity of someone who works on the target daily.

Rules for the floor:

1. It counts as a completed day in your log, marked as "floor."
2. It must involve real work on the target, not planning to work.
3. You decide in advance what a floor session looks like (for example, "send two outreach messages").
4. More than two floor days in a week trigger a review of whether the block's time or length needs to change.

## Shaping the Environment

Reduce friction for the target and increase friction for distractions:

- Prepare materials the night before (open documents, list of names, script).
- Silence notifications and place the phone out of reach.
- Tell your team when you are unavailable and who handles urgent issues.
- Use a visible timer.
- End each block by writing the first step for tomorrow.

:::example Illustrative: A Contractor's Daily Block
Devon owns a remodeling business. His target is to write and publish a set of 12 project case pages on his website in 30 days. His block: "When I arrive at the office at 6:45 a.m. on Monday through Friday, before checking email, I will write for 60 minutes at the back desk with notifications off." His 15-minute floor: "Write captions for three photos from one project." On a day when a burst pipe calls him to a job site at 6 a.m., he does his floor from his truck at lunch and logs it.
:::

# Chapter 5: The Pre-Mortem and If-Then Plans

:::objectives
- Run a personal pre-mortem on your 30-day plan.
- Rank obstacles by likelihood and impact.
- Write if-then plans for the most important obstacles.
- Distinguish obstacles you can prevent from those you must respond to.
:::

## The Pre-Mortem

Psychologist Gary Klein described the **pre-mortem** in Harvard Business Review in 2007. Instead of asking what might go wrong, a team assumes the project has already failed and generates plausible reasons for the failure ([Klein, HBR](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)). Klein's article cites 1989 research on "prospective hindsight," imagining an event has already occurred, as increasing the ability to identify reasons for future outcomes by 30 percent. Note that the underlying 1989 study measured the number of reasons people generated rather than the accuracy of their predictions, so the technique is best viewed as a reliable way to surface more potential problems, which you then must triage.

## Running a Personal Pre-Mortem

1. **Set the scene.** Imagine it is day 31. You did not reach your target. Not even close.
2. **Write for five minutes.** List every reason it failed. Be specific and honest: "Tax deadlines took every morning in week three." "I felt awkward asking for referrals and kept postponing."
3. **Include internal and external causes.** Energy, fear, and avoidance belong on the list alongside interruptions and other people.
4. **Rank each reason** by likelihood (1 to 5) and impact (1 to 5).
5. **Select the top five** by combined score.

## If-Then Plans for Obstacles

For each top obstacle, write an if-then plan. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's analysis found that implementation intentions helped with several distinct problems, including preventing derailment of goal striving, where the NCI summary reports an effect of d = .77 ([NCI](https://cancercontrol.cancer.gov/brp/research/constructs/implementation-intentions)).

| Obstacle | If (situation) | Then (response) |
|---|---|---|
| Urgent client call during block | If a client calls during my block | Then my assistant takes a message and I return the call at 9:00 |
| Low energy | If I feel too tired to start | Then I do the 15-minute floor and decide afterward whether to continue |
| Avoidance of uncomfortable task | If I notice myself reorganizing files instead of calling | Then I make one call immediately, using the script |
| Travel day | If I am traveling | Then I do the floor action from my phone before leaving the hotel |
| Missed day | If I miss a day entirely | Then I do the full block the next day, starting with yesterday's action |

## Prevent or Respond

Some obstacles can be prevented in advance (block the calendar, prepare materials, delegate a task). Others can only be responded to when they occur (illness, emergencies). Prevent what you can during setup; write if-then responses for the rest.

:::example Illustrative: Maria's Pre-Mortem
Reasons for imagined failure: tax deadlines consumed mornings (likelihood 5, impact 5); felt awkward asking (4, 5); clients did not respond to emails (3, 3); forgot to follow up (3, 4); partner check-ins lapsed (3, 3).
Prevention: Move the daily block to 7:00 to 8:00 a.m. before client work; script practice in week 1.
If-then plans: "If a client meeting is scheduled during my block, then I move the block to 4:30 p.m. the same day." "If I hesitate before a call for more than 60 seconds, then I dial the next name on the list." "If someone does not reply within 3 business days, then I send the follow-up template."

# Chapter 6: Tracking and Recording Progress

:::objectives
- Explain why recording progress improves goal attainment.
- Design a simple daily log.
- Choose a visible scoreboard.
- Avoid common tracking mistakes.
:::

## What the Evidence Says

Harkin and colleagues analyzed 138 randomized studies with nearly 20,000 participants and found that interventions that increased progress monitoring promoted goal attainment (d = 0.40). Effects were larger when progress was reported or made public and when the information was physically recorded ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)). In their data, physically recording progress produced larger effects (d = 0.43) than not recording it (d = 0.29) ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)). The American Psychological Association summarized the practical lesson: record, report, or make public what you find as you assess progress ([APA](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals)).

## The Daily Log

Keep the log short enough to complete in two minutes at the end of each block. Each entry records:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Date and day number | Keeps the 30-day count visible |
| Block completed? (Full / Floor / Missed) | Tracks consistency |
| Action planned | Links to the milestone plan |
| Action completed | Records what actually happened |
| Activity count | Updates the activity measure (for example, conversations held) |
| Small win | Names one bit of progress, however small |
| Obstacle met and response | Feeds the weekly review |
| First step for tomorrow | Reduces startup friction |

## The Scoreboard

A **scoreboard** displays cumulative progress toward the target where you see it daily: a whiteboard, a printed chart, or a shared spreadsheet. Show the activity count against the target and the outcome count against its target. A simple line or bar chart that rises over 30 days provides a visual record of progress.

## Tracking Mistakes to Avoid

- **Tracking too much.** Many metrics dilute attention; track one outcome and one or two activities.
- **Tracking only outcomes.** When outcomes lag, the log looks empty and discouraging; track activities too.
- **Back-filling.** Logging days later from memory reduces accuracy; log at the end of each block.
- **Private-only tracking.** Harkin's findings suggest reporting progress to someone strengthens the effect (Chapter 7).

:::example Illustrative: Devon's Log Entry
Day 8 (Tuesday). Block: Full (60 min). Planned: draft case page for the Elm Street kitchen. Completed: draft finished, 640 words, 8 photos captioned. Activity count: 4 of 12 pages drafted. Small win: client agreed to a quote for the page. Obstacle: supplier call at 7:10; let it go to voicemail per if-then plan, returned at 8:00. Tomorrow's first step: open Maple Avenue folder and choose the six best photos.
:::

:::checklist Tracking Setup
- Daily log template ready (printed or digital).
- Log completed at the end of each block, not later.
- One outcome measure and one or two activity measures defined.
- Scoreboard posted where I see it daily.
- Weekly totals carried into the sprint log.
- Progress reported to my accountability partner (Chapter 7).
:::

:::summary
A meta-analysis of 138 studies found that monitoring progress promotes goal attainment, especially when progress is physically recorded and reported to others. Keep a two-minute daily log with completion status, actions, counts, a small win, obstacles, and tomorrow's first step. Post a scoreboard, track activities as well as outcomes, and log at the time rather than later.
:::

# Chapter 7: Accountability Partners and Reporting

:::objectives
- Explain why reporting progress to another person helps.
- Choose an effective accountability partner.
- Structure a short weekly check-in.
- Set ground rules that keep the partnership useful.
:::

## Why Reporting Matters

In Harkin and colleagues' meta-analysis, monitoring had larger effects when progress was reported to others or made public than when it was kept private ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)). The APA's summary of the study put the point plainly: the more frequently you monitor progress, the more likely you are to succeed, and reporting or publicly sharing it strengthens the effect ([APA](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals)). A caution is warranted: the subset of studies involving fully public reporting was small, so the size of that particular effect is less certain. The broad direction, that telling someone your progress helps, is consistent across the analysis.

## Choosing a Partner

An **accountability partner** is a person who agrees to receive your progress reports on a fixed schedule and to ask direct questions. Good partners share four traits:

| Trait | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Reliable | Shows up to every check-in; inconsistency teaches you that reports are optional |
| Candid | Willing to ask "Did you do what you said?" without softening |
| Neutral | Not your direct report, whose candor may be limited by the relationship |
| Engaged in their own goal | Mutual partnerships create balance and shared commitment |

Options include a peer business owner, a mastermind group, a mentor, or a business coach. A spouse or close friend can work, but the relationship sometimes makes candor harder.

## The Weekly Check-In

Keep the check-in to 15 minutes, at the same time each week, with a fixed agenda:

1. **Numbers (3 minutes).** Activity count and outcome count versus milestone.
2. **Commitments (3 minutes).** What you said you would do last week, and what you did.
3. **Obstacle (4 minutes).** The biggest obstacle and how you will handle it.
4. **Next week (3 minutes).** Your milestone and three specific commitments.
5. **Partner's turn (if mutual).** Repeat for the other person.

A brief daily text, "Day 12: full block, 2 conversations, 9 total," adds a lightweight daily reporting layer without more meetings.

## Ground Rules

- Report facts first, explanations second.
- The partner asks questions rather than giving lectures.
- No rescheduling except by mutual agreement made at least 24 hours in advance.
- Missed check-ins are themselves reported and discussed.
- Either party may end the partnership after the 30 days without explanation.

:::case Progress Reporting in Goal Research
The strongest public evidence for reporting comes from research rather than a single company. Harkin and colleagues, at the University of Sheffield, pooled 138 randomized studies covering 19,951 participants across goals such as weight loss, exercise, and blood pressure. Interventions that prompted people to monitor progress improved goal attainment, and the effect grew when progress was reported to someone or recorded physically ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/); [APA](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals)). The studies concern health and personal goals, so applying them to business targets is an inference, though a reasonable one. These are research findings; their results are their own, not TTI's.
:::

Commitments: I said I would call 10 clients; I called 11 and reached 9. Obstacle: two clients asked what to say to the people they refer. # Chapter 8: The Weekly Sprint Review

:::objectives
- Conduct a 30-minute weekly sprint review.
- Make one of four decisions: continue, adjust, accelerate, or reset.
- Use small wins to maintain motivation.
- Plan the next week's milestone and daily actions.
:::

## Why Review Weekly

A weekly review turns the daily log into decisions. Without it, you may repeat ineffective actions for weeks. Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer studied nearly 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 knowledge workers in seven companies. They found that making progress in meaningful work was the event most frequently associated with people's best days, and that setbacks had a negative effect on mood two to three times stronger than the positive effect of progress ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity); [Amabile and Kramer, HBR](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)). A weekly review helps you notice progress that might otherwise be overshadowed by a few setbacks.

## The 30-Minute Review Agenda

| Step | Time | Questions |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Gather data | 5 min | What do the log and scoreboard show? Blocks completed? Counts? |
| 2. Compare to milestone | 5 min | Did I hit this week's milestone? By how much did I miss or exceed it? |
| 3. Name small wins | 5 min | What progress did I make, however small? |
| 4. Analyze obstacles | 5 min | What got in the way? Did my if-then plans work? |
| 5. Decide | 5 min | Continue, adjust, accelerate, or reset? |
| 6. Plan next week | 5 min | Milestone, daily actions, block times, partner commitments |

## The Four Decisions

- **Continue.** You hit the milestone and the approach works. Keep going.
- **Adjust.** You missed the milestone for a fixable reason. Change one thing: the block time, the script, the channel.
- **Accelerate.** You exceeded the milestone easily. Raise next week's milestone or add a stretch action.
- **Reset.** The target itself was wrong (unrealistic, irrelevant, or blocked by an outside event). Rewrite the target for the remaining days. Use this rarely; it is not a way to avoid discomfort.

Change only one major variable at a time so you can tell what made a difference.

## Small Wins

Amabile and Kramer reported that even small steps forward frequently had an outsized effect on how people felt about their work: 28 percent of minor incidents in their data had a major effect on people's feelings ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity)). Write down small wins each day and review them weekly: a hard call made, a page finished, a process documented.

:::case Amabile and Kramer's Progress Principle
Harvard Business School professor Teresa Amabile and researcher Steven Kramer asked 238 professionals on 26 project teams to complete daily diaries over the course of their projects. Analyzing nearly 12,000 entries, they found that on days when workers reported progress, their inner work life (emotions, motivation, perceptions) was more positive, and that progress was the most prominent feature of their best days. They called this the "progress principle" and recommended that managers support progress through "catalysts," actions that directly support work, and "nourishers," such as encouragement and respect ([Amabile and Kramer, HBR](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins); [HBS faculty page](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=40244)). These are research findings from their study participants; their results are their own, not TTI's.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Devon's Week 2 Review
Data: 4 full blocks, 1 floor. 3 pages drafted this week (5 total) against a milestone of 6. Small wins: two clients approved quotes; photo folders organized. Obstacle: photo selection took longer than writing. Decision: Adjust. Devon will ask his office manager to preselect six photos per project each Friday, so his blocks go to writing. Next week's milestone: 9 pages total.
:::

# Chapter 9: Setbacks, Missed Days, and Restarting

:::objectives
- Treat missed days as data rather than failure.
- Apply a "never miss twice" restart rule.
- Diagnose the cause of a setback.
- Recover from a lost week without abandoning the sprint.
:::

## Setbacks Are Expected

Every 30-day sprint includes missed days. The goal is not perfection but recovery. Because setbacks affect motivation more strongly than progress, as Amabile and Kramer found ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)), a single missed day can feel like failure and lead to abandoning the plan entirely. A deliberate recovery plan prevents that spiral.

## The Restart Rule

The **restart rule** states: never miss twice in a row. If you miss a day, the next day's block is non-negotiable, even if only the 15-minute floor. Write it as an if-then plan: "If I miss my block, then the next morning I start with the floor action before opening email." Implementation intentions have been found to help with derailment of goal pursuit, with the NCI summary reporting d = .77 for that category ([NCI](https://cancercontrol.cancer.gov/brp/research/constructs/implementation-intentions)).

## Diagnosing a Setback

| Cause category | Signs | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Schedule | Block repeatedly displaced by meetings or client work | Move block earlier; protect with calendar and team instructions |
| Energy | Starting is hard; sessions are unproductive | Shorten block; move to higher-energy time; check sleep |
| Clarity | Unsure what to do when sitting down | Write tomorrow's first step at end of each block |
| Avoidance | Busy with other tasks instead of the hard one | Do the hard action first; use a script; tell the partner |
| Capacity | Target requires skills or resources you lack | Get help, delegate, or reset target |
| External | Illness, family emergency, disaster | Pause, then reset the timeline; no self-blame |

## Recovering from a Lost Week

If you lose most of a week:

1. Report it to your partner within 24 hours.
2. Hold an unscheduled review using the Chapter 8 agenda.
3. Decide whether to extend the sprint (for example, to 37 days) or reduce the target proportionally.
4. Restart with a full block the next day.

:::example Illustrative: Maria's Lost Days
In week 3, a client's audit notice consumed three mornings. Maria did the floor action (two text messages to clients) on two of them and missed one entirely. She reported to Janelle that evening, diagnosed the cause as "external plus schedule," and moved her blocks to 4:30 p.m. for the rest of the week. She finished the week at 14 conversations against a milestone of 15, then made up the difference on day 22.
:::

:::checklist Setback Recovery
- Missed day logged honestly as "missed."
- Next day's block completed (never miss twice).
- Cause categorized: schedule, energy, clarity, avoidance, capacity, external.
- One fix chosen and scheduled.
- Partner informed within 24 hours.
- Timeline or target adjusted if a week or more was lost.
:::

:::summary
Missed days are expected; recovery matters more than perfection. Follow the restart rule of never missing twice, diagnose setbacks by cause, choose one fix, and report promptly to your partner. After a lost week, hold an extra review and either extend the sprint or reduce the target.
:::

# Chapter 10: The Day 30 Review and the Next Cycle

:::objectives
- Conduct a structured final review on day 30.
- Separate outcome results from process lessons.
- Decide what to keep, drop, and systematize.
- Plan the next 30-day cycle within a longer-term plan.
:::

## The Final Review

Day 30 is not just the end of a sprint; it is the most valuable learning moment of the cycle. Set aside 60 to 90 minutes. Bring your log, scoreboard, weekly reviews, and pre-mortem.

## Four Questions

1. **Results.** What were the final outcome and activity numbers versus the target?
2. **Process.** What percentage of blocks were full, floor, or missed? Which if-then plans worked?
3. **Lessons.** What did I learn about the work, the market, and myself?
4. **Next.** What will I keep, drop, and systematize?

Separate results from process. A missed target with strong execution may reveal that the target was too ambitious or that the market needs more time. A hit target with weak execution may reflect luck rather than a repeatable process.

## Keep, Drop, Systematize

| Category | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Keep | What habit or practice should continue? | Daily 7:00 a.m. block |
| Drop | What wasted time or did not work? | Email outreach with no phone follow-up |
| Systematize | What should become a documented process or be delegated? | Referral thank-you note sent by assistant within 48 hours |

## Connecting Cycles to a Longer Plan

A single 30-day sprint produces focused progress. A series of sprints, each chosen deliberately, can build toward annual goals. Martell's Buy Back Your Time extends the Perfect Week into a "Preloaded Year," which schedules the year's most important commitments, Covey-style "big rocks," onto a 12-month calendar before other demands fill it, and works backward from longer-range goals in checkpoints ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). You can use the same logic to map your next three 30-day targets against a quarterly or annual goal.

:::case Dan Martell's Perfect Week and Preloaded Year
Dan Martell is a Canadian entrepreneur and coach who wrote Buy Back Your Time (2023). The book argues that owners should design their time proactively rather than reacting to whatever arrives. Summaries describe his Perfect Week as a templated calendar with creative work in the mornings, a lunchtime workout, and meetings batched into afternoons, and his Preloaded Year as a way of placing the year's key commitments on the calendar in advance ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time); [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/buy-back-your-time/summary/)). Martell's practices and any results he reports are his own, not TTI's; this book uses his framework only as a public example of planning time around priorities.
:::

If-then plans for client meetings worked; the email follow-up template was rarely needed because phone calls worked better. Keep: morning block and Friday check-ins. Drop: email-first outreach. Systematize: her assistant sends a handwritten thank-you card to every referrer. Next cycle target: convert introductions into onboarding meetings, with a goal of 5 onboarding meetings by April 30.

# Chapter 11: Running a 30-Day Sprint with a Team

:::objectives
- Adapt the 30-day method for a small team.
- Assign clear ownership for team targets.
- Run a short weekly team review.
- Avoid common pitfalls of team accountability.
:::

## From Individual to Team

The same method works for a team of three to fifteen people with a few adjustments. The team chooses one shared target, each member owns specific milestones or activities, and progress is visible to everyone.

## Ownership

Every target, milestone, and action needs a single named owner. Shared ownership often becomes no ownership. Martell's book describes a "Definition of Done," a clear description of what a completed task looks like, and a "1-3-1" rule in which team members bring one problem, three possible solutions, and one recommendation rather than bringing problems alone ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). Both are useful in team sprints: define done for each milestone and ask owners to bring 1-3-1 proposals when stuck.

## Team Scoreboard and Review

| Element | Team version |
|---|---|
| Target | One shared, specific, measurable target |
| Milestones | Weekly, each with a named owner |
| Daily block | Each owner protects time for their actions |
| Log | Shared spreadsheet updated daily by each owner |
| Scoreboard | Visible board or dashboard showing team totals |
| Check-in | 15-minute weekly team review; brief daily update in chat |
| Final review | 60-minute team retrospective on day 30 |

## The Weekly Team Review

1. Scoreboard review (5 minutes).
2. Each owner: done, not done, blocker (2 minutes each).
3. Blockers discussed using 1-3-1 proposals.
4. Decision: continue, adjust, accelerate, or reset.
5. Next week's milestones and owners confirmed.

## Pitfalls

- **Blame culture.** Treat misses as process data. Amabile and Kramer's research emphasizes that supporting progress and respecting people improves inner work life ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)).
- **Too many targets.** One team target per cycle.
- **Invisible progress.** Update the scoreboard daily.
- **Manager does the work.** Owners own; the manager removes obstacles.

:::example Illustrative: A Dental Office Team Sprint
An office manager and four staff choose a 30-day target: "Reduce unconfirmed appointments for the following day to fewer than 5 by 3:00 p.m. each day, measured for the last 10 business days of the month." Front desk lead owns the confirmation call list; a hygienist owns a script for text reminders; the office manager owns the weekly review. They track daily counts on a whiteboard in the break room. This example is hypothetical.
:::

:::checklist Team Sprint Setup
- One shared target written and posted.
- Weekly milestones with named owners and definitions of done.
- Shared log created; owners know when to update it.
- Team scoreboard visible.
- Weekly 15-minute review scheduled.
- 1-3-1 rule explained.
- Day 30 retrospective scheduled.
:::

:::summary
A team sprint uses one shared target, named owners for each milestone, a shared log and visible scoreboard, and a short weekly review. Martell's Definition of Done and 1-3-1 rule help clarify completion and problem solving. Avoid blame, multiple targets, invisible progress, and managers doing the owners' work.
:::

# Appendix B: If-Then Plan Library

Choose, adapt, and write down the plans that fit your most likely obstacles.

## Getting Started

- If it is my block time and I have not started, then I set a timer for 15 minutes and begin the floor action.
- If I sit down and do not know what to do, then I read yesterday's "first step for tomorrow" line and do exactly that.
- If I feel resistance to a hard task, then I do the hardest action first for 10 minutes.

## Interruptions

- If a team member interrupts during my block, then I say, "I'm in my block until 8:30; can it wait, or is it an emergency?"
- If my phone buzzes, then I leave it face down until the timer ends.
- If an email notification appears, then I close the email program.

## Schedule Conflicts

- If a meeting is scheduled over my block, then I move the block to the next open 60-minute slot the same day.
- If I am traveling, then I do the floor action before leaving my lodging.
- If a client emergency consumes the day, then I do the floor action before bed.

## Motivation and Setbacks

- If I miss a day, then I do the block first thing the next day before opening email.
- If I miss a weekly milestone, then I hold the weekly review on schedule and choose one adjustment.
- If I feel discouraged, then I reread my small wins from the past seven days.
- If I want to quit the target, then I talk to my accountability partner before deciding.

## Accountability

- If I have not sent my daily update by 6:00 p.m., then I send it immediately, even if the update is "missed."
- If a check-in is about to be canceled, then I propose a new time within 24 hours.

# Appendix E: Frequently Asked Questions

**What if my goal needs more than 30 days?** Break it into 30-day targets that each produce a visible, measurable result. A six-month project becomes six sprints with defined outputs.

**Can I run two targets at once?** It is possible, but this workbook recommends one. Locke and Latham note that goal conflict can undermine performance ([Locke and Latham, 2006](https://www.nj.gov/highereducation/documents/pdf/NewAdvancesinGoalSettingTheory-Locke_Latham2006.pdf)), and a single target keeps the daily block focused.

**What if I have no one to be my partner?** Use a written weekly report sent to a mentor, a peer group, or a coach. Even recording progress yourself, physically and daily, was associated with benefits in Harkin and colleagues' analysis ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)).

**What if I hit the target early?** Choose "accelerate" at the next weekly review: raise the target or add a stretch milestone. Record the early finish as data for setting future targets.

**What if I miss the target?** Run the day 30 review honestly. Separate results from process. A missed target with strong execution is useful information for the next cycle.

**Is the 15-minute floor cheating?** No. It is a planned minimum that keeps the daily habit intact on difficult days. If floor days become frequent, adjust the block.

**Should I use an app?** Any tool that is fast and visible works. Paper often works well because it is physically recorded and always available.

# Glossary

**Accountability partner.** A person who agrees to receive progress reports on a fixed schedule and to ask direct questions.

**Activity measure.** A count of actions you control, such as calls made or pages written.

**Daily block.** A fixed, recurring period reserved for work on the 30-day target.

**Definition of Done.** A clear description of what a completed task or milestone looks like.

**Floor day.** A day on which only the 15-minute minimum was completed.

**15-minute floor.** The minimum daily commitment honored regardless of circumstances.

**If-then plan.** See implementation intention.

**Implementation intention.** A plan linking a specific situation to a specific response: "When X, I will Y."

**Milestone.** A weekly checkpoint that breaks the 30-day target into four measurable steps.

**Outcome measure.** A count of results, which may lag behind activity, such as introductions received.

**Perfect Week.** Dan Martell's term for a templated weekly calendar designed in advance.

**Pre-mortem.** A planning technique in which you assume the project has failed and list reasons why.

**Preloaded Year.** Martell's term for scheduling a year's key commitments on the calendar in advance.

**Progress principle.** Amabile and Kramer's finding that progress in meaningful work is strongly associated with positive inner work life.

**Restart rule.** The commitment never to miss the daily block two days in a row.

**Scoreboard.** A visible display of cumulative progress toward the target.

**Sprint.** A focused, time-limited period of work on one target; in this workbook, 30 days.

**Weekly sprint review.** A 30-minute session that converts the week's data into a decision.

**1-3-1 rule.** A practice in which team members bring one problem, three options, and one recommendation.
