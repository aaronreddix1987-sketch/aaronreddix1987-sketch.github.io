# How to Use This Book

This book is written for people who intend to open a small online store and want to do it carefully: a maker selling goods from a spare room, a retailer adding a web channel, or a first-time founder testing a product idea before committing savings to inventory. It assumes no background in accounting, law, or marketing. It does assume that you are willing to do arithmetic, read primary sources, and make decisions on evidence rather than enthusiasm.

The central argument of the book is simple. An online store is not a website; it is a promise. When a customer clicks "Buy," you have promised to charge the correct amount, protect their payment data, ship the right item within the time you stated, answer their questions, and treat a return fairly. The storefront software is the easiest part of that promise to keep. The hard parts are the economics underneath each order and the operations that follow it. Most of this book is therefore about those hard parts.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of the book you should be able to:

- Test whether a product has real demand before buying significant stock.
- Build a per-order profit model (unit economics) and a break-even estimate for your first year.
- Identify the federal and state obligations that commonly apply to small online sellers, including sales tax collection and the Federal Trade Commission's shipping rule.
- Select and configure a store platform against written criteria instead of advertising.
- Write product pages that persuade without making claims you cannot support.
- Design a fulfillment, shipping, and returns process that a customer would describe as fair.
- Plan a modest, honest launch and measure the first ninety days with a small set of metrics.

## How each chapter is built

Every chapter follows the same pattern so that you can study it the way you would study a university text. It opens with learning objectives. It then defines the key terms in plain language and explains the reasoning behind them. Each chapter presents a step-by-step framework, at least one worked example with real arithmetic, and, where relevant, a case study of a real company drawn from public reporting. Chapters close with a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. The appendices collect the templates you will use repeatedly.

The worked examples follow a fictional seller, Maya, who makes and sells soy candles. Maya is illustrative only. She is not a TTI customer, and her numbers are not predictions. The case studies describe real companies, such as Gymshark, Shopify, and Spanx, using facts reported by the companies themselves or by reputable publications; their results are their own, not TTI's, and are included to illustrate principles, not to suggest that similar outcomes are typical or likely.

:::callout A note on evidence
Where this book cites a law, rule, or statistic, the full source appears in the References chapter. Rules change and vary by state and city. Treat this book as a map of the questions to ask, then confirm the current answer with the agency or a licensed professional before you act. Nothing in this book is legal, tax, or financial advice, and no sales, income, or profit is promised.
:::

# Chapter 1: The Economics of an Online Store

:::objectives
- Define revenue, cost of goods sold, gross margin, and contribution margin as they apply to a single order.
- Explain why many small stores lose money on orders that appear profitable.
- Build a one-order profit model that includes every variable cost.
- Distinguish fixed costs from variable costs and explain why the distinction matters for decisions.
:::

Students of business are often surprised to learn that a store can grow its sales and its losses at the same time. The explanation is almost always found in the economics of a single order. If each order loses a little money after every cost is counted, then more orders produce more losses. Before you think about logos, platforms, or advertising, you need to understand what one order earns.

## Key definitions

Revenue is the money the customer pays you, usually the product price plus any shipping you charge. Sales tax you collect is not revenue; you are holding it on behalf of the state.

Cost of goods sold (COGS) is what it costs you to make or buy the item you ship: materials, the wholesale price, inbound freight, and direct labor if you pay someone to make it.

Gross margin is revenue minus COGS, often expressed as a percentage of revenue. A product that sells for $30 and costs $9 to make has a gross margin of $21, or 70 percent.

Variable costs are costs that occur because an order occurs. Beyond COGS, the common variable costs for an online store are payment processing fees, packaging, outbound shipping, platform transaction fees, and the expected cost of returns and damaged goods.

Contribution margin is revenue minus all variable costs. It is the amount each order contributes toward paying fixed costs and, eventually, profit. It is the single most important number in this book.

Fixed costs are costs that do not change with the number of orders in a month: the platform subscription, software, insurance, a storage unit, your accountant, and any salary you pay yourself.

## Why apparently profitable orders lose money

New sellers tend to compare the price with the product cost and stop there. The gap between gross margin and contribution margin is where most small stores quietly fail. Shipping is commonly the largest leak, particularly when a seller offers "free shipping" without raising prices to absorb it. Payment processing typically takes a few percent of each transaction plus a fixed per-transaction fee; on a small order the fixed fee is a meaningful share. Returns carry the cost of return postage, the labor to inspect the item, and the loss on items that cannot be resold. Finally, if you acquire customers through paid advertising, the advertising cost per order is a variable cost too.

## A framework: the one-order profit model

Work through these five steps for every product before you buy inventory.

1. Record the price the customer pays, including any shipping charge, and exclude sales tax.
2. List COGS per unit, including inbound freight divided across units.
3. List every other variable cost per order: packaging, outbound postage, payment fees, platform fees, and a returns allowance.
4. Subtract the total variable cost from revenue to get contribution margin in dollars and as a percentage.
5. Divide your expected monthly fixed costs by the contribution margin per order to estimate how many orders you need each month to break even.

:::example Maya's first candle (illustrative)
Maya plans to sell an 8-ounce candle for $28 and charge $5 for shipping, so revenue per order is $33. Her costs per candle are wax, wick, fragrance, and jar at $6.40, plus $0.60 of inbound freight, for COGS of $7.00. Packaging (box, filler, label) costs $1.40. Ground postage for a 1.5-pound parcel averages $9.20 in her rate quotes. Her processor charges 2.9 percent plus $0.30, which on $33 is $1.26. She sets a returns and breakage allowance of 3 percent of revenue, or $0.99.

Total variable cost: 7.00 + 1.40 + 9.20 + 1.26 + 0.99 = $19.85.
Contribution margin: 33.00 − 19.85 = $13.15 per order, or about 40 percent.

Maya's fixed costs are a $39 platform plan, $25 of software, $30 of insurance, and $60 of other expenses, for $154 per month. Break-even volume is 154 ÷ 13.15 ≈ 11.7, so she needs about 12 orders a month before she pays herself anything. If she wants $1,500 a month for her own labor, she needs (154 + 1,500) ÷ 13.15 ≈ 126 orders.

Notice what happens if Maya offers free shipping without changing her price: revenue falls to $28, payment fees fall slightly to $1.11, and contribution falls to roughly $8.30. Her break-even for the same goals rises to about 199 orders a month.
:::

| Line item | Per order | Share of revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (price + shipping charged) | $33.00 | 100% |
| Cost of goods sold | $7.00 | 21% |
| Packaging | $1.40 | 4% |
| Outbound postage | $9.20 | 28% |
| Payment processing | $1.26 | 4% |
| Returns and breakage allowance | $0.99 | 3% |
| Contribution margin | $13.15 | 40% |

The table makes visible what a quick glance hides: shipping, not the product, is Maya's largest cost. That single observation will shape her packaging choices, her pricing, and the products she adds later.

:::case Shopify began as one merchant's problem
Shopify's own history describes how its founders started an online snowboard store called Snowdevil in 2004 and, finding existing e-commerce software inadequate, built their own [1]. NPR's interview with Tobias Lütke reports that other merchants asked to license the software, and the founders launched Shopify as a platform in 2006 [2]. The lesson for a new seller is not about software. It is that the founders were close enough to their own operations to see where the friction was. Your one-order model is the same habit applied to money: you look at every step between the click and the delivered parcel and ask what it costs.
:::

## Fixed costs and the danger of premature overhead

Fixed costs are dangerous early because they are owed whether or not you sell. A common pattern is the founder who signs a twelve-month software contract, rents storage, and orders a large print run of branded boxes before the first sale. Each decision may be sensible on its own; together they raise the break-even point before demand has been demonstrated. A prudent rule is to keep fixed costs as low as you reasonably can until you have three consecutive months of orders at or above break-even.

:::checklist One-order model check
- I have excluded sales tax from revenue.
- COGS includes inbound freight spread across units.
- Postage is based on real quotes for my actual packed weight and dimensions, not a guess.
- Payment fees include both the percentage and the per-transaction fee.
- I have a returns and breakage allowance, even if it is small.
- I have calculated contribution margin in dollars and as a percentage.
- I know my monthly fixed costs and my break-even order count.
:::

:::summary
The economic unit of an online store is the order. Gross margin compares price with product cost; contribution margin subtracts every variable cost, including shipping, payment fees, packaging, and returns. Contribution margin divided into fixed costs gives a break-even order count, which is the first realistic test of whether a product can support a business. Shipping is frequently the largest variable cost, and free-shipping offers must be paid for somewhere. Keep fixed costs low until demand is proven.
:::

# Chapter 2: Product Selection and Demand Validation

:::objectives
- Apply explicit criteria to screen product ideas for shipping, regulatory, and margin risk.
- Distinguish stated interest from revealed demand.
- Design and run a low-cost demand test before buying significant inventory.
- Interpret the results of a demand test without wishful thinking.
:::

The most expensive mistake in e-commerce is buying a large quantity of something that does not sell. Product selection is therefore not a matter of taste but of evidence. In this chapter we treat it as a research problem: state a hypothesis about who will buy and why, then collect the cheapest evidence that could prove the hypothesis wrong.

## Screening criteria

Before you test demand, eliminate ideas that are structurally difficult for a small seller. A useful screen asks six questions.

1. Shipping profile: Is the item small, light, and sturdy? Bulky or fragile goods raise postage and breakage costs.
2. Regulation: Does the product fall into a category with extra rules, such as food, cosmetics, dietary supplements, children's products, or items containing lithium batteries? These categories may require testing, labeling, or registration. They are not forbidden, but they demand preparation.
3. Margin room: Can you sell at a price that leaves a healthy contribution margin after shipping? A product that must sell for under $15 rarely survives postage.
4. Differentiation: Can you say in one sentence why a buyer would choose yours over the alternatives?
5. Repeat potential: Will a satisfied customer buy again, or is it a once-in-a-lifetime purchase?
6. Founder fit: Do you understand the product and the buyer well enough to answer questions credibly?

| Criterion | Low risk | High risk |
|---|---|---|
| Shipping | Under 2 lb, sturdy | Oversized, fragile, liquid |
| Regulation | General merchandise | Food, cosmetics, supplements, kids' items, batteries |
| Price point | $25 or more | Under $15 |
| Differentiation | Clear one-sentence reason | "Like the others, but mine" |
| Repeat purchase | Consumable or collectible | One-time purchase |
| Founder fit | Deep knowledge of buyer | New to the category |

## Stated interest versus revealed demand

Economists distinguish stated preferences, which are what people say they would do, from revealed preferences, which are what they actually do when money is at stake. Friends who tell you they would "definitely buy that" are offering stated preferences, and politeness inflates them. Revealed demand is a deposit, a pre-order, a paid sample, or a purchase by a stranger. Your research should move as quickly as possible from the first kind of evidence to the second.

The U.S. Small Business Administration recommends that new businesses study their market and competitors using both published data and direct research with potential customers [3]. For a small store, direct research usually means three activities: reading reviews of competing products to learn what buyers complain about, interviewing people who already buy in the category, and running a small paid test.

## A five-step demand test

1. Write the hypothesis in one sentence: "Adults who buy candles for home use will pay $28 for a lightly scented candle with a clean ingredient list."
2. Read at least fifty public reviews of comparable products and tally the most common complaints and praises.
3. Interview eight to twelve people who have bought in the category in the last six months. Ask about their last purchase, not about your product: what they bought, why, what they paid, and what disappointed them.
4. Make a small batch and offer it for sale to people outside your personal circle through a simple product page, a local market, or a pre-order form with a clear delivery date.
5. Decide in advance what result would count as success, such as twenty paid orders from strangers within thirty days at full price.

:::example Maya's pre-order test (illustrative)
Maya's review reading showed that the most common complaint about competing candles was overpowering fragrance, followed by tunneling (the wax burning down the middle). Her interviews confirmed that several buyers chose unscented candles because scented ones gave them headaches. She made 40 candles in two lightly scented versions and one unscented version and offered them on a one-page store and at one weekend market.

Her success threshold, written before the test, was 25 paid orders at $28 from people she did not know. She sold 31 candles: 19 at the market and 12 online. The unscented version sold 15 units, nearly half the total. Two buyers asked about a larger size. She did not count the four purchases made by friends.

Maya concluded that demand existed at her price and that the unscented product deserved more inventory than she had planned. She also noted the limitation of her test: one market and a small online audience are not a national sample.
:::

:::case Gymshark started by making the product its founder wanted
Ben Francis and Lewis Morgan founded Gymshark in 2012, first drop-shipping supplements and then making fitted gym clothing in Francis's parents' garage using a sewing machine and screen printer [4][5]. Forbes reports that Francis sent product to fitness YouTubers he admired and that a booth at the BodyPower expo in 2013 produced a sell-out, after which the brand's daily sales grew sharply [4]. Two lessons are relevant at this stage. First, Francis was a member of the market he served and could judge fit and style credibly. Second, the early product was made in small quantities, close to demand, before any large inventory commitment. Gymshark's later scale is unusual and should not be treated as a typical outcome.
:::

## Interpreting results honestly

A demand test can fail in three ways: too few sales, sales only to friends, or sales only at a discount. Each tells you something different. Too few sales at full price may mean the wrong product, the wrong price, or the wrong audience; change one variable and retest. Sales only to friends mean you have not yet tested the market at all. Sales only at a discount mean your real price point may be lower than your model can tolerate. In every case, return to your one-order model before ordering more stock.

Product selection is a research task. Screen ideas for shipping, regulation, margin, differentiation, repeat potential, and founder fit before testing them. Prefer revealed demand, such as paid orders from strangers, to stated interest. Set your success threshold before you begin, and interpret disappointing results by changing one variable at a time. # Chapter 3: Pricing, Costs, and the Break-Even Model

:::objectives
- Compare cost-plus, competitor-based, and value-based pricing and know when each is appropriate.
- Set a price that preserves a target contribution margin after shipping.
- Build a twelve-month break-even and cash model for a first inventory order.
- Explain how discounts affect the number of orders required to earn the same profit.
:::

Price is the most powerful lever a small seller controls. A modest change in price often changes profit more than a large change in volume, because every additional dollar of price flows directly to contribution margin. Yet new sellers commonly underprice, reasoning that a low price will win customers. This chapter shows how to set a defensible price and how to see in advance what a given price implies for your workload and cash.

## Three pricing methods

Cost-plus pricing starts with your total variable cost and adds a target margin. It guarantees that each order is profitable on paper but ignores what customers are willing to pay.

Competitor-based pricing places your product relative to comparable items. It anchors you in market reality but can trap you in a race to the bottom if your costs differ from those of larger competitors.

Value-based pricing asks what the outcome is worth to the buyer. Alex Hormozi's widely cited "value equation" in $100M Offers frames perceived value as rising with the dream outcome and the buyer's perceived likelihood of achieving it, and falling with time delay and effort [6]. For a physical product, this translates into concrete questions: does the product reliably solve the problem, does it arrive quickly, and is it easy to use?

In practice, sound pricing uses all three: cost-plus sets the floor, competitor prices describe the market, and value determines where within that range you can credibly sit.

## Pricing to a target contribution margin

If you know your variable costs and the contribution margin percentage you need, you can solve for price. Percentage-based costs, such as payment fees and a returns allowance, complicate the arithmetic slightly because they rise with price. The formula is:

Price = (fixed per-order costs) ÷ (1 − target margin % − percentage costs %)

where fixed per-order costs are COGS, packaging, postage, and the fixed part of the payment fee.

:::example Solving for Maya's price (illustrative)
Maya wants a 45 percent contribution margin and wants to offer free shipping. Her fixed per-order costs are COGS $7.00 + packaging $1.40 + postage $9.20 + payment fixed fee $0.30 = $17.90. Her percentage costs are payment 2.9 percent plus returns 3 percent = 5.9 percent.

Price = 17.90 ÷ (1 − 0.45 − 0.059) = 17.90 ÷ 0.491 ≈ $36.46.

To offer free shipping at a 45 percent contribution margin, Maya would need to price her candle at about $36. Comparable candles in her research sell for $24 to $38. She therefore has three options: charge $36 with free shipping and position the candle at the premium end, keep $28 and charge for shipping, or set a free-shipping threshold, such as free shipping on orders of two or more candles, so that postage is spread across more product.
:::

## The free-shipping threshold

A free-shipping threshold is a common compromise. The idea is that the second item in a parcel adds a small amount of weight but a full item's worth of revenue. If one candle costs $9.20 to ship and two cost $11.10, the per-candle postage falls from $9.20 to $5.55. Set the threshold just above the price of your most common single-item order so that customers have a reason to add a second item.

## Discounts and the volume required to compensate

Discounts are attractive at launch, but the arithmetic is unforgiving. Because the discount comes entirely out of contribution margin, a 20 percent discount on a product with a 40 percent contribution margin cuts the margin per order in half. You would need roughly twice as many orders to earn the same total contribution.

| Discount | Contribution per $33 order (40% base) | Orders needed to match 100 full-price orders |
|---|---|---|
| None | $13.15 | 100 |
| 10% | about $9.90 | about 133 |
| 15% | about $8.27 | about 159 |
| 20% | about $6.65 | about 198 |
| 25% | about $5.02 | about 262 |

Figures assume the discount applies to the whole $33 order and payment fees fall proportionally; they are illustrative and rounded.

## The first-year cash model

Break-even per month is not the same as cash survival. Inventory is purchased before it is sold, so the first inventory order is a cash outflow that may take months to recover. A simple cash model has four lines per month: opening cash, cash in from orders, cash out for inventory and variable costs, and cash out for fixed costs. The question it answers is "What is the lowest point my bank balance reaches?"

:::example Maya's first inventory order (illustrative)
Maya plans to buy materials for 300 candles at $7.00 each, or $2,100, plus $420 for packaging. She has $4,000 of savings set aside. She forecasts 40 orders in month one, 60 in month two, and 80 in month three, each with $13.15 of contribution margin before inventory (since the inventory was prepaid, she adds back COGS of $7.00 to get $20.15 of cash per order in those months). Fixed costs are $154 a month.

Month 1: 4,000 − 2,520 + (40 × 20.15) − 154 = $2,132.
Month 2: 2,132 + (60 × 20.15) − 154 = $3,187.
Month 3: 3,187 + (80 × 20.15) − 154 = $4,645.

Her lowest balance is $2,132, at the end of month one, and she has sold 180 of 300 candles by month three. If month-one sales were only 15, her balance would fall to about $1,628, still positive. The model tells her she can afford the order with a margin of safety, but that she should not also buy new equipment in the same month.
:::

# Chapter 4: Legal and Tax Foundations

:::objectives
- Identify the registrations commonly required to open an online store in the United States.
- Explain the difference between a sole proprietorship and a limited liability company in practical terms.
- Describe economic nexus and why South Dakota v. Wayfair matters to small online sellers.
- Build a compliance calendar for federal and state filings.
:::

Online selling feels placeless, but legal obligations attach to real places: where you live, where you store goods, and where your customers are. This chapter outlines the foundations most small U.S. sellers must address. It is a guide to the questions, not the answers; requirements differ by state, county, and city, and you should confirm them with the relevant agency or a licensed professional.

## Business structure

A sole proprietorship is the default if you sell on your own without forming an entity. It is simple, but the law does not separate your personal assets from the business's obligations. A limited liability company (LLC) is formed under state law and can, when properly maintained, separate business liabilities from personal assets. The SBA's guide to choosing a business structure compares these and other forms, including corporations and partnerships [7]. Many states charge annual fees for LLCs; California, for example, imposes an annual LLC tax. Ask a tax professional how the structure affects your federal and state taxes before deciding.

## Common registrations

1. Business name: If you sell under a name other than your own legal name, many counties require a fictitious business name (also called a DBA, "doing business as") filing.
2. Employer Identification Number (EIN): The IRS issues EINs free of charge through its website. LLCs with employees, and many banks opening business accounts, require one [8].
3. Seller's permit or sales tax registration: Most states that impose a sales tax require sellers of tangible goods to register before making taxable sales.
4. Local business license: Many cities require a general business license, even for home-based businesses, and some zoning rules limit home businesses.
5. Product-specific permits: Food, cosmetics, and certain other categories may require additional registrations or inspections.

## Sales tax and economic nexus

Sales tax is a state and local tax collected by the seller from the buyer and remitted to the state. Historically, under the Supreme Court's 1992 Quill decision, a state could require a remote seller to collect its sales tax only if the seller had a physical presence in the state. In South Dakota v. Wayfair (2018), the Supreme Court overruled that physical presence rule and upheld a South Dakota law requiring remote sellers to collect tax if they delivered more than $100,000 of goods or services into the state, or made 200 or more separate transactions there, in a year [9][10].

The Congressional Research Service notes that the decision replaced the physical presence test with an economic nexus test [10]. The U.S. Government Accountability Office reported that by June 2021, all 45 states with a statewide sales tax and the District of Columbia had adopted economic presence requirements for remote sellers, with varying thresholds and rules [11]. In practice, a small seller usually must collect tax in its home state from the first sale, and in other states once its sales into those states cross each state's threshold.

:::warning Marketplaces and thresholds vary
Many states require large online marketplaces to collect and remit tax on sales they facilitate, which can change your obligations for sales made through those channels. Thresholds, what counts toward them, and filing frequencies differ by state and change over time. Track your sales by destination state from the first day and review them quarterly against current state guidance.
:::

## Income tax and recordkeeping

Profit from your store is generally taxable income. Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs typically report business income on Schedule C of their federal return, and self-employment tax may apply. The IRS's small business resources explain estimated quarterly payments, which are often required when you expect to owe tax that is not withheld [8]. Good records are not a formality; they are what allow you to deduct legitimate expenses such as materials, packaging, postage, and software. Use a separate business bank account and keep receipts.

:::example Maya's compliance setup (illustrative)
Maya lives in California and sells under the name "Quiet Flame Candles." She files a fictitious business name statement with her county, obtains a free EIN from the IRS, and registers for a seller's permit with the California Department of Tax and Fee Administration (CDTFA). She checks her city's home-occupation rules and buys a business license for $95. She opens a business checking account and connects it to bookkeeping software.

Her store platform calculates sales tax at checkout for California orders. She builds a spreadsheet tab that totals sales and transactions by destination state each month. After six months, her largest out-of-state market is Oregon, which has no statewide sales tax, and no state is near its threshold. She sets a quarterly reminder to recheck.
:::

## Consumer protection basics

Federal consumer protection law, enforced by the Federal Trade Commission, applies to online sellers of every size. The core principle is that advertising must be truthful, not misleading, and supported by evidence. Later chapters cover the FTC rules most relevant to stores: the shipping rule (Chapter 8), endorsements and reviews (Chapter 9), and commercial email (Chapter 9). Your state may have additional consumer protection and privacy laws, especially regarding automatic renewals and the collection of personal data.

| Obligation | Typical agency | When | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fictitious business name | County clerk | Before trading under a brand name | Renew per county rules |
| EIN | IRS | Before opening business bank account | Once |
| Seller's permit / sales tax registration | State tax agency | Before first taxable sale | Returns monthly, quarterly, or yearly |
| Local business license | City or county | Before operating | Usually annual |
| Estimated income tax | IRS and state | When tax not withheld is expected | Quarterly |
| Remote state registration | Other state tax agencies | When economic threshold is crossed | Per state |

:::summary
Online stores carry obligations tied to real places. Choose a structure deliberately, register your name, obtain an EIN, register for sales tax where you are located, and check local licensing. Since South Dakota v. Wayfair, states may require remote sellers to collect sales tax once their sales into the state exceed an economic threshold; all states with a sales tax have adopted such rules, with varying details. Keep separate accounts and good records, and calendar every filing.

# Chapter 5: Choosing and Configuring the Platform

- Calculate the true monthly cost of a platform, including transaction fees and apps.
- Configure the essential settings that most new stores overlook.
:::

The platform question attracts more attention than it deserves. Every major platform can process orders; the differences lie in cost structure, control over the customer relationship, and the amount of technical work you take on. Choosing well is mostly a matter of writing down what you need before you look at what vendors are selling.

## Three models

A hosted store builder (sometimes called software as a service, or SaaS) rents you a complete store for a monthly fee. The vendor handles hosting, security patches, and checkout. You control the brand and customer list, but you work within the vendor's features and pay for add-on apps.

A marketplace lists your products alongside those of many other sellers. It brings existing shoppers, which can produce early sales, but it typically charges a commission per sale, controls the customer relationship, and sets rules that can change.

Self-hosted software runs on a server you rent or manage. It offers the greatest flexibility and the greatest responsibility: you or a contractor must handle updates, security, backups, and payment compliance.

| Model | Upfront effort | Ongoing cost pattern | Customer data | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hosted store builder | Low | Monthly plan + payment fees + apps | You own the list | Most first stores |
| Marketplace | Very low | Commission per sale + listing fees | Marketplace controls | Testing demand, reaching existing shoppers |
| Self-hosted | High | Hosting + developer time + plugins | You own everything | Technical founders, unusual requirements |

Many small sellers use more than one model: a hosted store for their own brand and a marketplace as a secondary channel. If you do, plan inventory carefully so that the same item is not sold twice.

## Write criteria first

Before opening any vendor's website, list your requirements in three tiers. Must-haves are non-negotiable, such as accepting cards and digital wallets, calculating sales tax for your state, and printing discounted shipping labels. Should-haves materially improve operations, such as abandoned-checkout email and inventory alerts. Nice-to-haves are pleasant but not decisive. Then score each candidate platform on the must-haves only. A platform that fails one must-have is eliminated, however attractive its templates.

## The true monthly cost

Advertised plan prices understate the cost of running a store. The true monthly cost has four components: the plan fee, payment processing, any additional platform transaction fee if you use an outside payment processor, and the subscription apps you add for reviews, email, shipping, and so on. App subscriptions are the most commonly underestimated line.

:::example Comparing two platforms for Maya (illustrative)
Maya expects $3,000 of monthly sales across about 95 orders. Platform A costs $39 a month, charges 2.9 percent plus $0.30 per card transaction, and she would add two apps totaling $25. Platform B costs $29 a month, charges 2.9 percent plus $0.30, adds a 2 percent transaction fee because she would use an outside processor, and she would need three apps totaling $35.

Platform A: 39 + (0.029 × 3,000) + (0.30 × 95) + 25 = 39 + 87 + 28.50 + 25 = $179.50.
Platform B: 29 + 87 + 28.50 + (0.02 × 3,000) + 35 = 29 + 87 + 28.50 + 60 + 35 = $239.50.

The platform with the cheaper headline price costs Maya $60 more each month at her expected volume. These fee levels are illustrative; check each vendor's current published pricing before deciding.
:::

## Essential configuration

Most store setup guides cover themes and logos. The settings that cause real problems are less glamorous.

1. Legal pages: Publish a shipping policy, returns and refunds policy, privacy policy, and terms of service, and link them in the footer and at checkout. Appendix C provides outlines.
2. Tax settings: Enable tax collection for every jurisdiction where you are registered, and verify a test order's tax amount against the state's published rate.
3. Shipping profiles: Enter real package weights and dimensions so that calculated rates are accurate.
4. Notifications: Customize order confirmation, shipping confirmation, and delivery emails with your contact details and expected timelines.
5. Staff accounts and two-factor authentication: Give each helper a separate login with only the permissions they need, and require two-factor authentication.
6. Test orders: Place at least three complete test orders, including one refund, before launch.

:::case Snowdevil's fifteen-minute product upload
Shopify's account of its origins describes how the Snowdevil founders found existing store software unable to provide the experience they wanted and built their own [1]. Profiles of Lütke report his frustration with interfaces that required many clicks to add a single product [2]. Few new sellers should build their own platform. The relevant lesson is that the operational friction of a platform, how long it takes to add a product, fulfill an order, or issue a refund, matters as much as its appearance. During your trial period, time yourself doing each routine task.

# Chapter 6: Product Pages, Photography, and Honest Claims

:::objectives
- Structure a product page so that it answers the questions buyers actually ask.
- Plan product photography that reduces returns and builds trust.
- Apply the FTC's requirement that claims be truthful and substantiated.
- Write product descriptions that persuade with specifics rather than superlatives.
:::

A product page does the work of a salesperson who cannot see the customer. It must anticipate questions, remove doubt, and set accurate expectations. A page that oversells may produce a sale and a return; a page that is vague produces neither. The goal is a page that a satisfied customer would say described the product exactly.

## Anatomy of an effective product page

Research on how people read web pages has long found that users scan rather than read word by word, focusing on headings, the first words of lines, and bulleted lists. Design for scanning.

1. Title: What it is, in plain words, with the key variant (size, color, scent).
2. Price and shipping signal: The price, and a short line about shipping cost or threshold and dispatch time.
3. Primary image: The product alone, well lit, on a plain background.
4. Benefit summary: Two or three short sentences on what the product does for the buyer.
5. Specifications: Size, weight, materials, ingredients, care instructions, and anything a careful buyer would want before purchasing.
6. Supporting images: Scale (the product in a hand or room), detail, packaging, and the product in use.
7. Policies: A short note on returns, linking to the full policy.
8. Reviews: Genuine reviews, displayed in full, including critical ones.

## Photography that reduces returns

Returns frequently occur because the product differed from the buyer's expectation. Photography is your main tool for aligning expectation with reality. Show scale explicitly; buyers routinely misjudge size from isolated product shots. Show true color under natural or daylight-balanced light, and avoid heavy filters. Show the product from every side a buyer would see in use. A smartphone, a window with indirect light, and a sheet of white poster board are sufficient for many small products.

## Truthful and substantiated claims

The FTC Act prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and the FTC's advertising guidance requires that advertising be truthful, not misleading, and, when appropriate, backed by scientific evidence [12]. "Substantiation" means that you have a reasonable basis for a claim before you make it. A claim that a candle burns for 50 hours should be based on your own burn tests under stated conditions. A claim that a product is "non-toxic," "eco-friendly," or "all natural" invites scrutiny; the FTC's Green Guides address environmental marketing claims specifically and caution against broad, unqualified environmental claims [13].

:::warning Health and safety claims
Do not claim that a product treats, cures, or prevents any condition unless you have competent and reliable scientific evidence and have checked whether the product would then be regulated as a drug or device. Claims about children's safety, allergies, and air quality deserve particular care.
:::

## Writing with specifics

Superlatives such as "best," "amazing," and "premium" carry little information and invite doubt. Specific, verifiable details persuade more effectively because they let the buyer picture the product. Compare "luxurious, long-lasting candle" with "8 oz soy wax in a reusable amber glass jar; about 45 hours of burn time in our tests when trimmed to 1/4 inch."

:::example Rewriting Maya's description (illustrative)
Original: "Our amazing all-natural candles are the best you'll ever buy. Totally non-toxic and eco-friendly, they'll transform your home!"

Problems: unsubstantiated superlative ("best"), unqualified environmental and safety claims ("all-natural," "non-toxic," "eco-friendly"), and an unmeasurable promise ("transform your home").

Revision: "An unscented candle for people who want the light without the fragrance. Hand-poured in small batches from soy wax with a cotton wick, in an 8 oz amber glass jar you can reuse. In our burn tests, each candle burned for about 45 hours when the wick was trimmed to 1/4 inch before each use. Burn for no more than 4 hours at a time, and never leave a burning candle unattended."

The revision is longer, but every sentence either informs or reassures, and every claim is one Maya can support.
:::

:::case Spanx and showing the product
Forbes reported that Sara Blakely launched Spanx with $5,000 of savings and, in an early pitch to a Neiman Marcus buyer, demonstrated the product by showing her own cream trousers with and without it [14]. Fortune's account of the same meeting describes the buyer understanding the product only after seeing that demonstration [15]. The principle translates directly to a product page: when a product's benefit is visual, show the before and after rather than describing it. Spanx's subsequent success reflects many factors and is not a typical outcome.
:::

| Weak phrasing | Problem | Stronger phrasing |
|---|---|---|
| "Best quality" | Unsubstantiated superlative | "Double-stitched seams; 12 oz cotton canvas" |
| "Eco-friendly" | Broad, unqualified environmental claim | "Shipped in a recyclable cardboard box; no plastic filler" |
| "Long-lasting" | Vague | "About 45 hours of burn time in our tests" |
| "Fits everyone" | Likely untrue | "Fits wrists 6 to 8 inches; see size chart" |
| "Ships fast" | Undefined | "Ships within 2 business days of your order" |

:::checklist Product page review
- The title states what the product is and its key variant.
- Price, shipping cost or threshold, and dispatch time are visible near the price.
- At least one image shows scale, and colors are true to life.
- Specifications include size, weight, materials, and care.
- Every factual claim is one I can substantiate today.
- There are no broad environmental or health claims without qualification and evidence.
- The returns policy is summarized and linked.
- Reviews are genuine and displayed without selective deletion.
:::

:::summary
An effective product page anticipates questions, supports scanning, and sets accurate expectations. Photography should show scale, true color, and every relevant angle, because mismatched expectations drive returns. The FTC requires that claims be truthful and substantiated; environmental and health claims carry particular risk. Specific, verifiable details persuade more effectively than superlatives.
:::

# Chapter 7: Payments, Fraud, and Data Security

:::objectives
- Explain how a card payment moves from customer to seller and where fees arise.
- Describe chargebacks and the steps that reduce them.
- Identify the basic data security obligations of a small online store, including PCI DSS.
- Build a written fraud review routine for higher-risk orders.
:::

Payment processing is invisible when it works and painful when it does not. For a small store, the risks fall into three groups: fees that erode margin, fraud and chargebacks that reverse revenue, and security failures that expose customer data. None requires deep technical knowledge to manage, but each requires a deliberate routine.

## How a card payment works

When a customer pays by card, your payment processor sends the transaction through the card network to the bank that issued the customer's card. The issuing bank approves or declines it. Funds then settle to your account, usually within a few business days, minus fees. The fees compensate the issuing bank, the card network, and the processor. Many hosted platforms bundle these into a single advertised rate, such as a percentage plus a fixed fee per transaction.

## Chargebacks

A chargeback occurs when a cardholder disputes a charge with their bank and the bank reverses it. Common reasons are fraud (the real cardholder did not make the purchase), item not received, and item not as described. When a chargeback occurs, you typically lose the sale amount and pay a dispute fee, and you may also lose the merchandise. You can contest a chargeback by submitting evidence, such as tracking showing delivery to the billing address, but outcomes are uncertain. Persistently high chargeback rates can lead processors to hold funds or close accounts.

The best defenses are operational: accurate product pages, prompt shipping with tracking, a clear returns process, and a recognizable business name on the customer's card statement. Many disputes are filed by customers who do not recognize a charge or could not reach the seller.

## Recognizing higher-risk orders

Most orders are legitimate. A small number of signals, especially in combination, warrant a closer look before shipping.

| Signal | Why it matters | Reasonable response |
|---|---|---|
| Billing and shipping addresses far apart | Common in fraud, also in gifts | Check for gift note; email customer if combined with other signals |
| Several failed payment attempts before success | Card testing | Hold and review |
| Unusually large order from a new customer | Higher loss if fraudulent | Verify by email or phone |
| Expedited shipping on a high-value first order | Fraudsters want goods before detection | Review before dispatch |
| Email address that does not resemble the name | Weak signal alone | Consider with others |
| Processor fraud score marked high risk | Aggregated signals | Follow your written routine |

:::example Maya's fraud routine (illustrative)
Maya's processor flags an order for six candles ($168 plus expedited shipping) with billing in one state and shipping to a freight-forwarding address in another; there had been three declined attempts with different cards in the previous ten minutes. Her written routine says that any order with two or more signals is held and the customer is emailed asking them to confirm the order details. No reply arrives within 48 hours, so she cancels and refunds the order. Had she shipped it, a likely chargeback would have cost her the $168 sale, the product cost, the postage, and a dispute fee.
:::

## Data security and PCI DSS

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) is a set of security requirements developed by the PCI Security Standards Council for organizations that store, process, or transmit cardholder data [16]. Merchants who accept cards are generally expected to comply, and the level of effort depends on how payments are handled. A small store that uses a hosted checkout or embedded payment fields from a compliant processor, so that card numbers never touch its own systems, typically has a much smaller compliance burden than one that collects card data directly. Never ask customers to email card numbers, and never write them down.

The FTC's guide Protecting Personal Information: A Guide for Business recommends five principles: take stock of the information you hold, scale down to what you need, lock it, pitch what you no longer need, and plan ahead for incidents [17]. For a small store, that means collecting only the customer data needed to fulfill and support orders, protecting accounts with strong unique passwords and two-factor authentication, limiting staff access, and knowing in advance whom you would call if an account were compromised.

:::case The FTC's five principles in a small business
The FTC guide is written for businesses of all sizes and frames security as a sequence of habits rather than a technology purchase [17]. A store applying "scale down" might stop collecting customers' dates of birth if it does not need them; applying "lock it" might mean turning on two-factor authentication for the store, email, and bank accounts; applying "plan ahead" might mean writing a one-page incident plan listing the platform's support contact, the processor's fraud line, and the steps to reset passwords.
:::

:::checklist Payments and security
- My checkout uses a hosted or embedded payment form so card numbers never touch my systems.
- My business name on card statements is recognizable to customers.
- Every order ships with tracking, and I keep proof of delivery.
- I have a written rule for which orders are held for review.
- Two-factor authentication is on for my store, email, payment, and bank accounts.
- Each helper has a separate login with limited permissions.
- I collect only the customer data I need.
- I have a one-page incident plan with key contacts.
:::

:::summary
Card payments pass through processors, networks, and issuing banks, each of which earns part of the fee. Chargebacks reverse revenue and add fees; accurate pages, tracked shipping, clear policies, and a recognizable statement name prevent many of them. A written review routine for orders with multiple risk signals limits fraud losses. Use a compliant hosted or embedded checkout to minimize PCI DSS scope, and apply the FTC's five data security principles.
:::

# Chapter 8: Fulfillment, Shipping, and Returns

:::objectives
- Design a fulfillment workflow from order receipt to delivery.
- Apply the FTC Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule to shipping promises and delays.
- Compare carriers and packaging choices on cost and damage risk.
- Write a returns policy that is fair, clear, and economically sustainable.
:::

Fulfillment is where the promise of the product page meets physical reality. A customer who waits too long or receives a damaged parcel rarely returns, and may dispute the charge. This chapter covers the workflow, the federal rule on shipping times, packaging and carrier choices, and returns.

## The fulfillment workflow

A reliable workflow has six stages, each with a clear owner and a daily rhythm.

1. Receive: Orders arrive and are checked for risk signals (Chapter 7).
2. Pick: The correct items are pulled from stock using a packing slip.
3. Pack: Items are protected, the slip is included, and the parcel is weighed.
4. Label: A label is purchased at the actual weight and dimensions.
5. Dispatch: Parcels are handed to the carrier, and tracking is sent to the customer.
6. Follow up: Delivery is confirmed, and exceptions (delays, damage) are handled.

Set a daily cutoff time, such as orders received by 1 p.m. ship the same day, and a standard dispatch window that you can meet even on difficult weeks.

## The FTC shipping rule

The FTC's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule applies to most goods ordered by mail, phone, or online [18]. Under the Rule, when you advertise merchandise you must have a reasonable basis for stating or implying that you can ship within a certain time; if you make no shipping statement, you must have a reasonable basis for believing you can ship within 30 days [18]. If you later learn that you cannot ship on time, you must seek the customer's consent to the delay or, if you cannot obtain it, promptly refund the money paid for the unshipped merchandise [18]. The FTC's guidance explains that delay notices must give a revised shipping date or state that you do not know when you can ship, and must tell the customer of the right to cancel and receive a full and prompt refund [18][19].

The federal regulation, 16 CFR Part 435, contains the full text, including the 30-day period and the 50-day period that applies when a buyer applies for credit from the seller at the time of ordering [20].

:::warning Pre-orders and made-to-order goods
If you sell pre-orders or made-to-order items, state a realistic shipping time clearly and conspicuously on the product page and at checkout, and have a basis for it. If production slips, send a delay notice promptly. Do not wait until the promised date has passed.
:::

## Packaging and carriers

Packaging has two jobs: protect the product and minimize the billable weight and size. Carriers commonly price by the greater of actual weight and dimensional weight, a calculation based on box volume. An oversized box filled with padding can cost more to ship than a snug one with less padding. Test your packaging by shipping a few parcels to friends in distant states and asking them to photograph the contents on arrival.

| Decision | Lower cost option | Higher protection option | Guidance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Box size | Snug fit | Larger box, more padding | Use the smallest box that passes a drop test |
| Void fill | Paper | Molded inserts | Molded inserts for fragile, repeat items |
| Service level | Ground | Expedited | Offer expedited as a paid option |
| Insurance | Self-insure small items | Carrier insurance | Insure high-value parcels |
| Signature | None | Signature required | Use for high-value orders |

:::example Maya's packaging test (illustrative)
Maya's initial box measured 8 × 8 × 8 inches, and her quotes were based on its dimensional weight. She tested a 6 × 6 × 6 inch box with a fitted cardboard insert. She shipped six test parcels to friends in four states; one jar cracked in the original box and none in the new one. The smaller box reduced her average ground postage from $9.20 to $8.10 and her packaging cost from $1.40 to $1.25. Recalculating her one-order model raised contribution margin from $13.15 to about $14.40 per order, a gain of roughly $1.25 on every sale, with less breakage.
:::

## Returns: fair and sustainable

A returns policy balances customer trust against cost. Federal law does not generally require a merchant to accept returns of non-defective goods, but some states have disclosure rules, and your policy must be honored as written. A clear policy reduces disputes because customers know what to expect.

A complete returns policy answers seven questions: Which items can be returned? Within how many days? In what condition? Who pays return shipping? How does the customer start a return? When and how is the refund issued? What happens with damaged or incorrect items? Appendix C provides a template.

Track the reason for every return. If one product has a high return rate for "not as described," the problem is the product page, not the customer.

:::case Gymshark's early capacity strain
Profiles of Gymshark describe how the brand's online demand surged after the 2013 BodyPower expo, with reports of the website struggling under the load [4][5]. Rapid demand growth tests every stage of fulfillment, not only the website. A small seller planning a promotion should ask in advance: if orders triple this week, can I still ship within my stated window, and if not, what will I say to customers? The FTC Rule's delay-notice requirements make that a legal question as well as a service question.
:::

# Chapter 9: Launch Marketing That Respects the Rules

:::objectives
- Build a launch plan around owned, earned, and paid channels.
- Grow an email list and send commercial email that complies with the CAN-SPAM Act.
- Work with creators and reviewers in line with the FTC's Endorsement Guides.
- Set a test budget for paid advertising and judge results by contribution margin, not clicks.
:::

Marketing for a new store has one job: bring the right people to a product page that can convert them. The temptation at launch is to try every channel at once. A better approach is to choose a small number of channels, run them consistently, and measure what each order cost to acquire.

## Owned, earned, and paid channels

Owned channels are those you control: your email list, your website, and your social media accounts. They are cheap to use but slow to build. Earned channels are attention others give you: press coverage, word of mouth, and organic posts by customers. They are valuable and unpredictable. Paid channels are advertising: search ads, social ads, and paid creator partnerships. They are fast and scalable but cost money on every click.

A sensible launch sequence for a small store is to build owned channels first (a waiting list, a few months of social posts), seek earned attention at launch (local press, market appearances, sending samples to relevant reviewers), and use paid channels only after you know your contribution margin and conversion rate.

## Email and the CAN-SPAM Act

Email remains one of the most cost-effective channels for small stores because the list belongs to you. Grow it honestly: a sign-up form on your site, a clear description of what subscribers will receive, and, if you offer an incentive, a description of the incentive's terms.

The CAN-SPAM Act sets rules for commercial email. The FTC's compliance guide summarizes the main requirements: do not use false or misleading header information; do not use deceptive subject lines; identify the message as an advertisement where required; tell recipients where you are located with a valid physical postal address; tell recipients how to opt out of future email; honor opt-out requests promptly (within 10 business days); and monitor what others do on your behalf [21]. Each separate email in violation can carry substantial civil penalties.

## Creators, reviews, and the Endorsement Guides

Creator partnerships, from free product to paid posts, can introduce a store to a relevant audience. The FTC's Endorsement Guides require that when there is a material connection between an endorser and the brand—payment, free product, a family relationship, or a business relationship—that connection be clearly and conspicuously disclosed [22]. The FTC's plain-language guidance for businesses explains that disclosures should be hard to miss and placed with the endorsement itself, not buried in a profile or behind a "more" link [22]. Endorsements must reflect the endorser's honest opinion and cannot make claims the brand itself could not substantiate.

The FTC has also issued a rule addressing fake reviews and testimonials, which prohibits, among other things, buying or selling fake reviews and suppressing negative reviews [23]. Never write reviews of your own products, ask friends to post reviews without disclosure, or offer incentives conditioned on positive reviews.

:::case Gymshark and creator partnerships
Forbes reports that Ben Francis sent products to fitness YouTubers he admired and later paid fitness creators to wear Gymshark in their content, a strategy the magazine credits with much of the brand's growth [4]. The relevant lesson for a small seller is the fit between creator and product: Francis chose people whose audiences were exactly his buyers. Today, any such partnership in the United States must follow the FTC's disclosure requirements, and creators' posts should carry clear disclosures such as "Ad" or "Paid partnership" [22].
:::

## Paid advertising: a disciplined test

Treat paid advertising as an experiment with a fixed budget and a predetermined decision rule. The key figure is the maximum you can spend to acquire an order and still earn a profit, which is your contribution margin per order before advertising. If Maya's contribution margin is $14.40, any advertising that costs more than $14.40 per order loses money on the first purchase. Whether some loss on the first purchase is acceptable depends on how often customers buy again, which you will not know reliably until you have several months of data.

:::example Maya's first ad test (illustrative)
Maya sets a test budget of $300 over two weeks for social ads directing people to her unscented candle page. Her decision rule, written in advance: continue if cost per order is below $12; stop if above $18; adjust creative and retest if between.

Results: 4,100 people reached, 162 clicks, 5 orders. Cost per click: 300 ÷ 162 ≈ $1.85. Conversion rate: 5 ÷ 162 ≈ 3.1 percent. Cost per order: 300 ÷ 5 = $60.

At $60 per order against $14.40 of contribution, every ad-driven order loses about $45.60. Maya stops the campaign. She notes that her conversion rate was reasonable; the problem was cost per click relative to order value. She decides to focus on raising average order value with a two-candle bundle before testing paid ads again.
:::

| Metric | Formula | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Cost per click (CPC) | Ad spend ÷ clicks | Price of attention |
| Conversion rate | Orders ÷ sessions (or clicks) | Strength of the product page |
| Cost per order (CPO) | Ad spend ÷ orders | Compare with contribution margin |
| Average order value (AOV) | Revenue ÷ orders | Larger orders absorb acquisition cost |
| Return on ad spend (ROAS) | Revenue ÷ ad spend | Common but incomplete; ignores costs |

Return on ad spend is widely reported by ad platforms, but it measures revenue, not profit. A campaign with a ROAS of 3 (three dollars of revenue per dollar spent) can still lose money if contribution margin is below one-third of revenue.

:::checklist Launch marketing compliance
- My sign-up form states what subscribers will receive.
- Every marketing email includes my physical address and a working unsubscribe link.
- I honor opt-outs within 10 business days.
- Every creator or reviewer with a material connection discloses it clearly in the post itself.
- I never write, buy, or suppress reviews.
- My ad budget and decision rule were written before the test began.
- I judge ads by cost per order versus contribution margin, not by clicks or ROAS alone.
:::

:::summary
Build owned channels first, seek earned attention at launch, and use paid channels once you know your margins. Commercial email must follow CAN-SPAM, including a physical address and prompt opt-outs. Material connections with endorsers must be clearly disclosed, and fake or suppressed reviews are prohibited. Run paid advertising as a fixed-budget experiment and compare cost per order with contribution margin.
:::

- Design a simple post-purchase sequence that encourages honest reviews and repeat purchases.
:::

A small store cannot outspend large competitors, but it can out-serve them. Prompt, human, and fair service turns a first purchase into a relationship and reduces disputes. It also produces the most credible marketing a store can have: customers who recommend it without being asked.

## Service standards

Write down three standards and share them with anyone who helps you. Response time: for example, every message answered within one business day. Tone: courteous, specific, and free of blame. Resolution authority: what you or a helper may offer without further approval, such as a replacement or refund up to a certain amount for a damaged item.

## Templates for common questions

Most customer messages fall into a handful of categories: where is my order, can I change my order, the item arrived damaged, I want to return it, and product questions. Templates ensure consistent, accurate answers, but each response should be personalized with the customer's name, order details, and a specific next step.

:::example Damaged-item reply (illustrative)
"Hi Jordan, thank you for letting me know, and I'm sorry your candle arrived cracked. I've sent a replacement today by ground service; the tracking number is 9400 1000 0000 0000 0000 00, and it should arrive by Thursday. There's no need to send the damaged jar back. If you'd prefer a refund instead, just reply to this email and I'll process it the same day. — Maya, Quiet Flame Candles"

The reply apologizes once, states the action already taken, gives a date, removes the burden of returning the item, and offers an alternative. Maya's resolution authority permits replacement without return for damaged items under $40 because the cost of return postage exceeds the salvage value.
:::

## Why retention matters

Acquiring a new customer usually costs more than keeping an existing one, because a repeat customer does not need to be found again. In Maya's case, an advertising-acquired order cost $60 while a repeat order from her email list cost almost nothing beyond the email software. The economics of many small stores depend on whether customers come back.

A useful, simple measure is the repeat purchase rate: the share of customers in a given period who have bought more than once. Track it monthly. If it is low, ask why: was the product a one-time purchase by nature, was the experience disappointing, or did you simply never invite the customer back?

## The post-purchase sequence

A short, respectful sequence of emails after a purchase can improve the customer's experience and invite repeat business. A typical sequence has four messages.

1. Order confirmation: details, expected dispatch date, and a contact for questions.
2. Shipping confirmation: tracking and expected delivery.
3. Care or usage tips: sent shortly after expected delivery, helping the customer get the most from the product.
4. Review request and re-invitation: sent after the customer has had time to use the product, asking for an honest review and mentioning related products.

Review requests must not be conditioned on positive reviews, and any incentive for leaving a review must be offered regardless of the review's content and disclosed appropriately [22][23].

:::case Dan Martell's 1-3-1 rule for delegated service
Dan Martell, founder of SaaS Academy and author of Buy Back Your Time, describes a "1-3-1" rule for team members who bring him problems: identify one problem, propose three possible solutions, and recommend one [24]. For a store owner who eventually hires help with customer service, the rule is a practical way to delegate decisions without abandoning oversight. A helper who reports "a customer's parcel is lost; we can reship, refund, or file a carrier claim and wait; I recommend reshipping today because the customer has ordered three times" is solving the problem, not merely escalating it. Martell's results and recommendations are his own, not TTI's.
:::

# Chapter 11: Metrics and the First Ninety Days

:::objectives
- Select a small dashboard of metrics that reflect the health of a new store.
- Conduct a weekly review that leads to decisions, not just observations.
- Plan the first ninety days in three phases with specific goals.
- Recognize when to persist, adjust, or stop.
:::

New store owners can drown in data. Platforms report dozens of figures, most of which do not change a decision. This chapter narrows the field to a handful of metrics, sets out a weekly review routine, and offers a ninety-day plan built on the frameworks in earlier chapters.

## The essential dashboard

Seven numbers, reviewed weekly, describe the health of most small stores.

| Metric | Definition | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Sessions | Visits to your store | Whether marketing is reaching people |
| Conversion rate | Orders ÷ sessions | Whether pages and prices persuade |
| Average order value | Revenue ÷ orders | Whether bundles and thresholds work |
| Contribution margin per order | Revenue − all variable costs | Whether each order is profitable |
| Cost per order (paid) | Ad spend ÷ ad-driven orders | Whether paid acquisition pays |
| Return rate and reasons | Returns ÷ orders | Whether pages set accurate expectations |
| Cash balance | Bank balance | Whether you can keep going |

## The weekly review

Set aside one hour at the same time each week. Record the seven numbers, compare them with the prior week and with your plan, and answer three questions in writing: What changed? Why do I think it changed? What one thing will I do differently this week? A review that ends without a decision is a report, not a review.

## A ninety-day plan in three phases

Days 1 to 30, Stabilize: Ship every order within your stated window, answer every message within your standard, and fix any problem revealed by the first orders, such as packaging damage or confusing product information. Goal: zero late shipments and a written list of fixes completed.

Days 31 to 60, Learn: Run one controlled experiment at a time—a price test, a bundle, a new product photo, a small ad test—and measure its effect on conversion, order value, or contribution. Goal: three completed experiments with recorded results.

Days 61 to 90, Focus: Double down on the channel and products with the best contribution economics and drop what is not working. Goal: a written plan for the next quarter based on evidence from the first two months.

:::example Maya's day-60 review (illustrative)
After sixty days, Maya had 214 sessions per week, a 2.4 percent conversion rate, average order value of $41 after introducing a two-candle bundle, and contribution margin per order of $19.80. Her single paid test had failed (Chapter 9). Returns were 2 percent, all for breakage before her packaging change. Her cash balance was $3,960.

Decision: Focus on email and the bundle, which raised average order value from $33 to $41, and on local markets, which produced 40 percent of first-time customers. Retest paid ads only for the bundle, with a revised stop rule of $20 per order. Add a larger candle size, which customers had requested, as one controlled experiment.
:::

## Persist, adjust, or stop

Every founder eventually faces the question of whether to continue. Decide your criteria in advance. A reasonable framework: persist if contribution margin per order is positive and order volume is trending toward break-even; adjust if contribution is positive but volume is stagnant (change product, price, or channel); stop or fundamentally rethink if contribution margin remains negative after you have tested pricing and costs, or if the business threatens your essential savings. Stopping a business that does not work is not failure; it is the disciplined use of evidence.

:::case Buy Back Your Time and the founder's calendar
In Buy Back Your Time, Dan Martell recommends that founders audit how they spend their time, identify low-value tasks, and delegate or eliminate them so they can focus on work only they can do [24]. A store owner at day 90 can apply this directly: list every recurring task from the past two weeks, mark how much each would cost to delegate, and identify which tasks (such as packing orders) could be handed to a part-time helper when volume justifies it. This is a planning exercise, not a recommendation to hire before the numbers support it.
:::

:::checklist Ninety-day milestones
- Days 1–30: Every order shipped within the stated window.
- Days 1–30: Written list of fixes from early orders completed.
- Days 31–60: Three controlled experiments run and recorded.
- Days 31–60: Contribution margin per order recalculated with real costs.
- Days 61–90: Best channel and products identified with evidence.
- Days 61–90: Next-quarter plan written, including persist, adjust, or stop criteria.
:::

:::summary
Seven weekly metrics—sessions, conversion rate, average order value, contribution margin per order, cost per paid order, return rate, and cash balance—describe the health of a small store. A weekly review must end in a decision. Plan the first ninety days in three phases: stabilize, learn, and focus. Decide in advance what results would justify persisting, adjusting, or stopping.
:::

# Appendix C: Store Policy Templates

These outlines are starting points. Adapt them to your business and have them reviewed by a qualified professional; laws vary by state.

## Shipping policy outline

1. Processing time: "Orders placed by [time] [time zone] on business days ship within [number] business days."
2. Carriers and services: "We ship by [carrier] [service]. Expedited shipping is available at checkout."
3. Costs: "Shipping is calculated at checkout. Orders of $[amount] or more ship free within the contiguous United States."
4. Destinations: "We currently ship to [areas]. We do not ship to [exclusions]."
5. Delays: "If we cannot ship within the time stated, we will notify you with a revised date and your option to cancel for a full refund."
6. Lost or damaged parcels: "Contact us within [number] days of the expected delivery date at [email]."

## Returns and refunds policy outline

1. Eligibility: "Unused items in original condition may be returned within [number] days of delivery. [List exclusions, such as personalized items.]"
2. How to start: "Email [address] with your order number and reason for return."
3. Return shipping: "[We provide a prepaid label / The customer pays return shipping], except for damaged or incorrect items."
4. Inspection and refund: "Refunds are issued to the original payment method within [number] business days of receiving the return."
5. Damaged or incorrect items: "We will replace or refund at no cost. Please send a photo within [number] days of delivery."
6. Exchanges: "[Describe process or state that exchanges are handled as a return and new order.]"

## Privacy policy outline

1. What information you collect (name, address, email, order history) and how (checkout, sign-up forms, cookies).
2. Why you collect it (fulfilling orders, customer service, marketing with consent).
3. Who you share it with (payment processor, carriers, email service, platform).
4. How long you keep it and how you protect it.
5. Customer choices (unsubscribe, access, deletion where applicable by law).
6. Contact information and effective date.

:::warning State privacy laws
Several states, including California, have consumer privacy laws with specific notice requirements and thresholds. Check whether any apply to your business and update your policy accordingly.
:::

# Glossary

Average order value (AOV): Total revenue divided by number of orders in a period.

Break-even point: The volume of orders at which total contribution equals fixed costs.

CAN-SPAM Act: Federal law setting requirements for commercial email, enforced by the FTC.

Chargeback: Reversal of a card transaction initiated by the cardholder's bank after a dispute.

Contribution margin: Revenue minus all variable costs for an order or product.

Conversion rate: Orders divided by sessions or visitors.

Cost of goods sold (COGS): The direct cost of making or buying the product sold.

Cost per order (CPO): Advertising spend divided by orders attributed to that spend.

Dimensional weight: A carrier pricing method based on package volume rather than actual weight.

Economic nexus: A connection to a state, based on sales volume or transactions, sufficient to require sales tax collection.

Employer Identification Number (EIN): A federal tax identification number issued by the IRS.

Endorsement Guides: FTC guidance on the use of endorsements and testimonials in advertising.

Fixed costs: Costs that do not vary with order volume in the short term.

Gross margin: Revenue minus COGS.

Mail Order Rule: The FTC's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule, 16 CFR Part 435.

Material connection: A relationship between endorser and seller that might affect the weight a consumer gives the endorsement.

PCI DSS: The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard.

Repeat purchase rate: Share of customers in a period who have purchased more than once.

Return on ad spend (ROAS): Revenue divided by advertising spend.

Substantiation: A reasonable basis, existing before a claim is made, that supports an advertising claim.

Variable costs: Costs incurred because an order occurs.
