# How to Use This Book

Many small-business owners now hear that they can "run a company with AI agents." The reality is more modest and more useful. AI tools can draft, sort, summarize, and carry out routine steps, but they work best inside a clear structure: an owner who decides, a shared set of records that every tool reads and writes, narrowly defined jobs, and firm limits on what software may do without a person's approval.

This short guide presents that structure as a map. It has four layers: the human owner, a daily one-page brief, a shared "brain" of rules and records, and a small number of single-job departments that work inside the tools you already use. The guide explains each layer, shows how they fit together, and offers a simple plan for starting with one department rather than seven.

## Who this book is for

The guide is written for owners and managers of small businesses who are curious about AI assistance but want a sensible, low-risk way to organize it. It assumes no programming knowledge. It does not recommend specific vendors and is not a technical manual.

## What you will be able to do

After reading this guide, you should be able to describe how small businesses are actually using AI today; explain the difference between a predictable workflow and an autonomous agent; list the decisions that must remain with a human owner; design a shared record that keeps AI work visible; define single-job departments with clear "never" rules; use a daily one-page brief to supervise the work; and choose a first department to pilot.

## How each chapter works

Each chapter opens with learning objectives and closes with a summary and review questions. Chapters include a short explanation, a step-by-step framework, a table, a worked example labeled illustrative, a case study from a real public source with a numbered citation, and a checklist or worksheet. Case findings are their own, not Total Transformation Inc.'s.

This guide is for education only. It is not legal, financial, or technical advice and makes no promise of savings, revenue, or any other result. AI tools can make mistakes, and your business remains responsible for what they say and do. Review vendor terms, privacy obligations, and applicable law before connecting AI tools to customer data or accounts. # Chapter 1: Where Small Businesses Stand and Why Simple Wins

:::objectives
- Describe current AI adoption among U.S. businesses and how it differs by firm size.
- Distinguish predictable workflows from autonomous agents.
- Decide which kind of system fits a given task.
:::

Headlines can give the impression that every company has already handed its operations to AI. Government survey data tell a different story. Adoption is growing, but it is still a minority practice, especially among the smallest firms, and most adopters use AI in only a few functions.

## What the data show

The U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey reported that from December 2025 to May 2026, overall business AI use hovered between 17 and 20 percent, and that less than 20 percent of firms with four or fewer employees reported using AI [1]. AI use increased among firms with at least 20 employees over that period but did not change significantly among firms with fewer than 20 [1]. A Census working paper using the survey's AI supplement found that 57 percent of adopting firms used AI in three or fewer business functions, most commonly sales and marketing, strategy and business development, and IT, and that most users relied on AI to augment tasks rather than replace workers [2].

| Measure | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Overall business AI use, Dec 2025–May 2026 | About 17–20% | Census BTOS [1] |
| Firms with 250+ employees using AI | 37% | Census BTOS [1] |
| Firms with 4 or fewer employees using AI | Under 20% | Census BTOS [1] |
| Adopters using AI in 3 or fewer functions | 57% | Census working paper [2] |
| Users relying on AI only to augment tasks | 66% | Census working paper [2] |

## What this means for you

If you have not adopted AI, you are in the majority. If you have, you are probably using it narrowly. Either position is a reasonable starting point for a deliberate approach: choose a small number of well-defined jobs, keep a person in charge, and expand only when results justify it.

The word "agent" is used loosely. Some vendors use it for any AI feature; others mean software that plans and acts on its own for long periods. A clear distinction helps you choose wisely.

## Two kinds of systems

Anthropic, an AI developer, distinguishes workflows, "systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths," from agents, "systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage" [4]. Its guidance recommends "finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed," noting that this "might mean not building agentic systems at all" [4]. Workflows offer predictability and consistency for well-defined tasks; agents are better suited to tasks requiring flexibility [4].

| Feature | Workflow | Agent |
|---|---|---|
| Path | Fixed steps defined in advance | Decides its own steps |
| Predictability | High | Lower |
| Best for | Repetitive, well-defined tasks | Open-ended tasks needing judgment |
| Supervision needed | Spot checks | Closer review |
| Small-business fit | Usually the right starting point | Selective, with strong limits |

## Why simple wins

Most small-business tasks, such as answering common questions, sending a reminder, logging a lead, or drafting a follow-up, follow predictable steps. A workflow with one AI step and a human approval point is easier to test, cheaper to run, and easier to fix than an autonomous agent. McKinsey's 2025 survey found that although 62 percent of respondents said their organizations were at least experimenting with AI agents, no more than 10 percent reported scaling agents in any single business function [3].

## Four steps to assess your starting point and choose a design

1. List the AI tools anyone in your business already uses and the functions they touch.
2. Identify one repetitive task with a clear right answer and write it as a list of steps.
3. Mark judgment steps and routine steps; automate only routine steps and keep judgment with a person.
4. Add autonomy later only if the simple version works and the risk is low.

:::case Anthropic's guidance on building effective agents
In a December 2024 engineering article, Anthropic reported that across dozens of teams building agents, "the most successful implementations weren't using complex frameworks or specialized libraries" but "simple, composable patterns" [4]. It recommended starting simple and adding complexity only when it demonstrably improves results [4]. This is a developer's guidance, not TTI's, and the company notes that tooling has changed since. It supports beginning with predictable workflows.
:::

:::example Illustrative: an honest inventory and a simple workflow
Carla owns a six-person landscaping company. Her inventory shows that her office manager uses a free chat assistant to draft customer emails and that nobody uses AI anywhere else. Her most repetitive task is answering the same scheduling questions by phone and text. Those questions follow a pattern: a customer asks about availability, the system checks the calendar, and a reply is drafted. She sets up a workflow that drafts replies from the calendar for her office manager to approve. She does not let the system book or cancel jobs on its own.
:::

# Chapter 2: The Human Owner and the "Only You" List

:::objectives
- Identify decisions that must remain with a person.
- Explain why a business is responsible for what its AI tools say.
- Write an "only you" list for your business.
:::

At the top of the map is the human owner. AI tools can prepare, draft, and recommend, but certain decisions must stay with a person: they carry legal, financial, or ethical weight, or they require judgment that software cannot be trusted to exercise.

## Responsibility does not transfer

In Moffatt v. Air Canada, a British Columbia tribunal found the airline liable after its website chatbot gave a customer incorrect information about bereavement fares [5]. The airline argued the chatbot was responsible for its own actions; the tribunal rejected this, stating that the company "is responsible for all the information on its website," whether "from a static page or a chatbot" [5]. The amount was small, but the principle is clear: your business owns what your tools say.

## The "only you" list

| Category | Examples of decisions that stay with a person |
|---|---|
| Money | Payments, refunds, discounts, pricing changes, bank and payout details |
| Legal | Contracts, compliance filings, responses to legal notices |
| Identity and tax | Identity verification, tax filings, account ownership |
| People | Hiring, firing, discipline, pay |
| Customers | Complaints, exceptions to policy, anything promised in writing |
| Public claims | Advertising claims, testimonials, statements to media |

## Four steps to build your list

1. List every decision that involves money, law, identity, people, or public claims.
2. Mark which ones AI may prepare but not complete.
3. Mark which ones AI should not touch at all.
4. Post the list where every person and tool configuration can reference it.

:::case Moffatt v. Air Canada
In February 2024, British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal found Air Canada liable for negligent misrepresentation after its chatbot told a customer he could apply for a bereavement discount after travel, contrary to the airline's policy [5]. The tribunal rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate entity and awarded the customer about C$812 in damages, interest, and fees [5][8]. This is a single tribunal decision, not a TTI matter. It illustrates why customer-facing AI must stay within approved information and why exceptions belong with a person.
:::

:::example Illustrative: drawing the line
Carla's list states that only she approves refunds, discounts, and changes to bank details, and only her office manager may confirm exceptions to the cancellation policy. The scheduling workflow may draft replies about availability but must hand any request for a discount or refund to a person.
:::

# Chapter 3: The Shared Brain and the Morning Brief

:::objectives
- Explain why AI work needs a shared, visible record and describe its four parts.
- Describe what a daily one-page brief should contain.
- Use the brief and spot checks to supervise AI work efficiently.
:::

AI tools forget. Each session may start without knowledge of what happened yesterday, what rules apply, or what another tool already did. A shared brain is a simple, central record that every person and tool reads before acting and writes to afterward. It can be a set of plain documents, a project tracker, or a database.

## The four parts

| Part | What it contains | Who writes it |
|---|---|---|
| Master rules | Facts tools may state, red lines, tone, the "only you" list | Owner |
| Weekly orders | One to three priorities per department for the week | Owner |
| Activity log | Date, task, result, counts, problems, in append-only form | Each department |
| Only-you queue | Items waiting for a human decision | Each department; owner clears |

## Why this structure works

Master rules keep tools consistent and prevent invented facts. Weekly orders keep work focused. An append-only log, in which entries are added but never edited or deleted, creates an audit trail. The only-you queue ensures that decisions requiring a person are never lost. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, a voluntary federal framework, organizes AI risk management into four functions, govern, map, measure, and manage, and emphasizes documentation and accountability throughout [6]. A shared brain is a small-business version of that discipline.

## Four steps to set up a shared brain

1. Create one folder or tracker with four sections: rules, orders, log, queue.
2. Write the master rules, including approved facts and red lines.
3. Write this week's orders.
4. Require every tool and person to read rules and orders before acting and to log afterward.

## The morning one-page brief

Supervision is the price of delegation. The owner cannot review every action, but should review a short daily summary. The morning brief is a single page, drafted from the activity log and only-you queue, that tells the owner what happened, what needs a decision, and what went wrong.

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Yesterday's activity | Counts by department: inquiries handled, replies drafted, invoices sent |
| Decisions needed | Items in the only-you queue, each with a recommended action |
| Problems | Errors, complaints, failed steps, anything unusual |
| This week's priorities | Progress on weekly orders |
| Spot checks | Two or three randomly selected items for the owner to review in full |

## Why spot checks matter

Summaries can hide errors. Reviewing a few randomly selected actions in full each day, such as a drafted reply or a logged lead, catches problems that counts conceal. If spot checks reveal errors, tighten the rules or reduce the department's permitted actions.

:::case NIST AI Risk Management Framework
NIST released its AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023 for voluntary use, to help organizations incorporate trustworthiness into the design, use, and evaluation of AI systems, and published a generative AI profile in July 2024 [6]. NIST notes that the framework is being revised [6]. It is written for organizations of all sizes, and its emphasis on governance and documentation is not specific to TTI. The shared brain applies the same principles at small scale.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a shared brain and a morning brief
Carla creates a shared workbook with four tabs. The rules tab lists service areas, prices, hours, and red lines; the orders tab lists this week's priority, replying to scheduling questions within an hour; the log tab records each drafted reply; and the queue tab collects refund and discount requests. One morning her brief shows 14 replies sent after approval, 2 discount requests in the queue, and 1 problem: a reply quoted last year's spring-cleanup price. She decides both requests, updates the price in the master rules, and reviews two random replies in full.
:::

:::checklist Shared brain and daily brief
- Master rules written, with approved facts and red lines.
- Weekly orders written.
- Append-only activity log and only-you queue created.
- Every tool and person reads before acting and logs afterward.
- Brief read each morning; queue cleared; spot checks reviewed; rules updated if errors found.
:::

:::summary
A shared brain is a central record of rules, orders, activity, and pending decisions that compensates for AI tools' lack of memory and creates an audit trail, applying principles from frameworks such as NIST's at small scale. A morning one-page brief drawn from it lets the owner supervise in minutes, and random spot checks catch errors that counts conceal.
:::

# Chapter 4: Single-Job Departments and Honest Limits

:::objectives
- Define departments with one clear job and explicit "never" rules.
- Protect customer data and secure accounts that AI tools can access.
- Keep public claims about your AI use accurate.
:::

The map divides AI-assisted work into departments, each with a narrow job, a few permitted actions, and explicit "never" rules. Narrow jobs are easier to test and supervise. Your existing tools, such as your inbox, phone system, calendar, customer list, and accounting software, remain the system of record; departments read from them and carry out limited, approved actions.

## Seven possible departments

| Department | Example permitted actions | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | Log new inquiries; draft first replies; book intro calls | Send spam or bulk unsolicited messages |
| Deals | Send approved quotes; send polite follow-ups | Invent discounts or terms |
| Marketing | Queue approved posts; reply to routine comments | Make unsupported claims or fake proof |
| Customer | Answer routine questions from approved facts; flag issues | Argue, promise refunds, or make exceptions |
| Onboarding | Confirm payment is recorded; send welcome steps | Start work on unverified orders |
| Office and books | Send approved invoices and reminders; reconcile records | Change bank or payout details |
| Diligence | Check that a contact's business is real; prepare a brief | State guesses as facts |

You do not need all seven. Most small businesses should begin with one.

## Four steps to define a department

1. Write the department's single job in one sentence.
2. List two to four permitted actions.
3. List the "never" rules, drawn from your "only you" list.
4. Name the tools it may read and the actions it may take in them.

## Privacy, security, and honest claims

Connecting AI tools to your inbox, customer list, or accounting software creates real risks: data may be shared with vendors, accounts may be misused, and marketing claims about AI may overstate what your business delivers.

| Risk | Safeguard |
|---|---|
| Customer data shared with vendors | Read vendor terms; share only what each job needs |
| Overbroad access | Give each tool the narrowest permissions possible |
| Account compromise | Use two-factor authentication and separate credentials |
| Inaccurate customer answers | Limit tools to approved facts; route exceptions to people |
| Overstated AI claims | Describe what your tools actually do; avoid guarantees |

If you tell customers that you use AI, describe it accurately, and avoid promising that AI will produce particular savings, revenue, or outcomes.

:::case FTC's Operation AI Comply
In September 2024, the FTC announced five enforcement actions under "Operation AI Comply," including a case against DoNotPay, which marketed "the world's first robot lawyer" and allegedly promised consumers could "generate perfectly valid legal documents in no time" without testing whether its output matched a human lawyer's, and cases against schemes claiming AI could help consumers make money through online storefronts [7]. FTC Chair Lina Khan stated that "there is no AI exemption from the laws on the books" [7]. These are federal allegations, not TTI matters. They show why marketing and customer departments need firm rules against unsupported claims.
:::

:::example Illustrative: one department, defined
Carla defines a Customer department: "Answer routine scheduling questions using approved facts." Permitted actions: read the calendar, draft replies, and log each exchange. Never: promise refunds or discounts, make exceptions, or argue. She also narrows its access: instead of her full email account, it can read only a dedicated scheduling inbox and the calendar, and cannot send without approval.
:::

Single-job departments keep AI work narrow, testable, and safe, with existing business tools as the system of record. # Chapter 5: Starting With One Department

:::objectives
- Choose a first department to pilot.
- Run a 30-day pilot with clear measures.
- Decide whether to expand, adjust, or stop.
:::

The full map has seven departments, but the right start is one. A single, well-defined pilot teaches you how the shared brain, the "only you" list, and the morning brief work in practice, at low risk.

## Choosing the first department

Choose a department whose job is repetitive, has clear right answers, and does not directly involve money or legal commitments. For many service businesses, answering routine questions or logging new inquiries is a good start.

## A 30-day pilot

| Week | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1 | Write rules, orders, and department definition; set up log and queue; run with human approval on every action |
| 2 | Continue with approval; track errors and time spent |
| 3 | Adjust rules; consider relaxing approval only for the most routine actions |
| 4 | Review results: accuracy, time saved, customer reactions, problems |

## Measures

Track accuracy, measured as the share of actions needing correction; time spent by people, including supervision; response times; customer complaints; and items routed to the queue. Compare with the period before the pilot.

## Four steps to decide

1. Review the measures at day 30.
2. Ask whether accuracy is acceptable and supervision time reasonable.
3. Decide to expand, adjust, or stop.
4. Add a second department only after the first runs smoothly.

:::case Census data on how firms use AI
The Census Bureau's working paper on AI diffusion found that among adopting firms, most used AI in a limited number of functions and tasks, and that 66 percent relied on AI solely to augment tasks, while AI-related employment decreases occurred in only 2 percent of firms [2]. These are federal research findings, not TTI results. They suggest that a narrow, augmenting pilot reflects how most firms actually adopt AI.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a pilot review
After 30 days, Carla's scheduling workflow handled most routine questions with few corrections after week two, and her office manager spent less time on the phone. Customers did not complain, and discount requests reliably reached the queue. She keeps the pilot running and begins defining an Office and Books department for invoice reminders, with the same approval-first approach.
:::

# Glossary

**Agent.** An AI system that decides its own steps and tool use to accomplish a task.

**Append-only log.** A record to which entries are added but never edited or deleted.

**Department.** In this map, a narrowly defined AI-assisted job with permitted actions and "never" rules.

**Master rules.** Approved facts, red lines, and standards that every tool must follow.

**Morning brief.** A one-page daily summary of AI activity, decisions needed, problems, and spot checks.

**Only-you list.** Decisions that must remain with a person.

**Only-you queue.** Items awaiting a human decision.

**Shared brain.** A central record of rules, orders, activity, and pending decisions.

**System of record.** The existing business tool that holds authoritative data, such as a calendar or accounting system.

**Workflow.** An AI-assisted process that follows predefined steps.
