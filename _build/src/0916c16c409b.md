# How to Use This Book

This book is for people who want their business to reflect what they believe matters: owners of small service firms, makers, consultants, coaches, and people in the early stages of a venture. Some readers hold a religious faith that shapes their work. Others hold strong convictions that are not religious. Many are somewhere in between. The book is written for all of them. It does not ask you to adopt any belief, and it does not suggest that any belief makes a business more deserving of success.

The central idea is simple. A business reveals its values through ordinary decisions: how it describes an offer, what it charges, how it treats the people who work for it, how it handles personal information, how it repairs a mistake, and when it chooses to rest instead of grow. A mission statement can describe intentions, but customers and workers experience the decisions. This book offers a method for turning convictions into decisions that other people can see, test, and hold you to.

Each chapter follows the same structure. It begins with learning objectives and key definitions, presents a numbered framework, and works through an illustrative example with real arithmetic. It then presents a real, documented case study of a company or court decision, drawn from public sources and cited in the References. A table summarizes key distinctions, a checklist or worksheet turns the chapter into action, and a summary and review questions help you check your understanding.

The illustrative examples follow one fictional business: Clear Day Studio, a home organization service in Columbus, Ohio, owned by Naomi Brooks. Naomi works with one part-time assistant and charges by the session. She is a person of faith, but her customers and her assistant hold a range of beliefs, and part of her work in this book is to make her standards clear to all of them. Clear Day Studio and everyone in it are invented for teaching.

The case studies are real. They include large companies, such as Johnson & Johnson, Patagonia, and Chick-fil-A, and smaller ones, such as Basecamp. They were chosen because they illustrate a principle clearly and because the facts are documented in public sources, not because the companies are models in every respect. Several of the cases involve mistakes, disputes, or criticism, and those are often the most useful.

:::callout Scope and limits
This book is for education only. It is not legal, tax, financial, employment, or religious advice. Laws differ by state and change over time; consult a qualified professional before acting on matters such as employment policies, privacy, or business structure. Case studies describe the documented experiences of the organizations named, drawn from public sources; their results belong to them, not to Total Transformation Inc. (TTI), and they do not predict what any reader will achieve. No spiritual, ethical, or business practice in this book guarantees income, customers, health, or favor.
:::

## How to read this book

Read Chapters 1 and 2 first; they set out the method of translating values into visible actions and separating purpose from the offer. Chapters 3 through 7 apply the method to specific areas: belief and inclusion, boundaries, pricing, privacy, and repair. Chapters 8 through 10 address reflection, rest, and long-term stewardship. The appendices provide worksheets you can copy and use, including a values-to-actions map, a boundary charter, a pricing worksheet, a repair protocol, a premortem guide, a capacity check, and a 30-day plan.

Complete the worksheets in writing. The method depends on making commitments concrete enough that someone else could check whether you kept them. That is uncomfortable at first, because it exposes gaps between what we say and what we do. That discomfort is the point.

## A note on tone

The book is written in plain language and avoids promises. You will not find claims that faith leads to wealth or that values guarantee loyalty. The evidence does not support those claims, and making them would contradict the honesty the book recommends. What the evidence does support is narrower: businesses that state their standards clearly, keep them consistently, price for sustainability, protect people's information, and repair mistakes openly give customers and workers good reasons to trust them. Trust is not a guarantee of success, but it is hard to build a lasting business without it.

# Chapter 1: Values You Can See

:::objectives
- Distinguish a value from a behavior and a behavior from a claim.
- Translate three personal values into actions customers and workers can observe.
- Rank responsibilities so that conflicts can be resolved in advance.
- Test whether your standards apply fairly to people of different beliefs.
:::

Most business owners can name their values quickly: honesty, respect, care, excellence, faith, family. The words are sincere, but they are not yet useful. A customer cannot observe "respect." A customer can observe whether you arrive on time, explain the price before starting, and ask before moving personal belongings. A value becomes useful in business when it is translated into a behavior that someone else can see.

## Key definitions

A value is a principle a person holds as important, such as honesty or care. A behavior is an action that expresses the value, such as stating what a service does not include. A claim is a statement about oneself, such as "we are an honest company." Claims cost nothing to make and are hard to check. Behaviors cost something and can be checked. This book asks you to rely on behaviors and to be cautious with claims.

A ranking of responsibilities is a written order in which you will weigh the interests of different groups when they conflict, such as customers, workers, the community, and owners. Most of the time, these interests align. When they do not, a ranking decided in advance makes the decision faster and more consistent.

## From value to behavior

The translation has three parts. First, define the value in plain words, as you mean it. "Care" might mean "treating a customer's home and belongings as if they were precious to me." Second, name a behavior a customer could observe and one a worker could observe. For care, a customer might see you ask before discarding any item; a worker might see that the schedule never requires more than six hours of lifting in a day. Third, check that the behavior applies to everyone equally, regardless of belief, background, or ability.

| Value | Customer-visible behavior | Worker-visible behavior | How to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Honesty | Written list of what is and is not included before booking | Pay calculation shared in writing | Booking page and pay stubs reviewed monthly |
| Care | Ask before discarding or moving any item | No more than six hours of heavy lifting per day | Session checklist; schedule review |
| Respect | Customer may decline any suggestion without pressure | Assistant may decline a job that feels unsafe | Post-session survey question; incident log |
| Stewardship | Donation and recycling options offered | Supplies bought in reusable containers | Monthly count of items donated |

## Ranking responsibilities

A ranking does not mean that lower-ranked groups do not matter. It means that you have thought in advance about whose interests come first when a choice forces a trade-off. Some owners put customers first, then workers, then community, then owners. Others put workers first on the theory that well-treated workers serve customers well. Either can be defended; the important thing is to decide, write it down, and use it.

## A framework for visible values

1. Write three values that guide your work and define each in one sentence.
2. For each value, write one customer-visible behavior and one worker-visible behavior.
3. Check each behavior for fairness across beliefs, backgrounds, and abilities.
4. Write a ranking of responsibilities for when interests conflict.
5. Publish the customer-visible behaviors where customers will see them.
6. Choose one way to check each behavior monthly.
7. Review the list each quarter and remove anything you are not actually doing.

:::case Johnson & Johnson's Credo and the 1982 Tylenol recall (real, cited)
In 1943, shortly before Johnson & Johnson became a publicly traded company, Robert Wood Johnson, the company's chairman from 1932 to 1963, wrote the document now known as Our Credo [2]. It ranks the company's responsibilities: first to the patients, doctors, nurses, parents, and others who use its products; then to employees; then to communities; and finally to stockholders, stating that "when we operate according to these principles, the stockholders should realize a fair return" [2]. In 1982, seven people in the Chicago area died after taking Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules that had been laced with cyanide; according to a Milwaukee Journal Sentinel history of the company, chief executive James Burke "seemed to be guided by the credo," and the company quickly warned consumers not to take Tylenol, recalled 31 million bottles at a cost of about $100 million, and redesigned the bottles to be tamper-resistant [3]. The facts are from the company's published Credo and press reporting and concern Johnson & Johnson, not TTI. The episode is widely taught because a written ranking of responsibilities, prepared decades earlier, gave leaders a basis for an expensive decision under pressure.
:::

:::example Clear Day Studio values map (illustrative)
Naomi lists three values: honesty, care, and respect. She translates honesty into a written "included and not included" list on her booking page, and she discovers that the list changes her numbers. In the prior quarter, 9 of 60 sessions, or 15 percent, ran over the booked time because customers expected her to haul donations away, which she had never offered. At $55 per hour and an average overrun of 1.5 hours, those overruns cost either the customer an unexpected 9 × 1.5 × $55 = $742.50 in total, or Naomi the same amount in unpaid time. She adds a line: "Donation drop-off available for $40 per carload; not included in the session rate." In the following quarter, overruns fall to 3 of 62 sessions, about 5 percent. She also writes her ranking: customers' safety first, then her assistant's well-being, then the community, then profit.
:::

# Chapter 2: Purpose and the Offer

:::objectives
- Distinguish purpose, mission, and offer.
- Describe an offer by deliverable, duration, price, exclusions, and exit.
- Avoid language that turns a purchase into a test of character or belief.
- Revise a giving or impact model when evidence shows it is not working.
:::

Purpose is the reason you want your work to matter. An offer is the specific thing a customer can choose to buy. They are related, but they are not the same, and confusing them causes problems in both directions. When purpose crowds out the offer, customers cannot tell what they will receive. When an offer is sold mainly on purpose, customers may feel that declining means rejecting a cause, which is a form of pressure.

## Key definitions

Purpose is the deeper reason for the business, such as "helping families feel calm at home." Mission is a specific statement of what the business does to pursue its purpose, such as "providing in-home organizing sessions that leave every room usable." An offer is a defined product or service, with a deliverable, a duration, a price, exclusions, and terms for leaving. An impact model is the method a business uses to contribute beyond its offer, such as donating a share of profit.

## Describing a clear offer

A clear offer answers five questions. What will the customer receive? How long will it take? What does it cost, including any extras? What is not included? How can the customer cancel or leave? A customer who can answer those questions can decide on the merits. Purpose can appear in the description, but it should not substitute for any of the five answers.

| Element | Purpose-heavy wording (avoid) | Clear offer wording |
|---|---|---|
| Deliverable | "Transform your life through order." | "One room sorted, labeled, and arranged." |
| Duration | "However long it takes." | "Three hours; extra time billed at $55 per hour with your approval." |
| Price | "An investment in your peace." | "$165 per three-hour session." |
| Exclusions | Not stated | "Does not include hauling, cleaning, or purchases." |
| Exit | Not stated | "Cancel free up to 48 hours before; $40 fee after." |

## Purpose without pressure

Some business owners describe their work in moral or spiritual terms. That is their right, and many customers appreciate knowing what motivates a provider. The risk arises when the description implies that buying is a sign of virtue or faith, or that not buying is a failure. Write about your purpose in the first person ("I do this work because...") and keep the offer itself neutral and concrete.

## Impact models

Many businesses commit to giving, such as donating a product for each one sold or a share of profit. These commitments can be meaningful, but they are also promises, and they must be sustainable. An impact model that the business cannot afford, or that does not achieve what it claims, should be revised openly.

## A framework for purpose and offer

1. Write your purpose in one sentence, for your own planning.
2. Write a mission that states what you do to pursue it.
3. Describe each offer using the five elements: deliverable, duration, price, exclusions, exit.
4. Remove language that ties a purchase to character, virtue, or faith.
5. If you have an impact model, state exactly what it is and how it is funded.
6. Check that the impact model is affordable at current margins.
7. Review purpose, offer, and impact model once a year, and explain any changes publicly.

:::case TOMS and the end of One for One (real, cited)
TOMS became known for its "One for One" model, giving a pair of shoes to a person in need for each pair sold, and it reported donating more than 95 million pairs by 2019 [6]. In November 2019, TOMS announced that it was moving away from that model toward giving one-third of net profits to a giving fund, with its chief giving officer saying, "Maybe it's time to evolve a little bit and maybe it's time to do more than just our one-for-one giving" [4]. Retail Brew reported at the time that critics had questioned the program's effectiveness for years and that the company faced a large loan coming due in 2020 [6]. In April 2021, TOMS said it would stop shoe giving and commit at least one-third of annual net profits to grassroots organizations focused on mental health, access to opportunity, and ending gun violence; its chief strategy and impact officer said the company wanted to ensure it "could sustain this model" long-term [5]. The facts come from published reporting and concern TOMS, not TTI. The case shows that an impact model is part of the offer customers buy, that it must be financially sustainable, and that changing it is easier to defend when the reasons are explained openly.
:::

:::example Clear Day Studio offer and giving pledge (illustrative)
Naomi had been telling customers, "Part of every session goes to families in need." When she writes it down, she realizes she has no fixed amount. She calculates her margin: a $165 session costs her about $48 in assistant pay, $9 in supplies, and $12 in mileage and overhead, for a contribution of $165 − $69 = $96 before her own pay. She decides on a pledge she can sustain: $5 per session to a local family shelter, about 5.2 percent of contribution. At 62 sessions per quarter, that is $310 per quarter, which she will report on her website each January with the receipt total. She rewrites her offer page using the five elements and moves her purpose statement to a short "Why I do this work" paragraph in the first person.
:::

:::checklist Purpose and offer
- Purpose written in one sentence.
- Mission states what the business does.
- Each offer has deliverable, duration, price, exclusions, and exit terms.
- No wording ties purchase to virtue, character, or faith.
- Impact model stated with a fixed, funded amount.
- Impact model affordable at current margins.
- Annual public report of giving planned.
:::

:::summary
Purpose explains why you work; an offer explains what a customer receives. Describe offers by deliverable, duration, price, exclusions, and exit, and keep purpose language in the first person so customers do not feel pressured. Impact models are promises that must be sustainable. TOMS's shift away from One for One shows how a giving model may need to change and why explaining the change matters.
:::

# Chapter 3: Belief and Inclusion at Work

:::objectives
- Describe the religious diversity of the people you are likely to serve and employ.
- Summarize employers' basic duties regarding religion under federal law.
- Explain the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in Groff v. DeJoy.
- Write workplace practices that respect belief without imposing it.
:::

A business owner's convictions may be central to why the business exists. The people the business serves and employs, however, hold many different beliefs, and some hold none. Treating everyone fairly, whatever they believe, is both an ethical standard and, for many employers, a legal requirement.

## The religious landscape

The Pew Research Center's 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study, a survey of 36,908 U.S. adults, found that 62 percent of U.S. adults identify as Christians, 29 percent are religiously unaffiliated (identifying as atheist, agnostic, or "nothing in particular"), and about 7 percent belong to other religions, including Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, and Hindu Americans [7]. A small business in almost any community will serve and employ people across this range.

## Federal law in brief

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits employers with 15 or more employees from discriminating on the basis of religion in hiring, firing, pay, assignments, promotions, and other terms of employment [8]. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) explains that employers must reasonably accommodate employees' sincerely held religious beliefs or practices unless doing so would cause undue hardship, that common accommodations include flexible scheduling, voluntary shift swaps, and changes to dress or grooming rules, and that an employee cannot be forced to participate, or not participate, in a religious activity as a condition of employment [8]. State and local laws may cover smaller employers. Even a business too small to be covered by Title VII can adopt these practices as a matter of fairness.

| Practice | Fair approach | Practice to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Hiring | Same criteria for all applicants | Asking about religion or church attendance |
| Scheduling | Consider swaps and flexible hours for religious observance | Refusing any change without exploring options |
| Prayer or devotions | Optional, clearly voluntary, no effect on job | Required attendance or pressure to join |
| Dress | Accommodate religious dress unless real hardship | Uniform rules with no exceptions |
| Customer service | Same service for every customer | Different treatment based on a customer's faith |
| Owner's expression | Owner may share personal motivation | Owner implies that workers must share beliefs |

## The difference between expression and imposition

An owner may describe how faith motivates the business, close on a day of rest, or donate to causes that reflect their convictions. Problems arise when belief becomes a condition of work or service: when workers feel they must participate in religious activities, when customers are treated differently based on belief, or when an owner's convictions are used to deny a worker's own religious practice.

## A framework for belief and inclusion

1. Learn which employment laws apply to your business, including state and local rules.
2. Write hiring criteria that relate only to the job.
3. Make any religious or reflective activity at work clearly voluntary, with no effect on pay or assignments.
4. Write an accommodation process: how to ask, who decides, how options are explored.
5. Consider multiple options, such as swaps or schedule changes, before saying no.
6. Train anyone who supervises on these practices.
7. Keep a record of requests and responses.
8. Review practices annually with a qualified advisor.

:::case Groff v. DeJoy: accommodating a day of rest (real, cited)
Gerald Groff worked as a rural carrier associate for the U.S. Postal Service, covering for absent employees; in 2017, the Postal Service began requiring him to work certain Sundays, and Groff, who observes a Sunday Sabbath, missed more than 20 Sunday shifts, was disciplined, and resigned in 2019 [9]. The lower courts ruled for the Postal Service, but in June 2023 a unanimous Supreme Court held that Title VII requires an employer that denies a religious accommodation to show that the burden "would result in substantial increased costs in relation to the conduct of its particular business," and that showing "more than a de minimis cost" is not enough [10]. The Court added that an employer must consider other options, such as voluntary shift swapping, and that a hardship attributable to coworkers' animosity toward religion cannot count as "undue" [10]. The facts come from the Court's opinion and a Congressional Research Service summary and concern those parties, not TTI. For a small business, the decision is a reminder that requests for religious accommodation deserve serious consideration of alternatives, and that the same respect an owner wants for their own convictions should be extended to workers.
:::

:::example Clear Day Studio accommodation request (illustrative)
Naomi's assistant, Priya, asks not to work on Friday evenings for religious observance. Naomi books about 5 Friday evening sessions per month, worth 5 × $165 = $825 in revenue. She considers options: move those sessions to Saturday mornings (customers can choose), have Naomi work them alone at a slower pace (adding about 1 hour each, which costs her 5 hours of time), or hire a second on-call helper at $20 per hour for 3 hours per session, which costs 5 × 3 × $20 = $300 per month. She offers Saturday slots first; 3 of 5 regular Friday customers switch. She handles the remaining 2 sessions alone. The accommodation costs her about 2 extra hours per month, which is not a substantial burden on her business, and she records the request and the decision in writing.
:::

The people a business serves and employs hold many beliefs and none. Federal law prohibits religious discrimination by employers with 15 or more employees and requires reasonable accommodation unless it would cause undue hardship. Groff v. DeJoy raised the bar for refusing accommodation and requires employers to consider alternatives. Owners may express their convictions, but they should not impose them on workers or customers.

# Chapter 4: Boundaries and Walk-Away Rules

:::objectives
- Define scope boundaries, availability boundaries, and walk-away rules.
- Calculate the cost of a boundary honestly.
- Communicate boundaries before a customer commits.
- Decide in advance which requests you will decline.
:::

A boundary is a limit that protects something you value: your time, your family, your health, your workers, or the quality of the work. Boundaries are not refusals to serve; they are definitions of what you serve and when. A business without boundaries often drifts into work it does poorly, hours it cannot sustain, and customers whose demands crowd out others.

## Key definitions

A scope boundary defines what work the business does and does not do. An availability boundary defines when the business is open and when it is not. A walk-away rule is a written condition under which the business will end or decline a job, such as unsafe conditions, abusive behavior, or requests that conflict with the law or the owner's stated standards. The cost of a boundary is the revenue or convenience given up by keeping it.

## Counting the cost honestly

Every boundary has a cost. Closing one day a week gives up that day's sales. Declining a type of work gives up its revenue. Pretending a boundary is free makes it fragile, because the first time the cost becomes visible, the temptation to abandon it is strong. Estimate the cost in advance and decide whether the boundary is worth it.

| Boundary type | Example | What it protects | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | "We do not haul or dispose of hazardous materials." | Safety; licensing compliance | Lost add-on revenue |
| Availability | "Closed Sundays." | Rest; family; worship for those who choose | One day of potential sales |
| Customer conduct | "We end a session if anyone is verbally abusive." | Workers' dignity and safety | Occasional lost session |
| Values | "We do not discard items without the owner's approval." | Trust; respect for belongings | Slower sessions |
| Capacity | "No more than 12 sessions per week." | Quality; health | Turned-away demand |

## Communicating boundaries

Boundaries work best when customers learn about them before they commit. Put scope and availability on the booking page; put conduct and walk-away rules in the service agreement; and state them plainly, without apology or judgment. A boundary stated early is information. A boundary stated in the middle of a job feels like a surprise.

## A framework for boundaries

1. List what you are protecting: time, health, family, workers, quality, convictions.
2. Write one boundary for each, in a single sentence.
3. Estimate the cost of each boundary per month or per year.
4. Decide whether each boundary is worth its cost; keep only those you will hold.
5. Write walk-away rules for safety, conduct, and legal conflicts.
6. Publish scope and availability; put conduct rules in the agreement.
7. Decide in advance who may approve an exception, and under what conditions.
8. Review boundaries every six months.

:::case Chick-fil-A's Sunday closure (real, cited)
Chick-fil-A says its founder, S. Truett Cathy, decided to close on Sundays in 1946 when he opened his first restaurant in Hapeville, Georgia; having worked seven days a week in restaurants open 24 hours, he wanted himself and his employees to have one day to rest, spend time with family, or worship if they chose, and the company says it upholds the practice today [11]. In October 2026, Fortune reported that the chain had reached $23.9 billion in sales while closed every Sunday, and that chief executive Andrew Cathy, the founder's grandson, said he had never calculated the sales given up because changing the policy is "never something that we're entertaining"; he described the closure as a luxury of private, family ownership and said employees value having a guaranteed day off [12]. The facts come from the company and Fortune and concern Chick-fil-A, not TTI. The case is useful not because closing on a particular day produces sales (the evidence cannot show that) but because it illustrates a boundary that was stated publicly, applied consistently, explained in terms of what it protects, and kept despite its cost.
:::

:::example Clear Day Studio boundary costs (illustrative)
Naomi decides to keep Sundays free and to cap her week at 12 sessions. She estimates the cost. Over the past year, customers asked for Sunday sessions about 3 times per month; at $165 each, that is 3 × 12 × $165 = $5,940 in potential annual revenue declined. The cap turns away about 2 requests per month in busy seasons (roughly 5 months), or 10 × $165 = $1,650 per year. The total cost of both boundaries is about $7,590 per year. She asks herself whether rest and quality are worth that amount and decides they are. She adds a waitlist so that turned-away customers can book the next opening, and she writes one exception: she may approve a Sunday session for a move-out deadline, no more than twice per year.
:::

# Chapter 5: Pricing for Care

:::objectives
- Explain why underpricing can undermine care for customers and workers.
- Build a price from full costs, fair pay, and reserves.
- Apply Zeynep Ton's research on good jobs to a small business.
- Offer discounts or sliding scales without hiding costs.
:::

Many values-driven owners feel uncomfortable charging enough. They want their work to be accessible, they worry about seeming greedy, or they believe that low prices are a form of service. But a price that does not cover full costs, fair pay, and reserves is not sustainable. When the business cannot pay its workers well, rest, replace equipment, or absorb a bad month, quality slips and the business may close, which helps no one.

## Key definitions

Full cost is the total cost of delivering a service, including labor, supplies, travel, overhead, and the owner's own pay. Fair pay is compensation sufficient for workers to live with dignity in your area. A reserve is money set aside for future needs such as slow seasons, repairs, or emergencies. A sliding scale is a pricing system in which customers pay different amounts based on ability, with the terms stated openly.

## Evidence on good jobs

Zeynep Ton, now a professor at MIT Sloan School of Management, studied retailers that combine low prices with good jobs. In a 2012 Harvard Business Review article, she argued that the presumed trade-off between investing in employees and keeping prices low is false, describing companies such as QuikTrip, Mercadona, Trader Joe's, and Costco that create a cycle of investment in employees, strong operational execution, and higher sales and profits [13]. In a 2017 article, she described the "good jobs strategy" as a system combining investment in people with four operational choices: focus and simplify, standardize and empower, cross-train, and operate with slack [14]. A small business is not a national retailer, but the lesson transfers: paying people well works best when the business is designed to use their skills, and it requires prices that fund it.

| Price component | What it covers | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Direct labor | Worker wages plus taxes and benefits | Using wage only, ignoring payroll costs |
| Owner pay | The owner's own time at a fair rate | Leaving it out entirely |
| Supplies and travel | Materials, mileage, tolls | Underestimating travel time |
| Overhead | Insurance, software, phone, marketing | Spreading too thin across too many sessions |
| Reserve | Slow seasons, repairs, emergencies | No reserve at all |
| Giving or impact | Pledged contributions | Promising more than margins allow |

## Honest pricing

Honest pricing also means avoiding deceptive tactics. The Federal Trade Commission's advertising guidance for small businesses explains that claims in advertising must be truthful and not misleading, and that businesses must have evidence to back up their claims [15]. That rules out fake "regular prices" used to make a discount look larger, hidden fees, and claims of urgency that are not true.

## Discounts and sliding scales

Accessibility can be built into pricing without hiding costs. A sliding scale with stated tiers, a limited number of reduced-rate sessions per month, or a scholarship fund lets an owner serve people with less ability to pay while keeping the full price visible and funded.

## A framework for pricing with care

1. List full costs per session, including owner pay at a fair hourly rate.
2. Set worker pay at a level you would consider fair if you held the job.
3. Add a reserve percentage, such as 5 to 10 percent.
4. Add any giving pledge as a fixed amount.
5. Calculate the price that covers all components.
6. Compare with local competitors and decide how to explain any difference.
7. Design accessibility deliberately: stated sliding-scale tiers or a fixed number of reduced-rate slots.
8. Review prices every six months and when costs change.

:::case Zeynep Ton and the good jobs strategy (real, cited)
In "Why 'Good Jobs' Are Good for Retailers," published in Harvard Business Review in 2012, Zeynep Ton wrote that too many retail managers believe they must offer bad jobs to keep prices low, and that her research found the presumed trade-off false [13]. She described retailers that cut labor to hit short-term targets as falling into a vicious cycle in which unmotivated, poorly trained employees cannot keep up, leading to lower sales and more cuts, while companies such as QuikTrip, Mercadona, Trader Joe's, and Costco invested in employees and achieved strong execution and profits [13]. In a 2017 follow-up, she reported that, working with a large retail chain, she and her students found that increasing average employee hours from under 15 to 30 per week, reducing schedule variability, and cutting turnover by almost half could lift sales productivity by more than 20 percent [14]. The findings are Ton's and concern the companies she studied, not TTI. For a small business, the research supports pricing that funds fair pay and some slack, paired with simple, focused operations.
:::

# Chapter 6: Consent Without Surveillance

:::objectives
- Distinguish care from surveillance in customer and worker relationships.
- Apply consent principles to photos, recordings, and personal information.
- Use the FTC's principles for protecting personal information.
- Write a privacy promise you can keep.
:::

Service businesses often enter private spaces: homes, closets, finances, health histories, family conflicts. That access is a form of trust. Care means using it only for the customer's benefit. Surveillance means collecting or using information beyond what the customer agreed to, or watching workers in ways that strip them of dignity.

## Key definitions

Consent is permission given freely, with understanding of what is being agreed to, and capable of being withdrawn. Data minimization means collecting only the information needed for a defined purpose. Surveillance means monitoring people beyond what is necessary and agreed. A privacy promise is a short public statement of what information a business collects, why, who can see it, and how long it is kept.

## Consent in practice

Before taking photos in a customer's home, ask, explain the purpose, and offer a choice. "May I take before-and-after photos for your records? I won't share them without your written permission." Make declining easy and costless. If you want to use photos in marketing, get separate written permission and remove identifying details.

## Protecting information

The Federal Trade Commission's guide to protecting personal information sets out five principles: take stock of the information you have, scale down to what you need, lock it, pitch what you no longer need, and plan ahead for incidents [17]. For a small service business, that might mean keeping customer notes in one secured app, deleting photos after a set period, and knowing what to do if a phone is lost.

| Situation | Care approach | Surveillance approach (avoid) |
|---|---|---|
| Before-and-after photos | Ask; explain use; offer to skip | Take photos without asking |
| Marketing use | Separate written permission; no identifying details | Post without permission |
| Customer notes | Only what is needed to serve them | Personal judgments or unrelated details |
| Worker oversight | Clear expectations; check results | Constant location or camera monitoring |
| Data retention | Delete after a set period | Keep everything indefinitely |
| Access | Only people who need it | Shared logins; everyone sees everything |

## Workers deserve dignity too

Monitoring tools are easy to buy: GPS trackers, cameras, keystroke loggers. Some monitoring may be necessary for safety or legal reasons. But constant surveillance signals distrust and can harm the relationship it claims to protect. Set clear expectations, check results, and tell workers what, if anything, is monitored and why.

## A framework for consent and privacy

1. List every type of personal information you collect from customers and workers.
2. For each, write why you need it; stop collecting anything without a clear reason.
3. Ask for consent before photos or recordings; make declining easy.
4. Get separate written permission for any marketing use.
5. Limit access to those who need it, with individual logins and two-factor authentication.
6. Set deletion periods and follow them.
7. Tell workers what is monitored and why.
8. Publish a short privacy promise and keep it.

:::case Ring and the misuse of customer videos (real, cited)
In May 2023, the Federal Trade Commission charged Ring, the home security camera company, with compromising its customers' privacy by allowing any employee or contractor to access consumers' private videos and by failing to implement basic privacy and security protections [16]. According to the complaint, one employee over several months viewed thousands of video recordings belonging to female users of cameras in intimate spaces such as bathrooms and bedrooms, and was not stopped until another employee discovered the misconduct [16]. Under the proposed order, Ring was required to pay $5.8 million for consumer refunds, delete data products derived from videos it unlawfully reviewed, and implement a privacy and security program with safeguards on human review of videos and multi-factor authentication [16]. The facts come from the FTC and concern Ring, not TTI. For any business entrusted with access to people's homes or personal information, the case shows that care requires restricting who can see what, monitoring access, and keeping promises about privacy.
:::

:::example Clear Day Studio privacy audit (illustrative)
Naomi lists what she collects: names, addresses, phone numbers, gate codes, before-and-after photos, and notes about customers' preferences. She finds 1,840 photos on her phone from the past two years, 1,200 of which belong to customers whose projects are complete. # Chapter 7: Repairing an Error

:::objectives
- Describe the steps of a responsible repair after a mistake.
- Distinguish apology, explanation, remedy, and prevention.
- Write a repair protocol before you need it.
- Make commitments specific enough to be checked.
:::

Every business makes mistakes. A session runs late, an item is broken, a bill is wrong, a promise is forgotten. What customers remember is often less the mistake itself than how the business responds. For owners who care about integrity, repair is where values become most visible, because repair costs something and admits fault.

## Key definitions

An apology is a statement that acknowledges harm and expresses regret, without excuses. An explanation describes what happened and why, without shifting blame. A remedy is what the business does to make the affected person whole, such as a refund, replacement, or credit. Prevention is a change in process that makes the same mistake less likely. A repair protocol is a written plan for responding to mistakes, prepared in advance.

## The elements of repair

A responsible repair has five elements. Acknowledge quickly, ideally within a day. Apologize plainly, naming the harm. Explain what happened, accepting responsibility for what was within your control. Offer a remedy proportional to the harm. Change something to prevent a repeat, and tell the person what you changed.

| Element | Strong example | Weak example |
|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge | Call the same day | Wait for the customer to complain twice |
| Apologize | "I'm sorry I broke your vase. That was my mistake." | "Sorry if you were upset." |
| Explain | "I set it on an unstable shelf." | "These things happen." |
| Remedy | Pay replacement cost | Offer a small discount on the next session |
| Prevent | New rule: fragile items go on the floor, never on shelves | No change |

## Specific commitments

General promises ("we'll do better") are hard to check. Specific commitments ("we will call you within 24 hours if we must reschedule, and if we cancel within 24 hours, your next session is free") can be checked, and they show that the business is willing to bear a cost if it fails again.

## A framework for repair

1. Write a repair protocol before a mistake happens.
2. Set a response time, such as same day for any reported problem.
3. Use plain apology language that names the harm.
4. Explain what happened without blaming others or circumstances you controlled.
5. Offer a remedy proportional to the harm, with authority levels for who can approve what.
6. Identify one process change that prevents a repeat.
7. Tell the affected person what changed.
8. Log every mistake and review the log quarterly for patterns.

:::case JetBlue's Customer Bill of Rights (real, cited)
After a Valentine's Day ice storm in February 2007, JetBlue's operations broke down: the Los Angeles Times reported that about 1,100 flights were canceled over six days and more than 130,000 passengers were affected, and some passengers were stranded on aircraft at New York's JFK airport for up to 10 hours [20][18]. Chief executive David Neeleman apologized publicly; in an April 2007 statement to a House subcommittee, he called it "the singular worst operational day in our company's seven year history" and said, "JetBlue let our customers down and we know it" [18]. The company issued a Customer Bill of Rights, made retroactive to the storm, with specific compensation, including travel vouchers scaled to the length of delays, deplaning after five-hour ground delays, and $1,000 for customers involuntarily bumped [18]. The Los Angeles Times reported that Neeleman said the airline would spend $26 million to compensate affected customers, and Harvard Business School's Working Knowledge noted that the policy became a teaching case, with students debating whether such specific commitments were necessary [20][19]. The facts come from JetBlue's testimony and published reporting and concern JetBlue, not TTI. The case illustrates every element of repair: acknowledgment, apology, explanation, remedy, and specific commitments for the future.
:::

:::example Clear Day Studio repair (illustrative)
During a session, Naomi's assistant discards a box the customer had marked "maybe," which held family letters. The customer calls upset. Naomi calls back within two hours, apologizes ("We threw away something you had not approved, and I'm deeply sorry"), and explains that the box was not clearly separated from the discard pile. The bag has already gone to the landfill, so the letters cannot be recovered. Naomi refunds the full session ($225) and offers a free follow-up session ($225 value), for a total remedy of $450. She changes her process: discard bags stay in the customer's home until the customer approves them at the end of the session. Over the next quarter, her repair log shows zero items discarded without approval in 58 sessions.
:::

# Chapter 8: Reflection With Humility

:::objectives
- Use evidence, not feelings alone, to evaluate decisions.
- Run a premortem before a major change.
- Invite dissent safely from workers and customers.
- Recognize when a decision made on principle needs revisiting.
:::

Convictions can be a source of strength, but they can also make it hard to see mistakes. An owner who believes a decision was made for the right reasons may resist evidence that it caused harm. Humility, in a business sense, means holding decisions open to evidence, inviting people to disagree, and changing course when the facts call for it.

## Key definitions

Reflection is a regular, structured review of decisions and their effects. Evidence means observable information, such as numbers, written feedback, or documented events, as opposed to impressions. A premortem is an exercise in which a team imagines that a plan has already failed and lists reasons why. Dissent means disagreement expressed by someone with knowledge of the situation. Psychological safety is the belief that one can speak up without punishment.

## Evidence and feelings

Feelings are information too, but they are not enough. An owner who feels a new policy is working should check: Are complaints up or down? Has anyone left? What do workers say when asked privately? A monthly review of a few key numbers, plus written feedback, keeps reflection grounded.

## The premortem

Psychologist Gary Klein described the premortem in Harvard Business Review in 2007. The leader tells the team to imagine that the project has failed spectacularly; each person independently writes down every reason they can think of, especially reasons they would ordinarily be reluctant to mention, and the team then reviews the list to strengthen the plan [21]. Klein cited research finding that imagining an event has already occurred, which he called prospective hindsight, increases the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by 30 percent, and he noted that the method makes it safe for knowledgeable dissenters to speak up [21].

| Practice | Purpose | How often |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly numbers review | Check effects with evidence | Monthly |
| Anonymous feedback | Hear what people will not say directly | Quarterly |
| Premortem | Find weaknesses before a major change | Before each major decision |
| After-action review | Learn from what happened | After major projects or problems |
| Advisor check-in | Get an outside view | Twice a year |

## When principle meets evidence

Some decisions rest on principle and should not be reversed merely because they are inconvenient. Others turn out to rest on a mistaken assumption about how people will respond. The distinction matters: a boundary like a day of rest may be worth its cost, while a policy announced without consultation may need revisiting if it damages trust. Humility means asking honestly which kind of decision you are facing.

## A framework for reflection

1. Choose three to five numbers that show whether your business is serving people well.
2. Review them monthly, alongside written feedback.
3. Before any major change, run a premortem with those affected.
4. Collect anonymous feedback from workers at least quarterly.
5. Separate the principle behind a decision from the way it was implemented.
6. When evidence shows harm, say so, and decide what to change.
7. Keep a decision log with reasons, so later reviews can check assumptions.
8. Meet with an outside advisor twice a year.

:::case Basecamp's 2021 policy change (real, cited)
In April 2021, Basecamp chief executive Jason Fried announced in a blog post several policy changes, including that "societal and political discussions" would no longer be allowed on the company's internal Basecamp account; the changes also ended committees, 360-degree reviews, and certain benefits [22]. The company offered severance of up to six months' salary to employees who did not want to stay, and The Register, citing The Verge, reported that 18 of 57 employees accepted the package [22]. The Verge reported that the policy followed internal tensions over a long-running list of customer names some employees found offensive and over how the company should address it [23]. On May 4, Fried published an apology: "We started with policy changes that felt simple, reasonable, and principled, and it blew things up culturally in ways we never anticipated. David and I completely own the consequences, and we're sorry. We have a lot to learn and reflect on, and we will" [22]. The facts come from published reporting and concern Basecamp, not TTI. Whatever one thinks of the policy itself, the case shows how a decision that seemed principled to its authors can have consequences they did not foresee, and why consulting those affected before acting, as in a premortem, can surface concerns early.
:::

:::example Clear Day Studio premortem (illustrative)
Naomi plans to raise her standard price from $165 to $225 (Chapter 5). Before announcing it, she runs a premortem with her assistant and two long-time customers. Each writes reasons the change "failed." The list includes: regular customers felt surprised; the sliding scale was confusing; the website still showed the old price; new customers compared her to a $150 competitor. She makes four changes: she gives regular customers 60 days' notice and honors the old price for already-booked sessions; she simplifies the sliding scale to one reduced rate; she updates every page before announcing; and she adds a comparison of what is included. After 90 days, she reviews the evidence: bookings fell from 62 to 56 sessions per quarter, about 10 percent, but revenue rose from 62 × $165 = $10,230 to about 48 × $225 + 8 × $165 = $12,120.
:::

# Chapter 9: Rest and Capacity

:::objectives
- Treat rest as a business practice, not only a personal preference.
- Measure capacity in hours, energy, and quality.
- Evaluate evidence on reduced working time with appropriate caution.
- Decide what to delegate using a buyback calculation.
:::

Many spiritual and ethical traditions value rest: a Sabbath, a day of reflection, seasons of renewal. Many secular thinkers make similar arguments about sustainability and well-being. In a small business, rest is often the first thing sacrificed. The owner works evenings and weekends, answers messages at all hours, and postpones time off until the business is "stable." The result can be exhaustion, mistakes, and a business that depends on an owner who cannot keep going.

## Key definitions

Capacity is the amount of work a person or business can do well over a sustained period. Rest is planned time away from work. Delegation is assigning work to someone else. A buyback rate is the hourly cost below which it makes sense to pay someone else to do a task, freeing the owner's time.

## Evidence on working time

The largest trial of a four-day week to date took place in the United Kingdom from June to December 2022, with 61 companies and about 2,900 workers; companies maintained pay while reducing working time meaningfully [24]. According to the report, 56 of the 61 companies continued with the four-day week after the pilot, 18 confirmed it as permanent, 71 percent of employees reported reduced burnout, and revenue rose 1.4 percent on average across the 23 organizations that provided data [24]. Researchers at the University of Cambridge reported a 65 percent reduction in sick days and a 57 percent fall in staff leaving compared with the same period the previous year [25]. The evidence deserves caution: a House of Lords Library summary noted questions about whether the results are representative, given that substantial portions of participants did not complete final evaluations, and whether revenue is a good measure of productivity [26]. The trial suggests that reduced hours can work for some organizations; it does not prove they will work for every business.

## Buying back time

Entrepreneur and author Dan Martell has popularized a simple way to decide what to delegate: divide your annual income by 2,000 working hours to get an hourly rate, then divide by four to find a "buyback rate"; tasks that could be done by someone else for less than that rate are candidates to delegate. Entrepreneur magazine gives the example that an owner earning $100,000 has an hourly rate of $50 and a buyback rate of $12.50 [27]. The calculation is a rough guide, not a rule, and it works only if the freed time goes to higher-value work or rest.

| Capacity measure | How to track | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Hours worked | Weekly log | More than your stated limit for three weeks |
| Rest days | Calendar | No full day off in two weeks |
| Error rate | Repair log | Mistakes rising |
| Energy | Weekly 1–10 self-rating | Below 5 for three weeks |
| Response time | Message log | Slower replies despite longer hours |

## A framework for rest and capacity

1. Set a weekly hour limit and at least one full rest day.
2. Track hours, rest days, errors, and energy weekly.
3. Calculate your buyback rate.
4. List tasks that someone else could do for less than that rate.
5. Delegate one task at a time, with written instructions.
6. Plan at least one week of time off per year, with coverage arranged.
7. Treat warning signs as data, not as personal failure.
8. Review capacity quarterly before taking on more work.

:::case The UK four-day week pilot (real, cited)
From June to December 2022, 61 UK organizations, ranging from finance firms to a fish-and-chip shop, took part in a pilot organized by 4 Day Week Global with the think tank Autonomy, the 4 Day Week Campaign, and researchers from the University of Cambridge and Boston College; pay was maintained at 100 percent while working time was meaningfully reduced [24][25]. The February 2023 report found that 92 percent of the companies, 56 of 61, were continuing with the four-day week, 39 percent of employees were less stressed, and 15 percent said no amount of money would induce them to accept a five-day schedule again [24]. The Cambridge researchers reported a 65 percent reduction in sick days and a 57 percent fall in the number of staff leaving [25]. A House of Lords Library summary recorded criticism that the results may not be representative and that revenue is an imperfect measure of productivity [26]. The results belong to the organizations that took part and do not predict outcomes for TTI or any reader. For a small business, the pilot is evidence that rest and productivity are not always in conflict, and a reminder to test changes carefully rather than assume the results will transfer.
:::

:::example Clear Day Studio capacity check (illustrative)
Naomi logs her hours for four weeks: 58, 61, 55, and 63, for an average of 59.25 hours per week. Her energy ratings average 4 out of 10, and her repair log shows three mistakes in the month, compared with one in each prior month. She expects to earn about $60,000 this year. Her hourly rate is $60,000 ÷ 2,000 = $30, and her buyback rate is $30 ÷ 4 = $7.50. That is below the local wage for most help, so she adjusts: she will delegate tasks that cost less than her $30 hourly rate if they also free time for paid sessions or rest. Bookkeeping takes her 5 hours per month; a bookkeeper charges $150 per month, or $30 per hour, which is equal to her hourly rate. She hires the bookkeeper and uses the 5 hours for one more paid session ($225) and rest. She sets a limit of 48 hours per week and one full rest day, and checks her numbers again in a month.
:::

# Chapter 10: Growth and Stewardship

:::objectives
- Decide whether to grow, hold steady, or scale back using evidence.
- Consider how ownership structure protects or weakens values.
- Plan for the long-term future of the business.
- Measure stewardship in concrete terms.
:::

Stewardship is the idea that a business is held in trust: for customers, workers, community, and future generations, not only for its owner. Many faith traditions use the word; many secular thinkers express the same idea in terms of responsibility or legacy. Stewardship affects decisions about growth, because growth can strengthen a business's ability to serve or strain its values beyond recognition.

## Key definitions

Growth means increasing revenue, customers, staff, or locations. Stewardship means managing resources responsibly on behalf of others. Ownership structure is the legal arrangement of who owns and controls a business. Succession is the plan for who will lead or own the business in the future. A capacity check is a review of whether the business can take on more work without harming quality or people.

## Grow, hold, or scale back

Growth is not automatically good or bad. Before expanding, check capacity (Chapter 9), margins (Chapter 5), and evidence (Chapter 8). Ask whether growth would let you serve people better or simply busier. Many small businesses do not survive long: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that 34.7 percent of business establishments born in 2013 were still operating in 2023 [31]. A slower, sustainable path can be a form of stewardship. The U.S. Small Business Administration's guidance on writing a business plan is a useful starting point for testing growth plans against realistic numbers [1].

| Option | When it fits | Key risks |
|---|---|---|
| Grow | Demand exceeds capacity; margins healthy; systems ready | Quality slips; culture strains |
| Hold steady | Business meets goals; owner at sustainable capacity | Complacency; slow erosion |
| Scale back | Owner overworked; margins thin; quality falling | Revenue loss; disappointed customers |
| Change structure | Values need protection beyond the founder | Legal cost; loss of control |

## Ownership and values

How a business is owned affects whether its values survive the founder. A sole owner can keep values strong but may sell to someone with different priorities. Options such as written operating agreements, employee ownership, or purpose-oriented structures can protect values, though each has costs and trade-offs that require legal advice.

## A framework for growth and stewardship

1. Write what you are stewarding: people, quality, community, convictions.
2. Review capacity, margins, and evidence before deciding to grow.
3. Choose grow, hold, or scale back, and write why.
4. If growing, add one capacity at a time, such as one worker or one service.
5. Write down the values a successor must keep.
6. Learn about ownership structures that might protect those values, with legal advice.
7. Draft a succession plan, even a simple one.
8. Report stewardship measures annually, such as pay, giving, and customer satisfaction.

:::case Patagonia's ownership transfer (real, cited)
In September 2022, Patagonia founder Yvon Chouinard announced that the Chouinard family had transferred ownership of the company: 100 percent of the voting stock went to the Patagonia Purpose Trust, created to protect the company's values, and 100 percent of the nonvoting stock went to the Holdfast Collective, a nonprofit dedicated to fighting the environmental crisis [28]. The company said that each year, profits not reinvested in the business would be distributed as a dividend to the Collective, projected at about $100 million annually depending on the health of the business, and that Patagonia would remain a B Corp and continue giving 1 percent of sales to grassroots activists [29]. "We're making Earth our only shareholder," Chouinard said [29]. Fortune noted that the family retains significant control, because it guides the trust that holds all voting rights, and that dividends will be set at the board's discretion [30]. The facts come from Patagonia and Fortune and concern Patagonia, not TTI. The case shows how an ownership structure can be designed to protect a company's values after its founder, and why such structures deserve close reading of who controls them.
:::

:::example Clear Day Studio growth decision (illustrative)
Naomi's waitlist averages 8 requests per month. She considers hiring a second assistant to run a second team two days per week. Each second-team session at $225 would cost $60 for an assistant (3 hours at $20), $75 for a trained lead organizer (3 hours at $25), $15 for supplies, and $22.92 in overhead ($1,100 ÷ 48). The contribution would be $225 − $60 − $75 − $15 − $22.92 = $52.08 per session. At 8 sessions per month, that is about $416.64 per month, before about 20 hours of training time. She decides to hold steady for six months, train her current assistant as a lead, and revisit the decision when her own hours are within her 48-hour limit. She also writes a one-page "values a successor must keep" document and asks an attorney about including it in her operating agreement.
:::

:::checklist Growth and stewardship
- What I am stewarding is written down.
- Capacity, margins, and evidence reviewed.
- Decision to grow, hold, or scale back recorded with reasons.
- Growth added one capacity at a time.
- Values for a successor written.
- Ownership options explored with legal advice.

# Appendix F: Premortem Guide

The premortem, described by Gary Klein, asks a team to imagine that a plan has already failed and to explain why [21]. It works best when people write their reasons independently before discussing them.

1. Describe the plan in two or three sentences.
2. Say: "Imagine it is one year from now, and this plan has failed. Write down every reason why."
3. Give everyone five minutes to write alone.
4. Go around the group, taking one reason at a time, until all are listed.
5. Mark the three most serious reasons.
6. Change the plan to address each one.
7. Set a date to review the evidence.

# Glossary

Accommodation: a change to work rules or schedules that allows a person to observe a religious practice or meet another protected need.

Buyback rate: an hourly figure, popularized by Dan Martell, below which it may make sense to pay someone else to do a task.

Capacity: the amount of work a person or business can do well over a sustained period.

Consent: a clear, informed agreement given before information is collected or used.

Contribution: the money left from a sale after the direct costs of that sale.

Data minimization: collecting only the information needed for a stated purpose.

Dissent: disagreement expressed by someone with knowledge of a situation.

Giving pledge: a specific, measurable promise to donate money or time.

Mission: a short statement of whom a business serves and how.

Overhead: costs that do not vary with each job, such as insurance, software, and rent.

Premortem: an exercise in which a team imagines a plan has failed and lists reasons why.

Psychological safety: the belief that one can speak up without being punished.

Purpose: the reason a business exists beyond earning money.

Repair: the process of acknowledging, apologizing for, and remedying a harm, and changing what caused it.

Sliding scale: a pricing method that offers reduced rates under a written eligibility rule.

Stewardship: managing resources responsibly on behalf of others, now and in the future.

Succession: the plan for who will lead or own a business in the future.

Undue hardship: under Groff v. DeJoy, a substantial increase in costs in relation to the conduct of an employer's particular business.

Values: the principles that guide how a business behaves, especially under pressure.

Walk-away rule: a written condition under which the business will decline or end work.
