# How to Use This Book

This book is written for owners of small businesses—especially service firms, trades, makers, and shops with fewer than twenty employees—who want to read and use their own numbers with confidence. It assumes no accounting background. It does assume that you are willing to look at your records every month, ask questions when a figure does not make sense, and make decisions based on evidence rather than on the balance in your checking account.

Financial literacy, in the sense used here, is the ability to answer five practical questions at any time: Am I making money? Where did the cash go? What do I own and owe? How much must I sell to cover my costs? What will my bank balance look like in eight weeks? Each chapter builds one part of the toolkit needed to answer those questions, from clean bookkeeping through financial statements, cash forecasting, contribution and break-even analysis, job costing, taxes, debt, owner pay, and a monthly close routine.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this book you should be able to:

- Set up a bookkeeping system that separates business and personal money and supports tax filings.
- Read an income statement and calculate gross margin and operating margin.
- Build a simple balance sheet and explain how it connects to the income statement.
- Prepare a thirteen-week cash forecast and identify timing gaps before they arrive.
- Calculate contribution margin and break-even volume for a product or service.
- Price a job using direct costs, labor, overhead, and target profit, and compare estimates with actual results.
- Keep the tax records that matter, and plan for estimated taxes and payroll obligations.
- Compare financing offers by their true cost and repayment terms.
- Pay yourself on purpose and set aside profit and tax reserves.
- Run a monthly close and review a one-page dashboard.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, defines key terms, and presents a step-by-step framework. Every chapter includes a worked example with real arithmetic, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Each chapter also contains a case study drawn from public sources—government research, court records, regulatory actions, and company filings. The cases include successes, failures, and frauds, because the purpose is to show how numbers behave in real businesses. The results in those cases belong to the people and organizations involved, not to Total Transformation Inc. (TTI).

The worked examples follow one fictional business, Northside Print & Label, owned by a fictional entrepreneur named Andre Wallace. The shop designs and prints labels, signs, and branded packaging for local food producers and retailers. Its signature order is 40 units of custom labels at $75 per unit, or $3,000. The numbers stay consistent from chapter to chapter so you can see how a single month flows through the books, the statements, the forecast, and the close. Northside and Andre are illustrative only; they are not TTI customers, and their figures are not benchmarks or promises.

# Chapter 1: The Language of Numbers

:::objectives
- Explain why financial literacy affects business survival.
- Distinguish profit, cash, and wealth.
- Name the three core financial statements and what each answers.
- Set up a monthly rhythm for reviewing your numbers.
:::

Accounting is often called the language of business. Like any language, it can be learned in layers: first the vocabulary, then the grammar, then fluency. An owner does not need to become an accountant, any more than a traveler needs to become a linguist. But an owner who cannot read the basic statements is dependent on others to explain what is happening in their own company, and is more likely to be surprised by cash shortages, tax bills, or losses hidden behind growing sales.

## Why it matters

Most new businesses do not last a decade. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reported that only 34.7 percent of private-sector business establishments born in March 2013 were still operating in March 2023; in other words, about one-third survived ten years [1]. Survival rates varied by industry, but every industry lost a large share of its new establishments over the decade [1]. Many factors drive closure—demand, competition, health, family needs, the economy—and financial literacy alone does not guarantee survival. It does, however, give an owner earlier warning and better options.

The Federal Reserve's Small Business Credit Survey reinforces how central money management is. In the 2025 survey of 6,525 employer firms, rising costs of goods, services, or wages were the most common financial challenge, and 56 percent of firms that sought financing did so to meet operating expenses [2]. Those are cash and margin problems—problems that the tools in this book are designed to surface early.

## Profit, cash, and wealth

Three ideas are often confused:

1. Profit is revenue earned minus expenses incurred over a period. It is measured on the income statement.
2. Cash is money available in bank accounts and on hand at a moment. Its movements are shown on the cash flow statement.
3. Wealth (or equity) is what the owner would have left if the business sold its assets and paid its debts. It is shown on the balance sheet.

A business can be profitable and short of cash (customers have not paid yet), cash-rich and unprofitable (a loan just arrived), or profitable with little equity (because the owner withdraws everything). Learning to separate the three is the first step toward fluency.

## The three core statements

| Statement | Question it answers | Time frame | Key lines |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income statement (profit and loss) | Did we make money? | A period (month, quarter, year) | Revenue, cost of goods sold, gross profit, operating expenses, net income |
| Balance sheet | What do we own and owe? | A single date | Assets, liabilities, owner's equity |
| Cash flow statement | Where did the cash come from and go? | A period | Operating, investing, and financing cash flows |

The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) describes the balance sheet as the foundation of managing finances, a snapshot that tracks assets, liabilities, and equity, and recommends that owners maintain proper bookkeeping and a basic knowledge of business finances [4].

## A monthly rhythm

1. Record transactions weekly so the month-end is not a scramble.
2. Reconcile every bank and credit card account to its statement within ten days after month-end.
3. Produce an income statement and balance sheet for the month.
4. Update the thirteen-week cash forecast.
5. Review a one-page dashboard and write down one decision.
6. File the month's records where you and your tax professional can find them.

:::case The Small Business Credit Survey: what firms report about money (real, cited)
The Small Business Credit Survey is conducted by the twelve Federal Reserve Banks. Its 2025 survey, fielded from September to November 2025, received 6,525 responses from employer firms with 1 to 499 employees [2]. Among its findings: 31 percent of firms had no outstanding debt; of firms with debt, 59 percent used a personal guarantee to secure it; 60 percent of firms applied for financing in the prior twelve months, most commonly to meet operating expenses (56 percent) or to pursue expansion (46 percent); and 42 percent of applicants received the full amount they sought, while 22 percent received none [2]. Among borrowers from online lenders, 60 percent reported that actual borrowing costs were higher than expected [2]. These are survey findings about other firms, not TTI results. They suggest three lessons that recur in this book: cash for operations is a common need, personal guarantees put owners' household finances at risk, and the true cost of borrowing is frequently misunderstood.
:::

:::example Andre's first look (illustrative)
Andre opened Northside Print & Label two years ago. In April, he completed and invoiced $9,000 of work, including the 40-unit, $3,000 label order. His bank balance fell from $8,400 to $6,100 during the month, and he assumed April had been a losing month.

When his bookkeeper produced the April statements, the picture was different:

| Measure | April |
|---|---|
| Revenue earned (invoiced) | $9,000 |
| Expenses incurred | $7,350 |
| Net income (profit) | $1,650 |
| Cash collected from customers | $6,750 |
| Cash paid out (including a $1,200 equipment deposit and $600 owner draw) | $9,050 |
| Change in bank balance | −$2,300 |

April was profitable. Cash fell because $2,250 of invoices were still unpaid at month-end, and because Andre made an equipment deposit and an owner draw, neither of which is an operating expense. The rest of this book explains each of these differences.
:::

:::checklist Monthly rhythm
- Transactions recorded at least weekly.
- Bank and card accounts reconciled within ten days of month-end.
- Income statement and balance sheet produced.
- Cash forecast updated.
- Dashboard reviewed and one decision written down.
- Records filed for tax purposes.
:::

:::summary
Financial literacy is the ability to answer basic questions about profit, cash, and equity at any time. BLS data show that about one-third of new establishments survive ten years, and Federal Reserve survey data show that operating cash and costs are common pressures. Profit, cash, and wealth are different measures, reported on the income statement, cash flow statement, and balance sheet. A simple monthly rhythm turns records into decisions.

# Chapter 2: Clean Bookkeeping

:::objectives
- Choose between cash and accrual methods with professional guidance.
- Build a simple chart of accounts.
- Keep source documents that support every entry.
- Reconcile accounts and apply basic internal controls.
:::

Bookkeeping is the systematic recording of business transactions. Clean books are complete (every transaction recorded), accurate (correct amounts and categories), timely (recorded soon after they occur), and supported (backed by a source document). Clean books are the raw material for everything that follows; no analysis can be better than the records beneath it.

## Cash and accrual methods

Under the cash method, income is generally recorded when received and expenses when paid. Under the accrual method, income is generally recorded when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of when cash moves. IRS Publication 538 explains accounting periods and methods, including when businesses may use the cash method and when the accrual method is required [21]. Many very small service businesses use the cash method for taxes; many owners nevertheless find accrual-based reports more useful for management because they match revenue with the costs of earning it. Ask a qualified professional which method fits your obligations.

## A simple chart of accounts

A chart of accounts is the list of categories used to classify transactions. Keep it short enough to use consistently.

| Type | Example accounts |
|---|---|
| Assets | Checking, savings, accounts receivable, inventory/supplies, equipment |
| Liabilities | Accounts payable, credit card, sales tax payable, payroll liabilities, loans |
| Equity | Owner contributions, owner draws, retained earnings |
| Revenue | Label sales, sign sales, design fees, rush fees |
| Cost of goods sold | Label stock, ink, outsourced printing, direct labor |
| Operating expenses | Rent, utilities, software, insurance, marketing, vehicle, professional fees |

## Source documents

IRS Publication 583 explains that businesses must keep records that support the income, deductions, and credits shown on their returns, and describes supporting documents such as sales slips, invoices, receipts, deposit slips, and canceled checks [5]. The IRS also advises that records should show gross receipts, purchases, expenses, assets, and their sources [6]. A good practice is to name each digital file consistently—date, vendor or customer, and purpose—and store it in a folder by month.

## The bookkeeping framework

1. Open separate business checking and credit card accounts; never run personal spending through them. The FDIC, citing IRS Publication 583, recommends a business checking account kept separate from personal checking, and points owners to the free Money Smart for Small Business program it offers with the SBA [22].
2. Set up a chart of accounts like the one above.
3. Record each sale as an invoice and each payment received as a separate event.
4. Record each bill when it arrives and each payment when made (for accrual reporting).
5. Attach or file a source document for every transaction.
6. Reconcile every account to its statement monthly, investigating differences instead of forcing totals.
7. Apply internal controls appropriate to your size.

## Internal controls for small businesses

Internal controls are practices that protect assets and ensure records are accurate. In very small businesses, one person often handles everything, which removes natural checks. Practical controls include: the owner personally reviews bank statements every month; only the owner can add new vendors or payees; someone other than the bookkeeper opens bank statements or has view-only access; payments over a set amount require the owner's approval; and the owner periodically compares vendor payments with actual invoices.

:::case A trusted bookkeeper and two small businesses (real, cited)
In February 2025, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Western District of North Carolina announced that a bookkeeper was sentenced to 40 months in prison and ordered to pay $1,644,235.77 in restitution for embezzling about $1.6 million from two small businesses in Mooresville, North Carolina, between 2016 and 2022 [7]. According to court documents, he abused his access to the companies' accounts to make more than 100 fraudulent transfers to accounts he controlled, and disguised them with fake entries categorizing the transfers as payments to existing vendors for software development, advertising, and marketing [7]. He began stealing from the second company while already stealing from the first [7]. The facts are from the government's announcement. The case illustrates why bank reconciliation, owner review of payees, and comparison of vendor payments with real invoices matter even when the bookkeeper is trusted.
:::

:::example Recording the 40-unit order (illustrative)
On April 30, Northside invoices a customer $3,000 for 40 units at $75. The customer has paid a $2,250 deposit on April 10. Andre's records show:

| Date | Entry | Accrual effect | Cash effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| April 10 | Deposit received | Customer deposit (liability) $2,250 | +$2,250 |
| April 30 | Order delivered and invoiced | Revenue $3,000; deposit applied $2,250; receivable $750 | $0 |
| May 12 | Balance received | Receivable reduced $750 | +$750 |

Andre's month-end summary showed $2,250 unpaid. The $750 balance on the label order accounts for part of it; the rest comes from two other April invoices: a $1,200 sign order and a $300 design fee, both unpaid. Total receivables at April 30 are $750 + $1,200 + $300 = $2,250. Recording each sale and payment as separate events is what allows him to see that figure at all.
:::

:::checklist Clean books
- Separate business bank and card accounts.
- Chart of accounts with no more than about 30 categories.
- Every transaction has a source document.
- Sales and payments recorded as separate events.
- All accounts reconciled monthly.
- Owner reviews statements, payees, and large payments.
:::

:::summary
Clean books are complete, accurate, timely, and supported. Choose cash or accrual with professional guidance, use a short chart of accounts, keep source documents as IRS guidance describes, and reconcile monthly. Basic internal controls—owner review of statements and payees, approval thresholds, and comparing payments with invoices—protect against error and fraud, as the North Carolina bookkeeper case shows.
:::

# Chapter 3: Reading the Income Statement

:::objectives
- Identify the major sections of an income statement.
- Calculate gross margin, operating margin, and net margin.
- Distinguish costs of goods sold from operating expenses.
- Use comparisons over time to spot trends.
:::

The income statement, also called the profit and loss statement or P&L, reports revenue earned and expenses incurred over a period. It answers the question "Did we make money?" and, when read carefully, also answers "How?" and "Where could we improve?"

## The structure

1. Revenue (sales): amounts earned from customers during the period.
2. Cost of goods sold (COGS) or cost of sales: costs that rise directly with each unit sold or job completed—materials, outsourced production, and direct labor.
3. Gross profit: revenue minus COGS.
4. Operating expenses: costs of running the business that do not vary directly with each sale—rent, software, insurance, marketing, administrative salaries, depreciation.
5. Operating income: gross profit minus operating expenses.
6. Other income and expense: interest earned, interest paid, one-time gains or losses.
7. Income before taxes, taxes (for corporations), and net income.

Owner draws in a sole proprietorship or partnership are not expenses; they are distributions of equity and appear on the balance sheet. Loan principal payments are not expenses either; only the interest portion is.

## Margins

Margins express profit as a percentage of revenue, which allows comparison across months of different size.

- Gross margin = gross profit divided by revenue.
- Operating margin = operating income divided by revenue.
- Net margin = net income divided by revenue.

Gross margin shows how much of each sales dollar is left to cover overhead and profit after the direct costs of production. A falling gross margin usually signals rising material or labor costs, discounting, or a shift toward lower-margin work. A falling operating margin with stable gross margin points to rising overhead.

## Reading framework

1. Start at the bottom line: profit or loss, and how it compares with last month and the same month last year.
2. Move to gross margin: did direct costs keep pace with revenue?
3. Scan operating expenses for any line that changed by more than 10 percent.
4. Separate one-time items from recurring ones.
5. Ask what caused each major change, and write down one action.

:::case Costco: thin merchandise margins and membership fees (real, cited)
Costco Wholesale's fiscal 2025 results illustrate how margin structure shapes a business model. For the 52 weeks ended August 31, 2025, Costco reported net sales of $269.9 billion and membership fees of $5.323 billion, for total revenue of $275.235 billion [8]. Merchandise costs were $239.886 billion and selling, general, and administrative expenses were $24.966 billion, producing operating income of $10.383 billion and net income of $8.099 billion [8]. Simple arithmetic on those reported figures shows that net sales minus merchandise costs left about $30.0 billion, a merchandise gross margin of roughly 11 percent, and that membership fees equaled about 51 percent of operating income. Costco does not allocate expenses to membership, so this is a comparison of two lines, not a profit split. The results are Costco's own. The lesson for a small owner is that the income statement reveals the model: a business can thrive on thin product margins only if volume, cost discipline, or a second revenue stream covers overhead.

# Chapter 4: Building the Balance Sheet

:::objectives
- Explain the accounting equation.
- Classify assets and liabilities as current or long-term.
- Calculate working capital and the current ratio.
- Connect the balance sheet to the income statement and cash.
:::

The balance sheet is a snapshot of what a business owns, what it owes, and what is left for the owner on a specific date. It rests on the accounting equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity

Every transaction keeps the equation in balance. Buying supplies with cash swaps one asset for another. Buying them on credit raises both assets and liabilities. Earning a profit raises equity; an owner draw lowers it.

## Classifications

Current assets are expected to become cash or be used within a year: cash, accounts receivable, inventory and supplies, prepaid expenses. Long-term assets include equipment, vehicles, and leasehold improvements, usually shown at cost less accumulated depreciation. Current liabilities are due within a year: accounts payable, credit card balances, sales tax and payroll taxes payable, customer deposits, and the current portion of loans. Long-term liabilities are due later, such as the remaining balance of an equipment loan. Owner's equity includes contributions, accumulated profits, and draws.

## Key measures

- Working capital = current assets minus current liabilities. It indicates the cushion available to run day-to-day operations.
- Current ratio = current assets divided by current liabilities. A ratio above 1.0 means current assets exceed current obligations; many lenders look for 1.2 or more, though norms vary by industry.
- Debt-to-equity = total liabilities divided by owner's equity, indicating how much the business relies on borrowed money.

## Building framework

1. List every bank balance as of the statement date (reconciled).
2. List unpaid customer invoices (accounts receivable).
3. Count or estimate supplies and inventory at cost.
4. List equipment at cost and subtract accumulated depreciation.
5. List every amount owed: vendors, cards, taxes collected but not yet paid, customer deposits, loans.
6. Calculate equity as assets minus liabilities and check that it agrees with last month's equity plus net income, plus contributions, minus draws.
7. Calculate working capital and the current ratio.

## Concentration and safety of cash

A balance sheet also reveals concentration: how much of the business's cash sits in one bank, how much of receivables are owed by one customer, and how much of the liabilities come due at once. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) explains that deposit insurance covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category [10]. Businesses that hold more than that amount may consider how their deposits are structured.

:::case Silicon Valley Bank: a balance sheet under stress (real, cited)
Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) was closed by California regulators on March 10, 2023. The Federal Reserve's review of its supervision found that the bank's board and management failed to manage their risks [9]. SVB's parent saw its assets grow 271 percent from year-end 2018 to year-end 2021, compared with 29 percent for the banking industry, and invested a large portion of client deposits in long-dated securities [9]. As of year-end 2022, approximately 94 percent of its deposits were uninsured, and many depositors were technology and venture-backed companies that were drawing down cash to fund operations as funding slowed [9]. When rising interest rates reduced the value of long-dated securities and depositors withdrew funds, the mismatch between assets and liabilities became fatal. SVB is a bank, not a small business, and the facts are from the Federal Reserve's review. The lessons transfer: match the timing of assets and obligations, watch concentration, and know which of your cash balances are insured.
:::

:::example Northside's April 30 balance sheet (illustrative)
| Assets | | Liabilities and equity | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | $6,100 | Accounts payable | $1,400 |
| Accounts receivable | $2,250 | Credit card | $900 |
| Supplies | $1,800 | Sales tax payable | $350 |
| Equipment deposit | $1,200 | Current portion of loan | $2,400 |

Equity check: March 31 equity was $15,150. Add April net income of $1,650 and subtract the $600 owner draw: $15,150 + $1,650 − $600 = $16,200. The balance sheet ties to the income statement.
:::

| Measure | Formula | Northside, April 30 | What it tells Andre |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working capital | Current assets − current liabilities | $6,300 | Cushion for operations |
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ current liabilities | 2.25 | Comfortable short-term position |
| Debt-to-equity | Total liabilities ÷ equity | 0.56 | Moderate reliance on debt |
| Receivables share of current assets | AR ÷ current assets | 19.8% | Collections matter |

:::checklist Balance sheet review
- All bank balances reconciled to the statement date.
- Receivables list matches unpaid invoices.
- Supplies counted or estimated at cost.
- Every liability listed, including taxes collected and customer deposits.
- Equity ties to last month plus net income, contributions, and draws.

- Cash concentration and insurance limits considered.
:::

# Chapter 5: Forecasting Cash and Timing

:::objectives
- Explain why profitable businesses run out of cash.
- Calculate cash buffer days.
- Build a thirteen-week cash forecast.
- Use timing levers to close gaps.
:::

Cash is the oxygen of a small business. A business can survive a loss for a while; it cannot survive an empty bank account when payroll, rent, or a tax payment is due. Cash problems usually come from timing: customers pay later than suppliers must be paid, large annual or quarterly bills arrive at once, or growth requires materials and labor before the revenue is collected.

## Cash buffer days

The JPMorgan Chase Institute studied the bank accounts of more than 45,000 small business customers and introduced a simple measure: cash buffer days, the number of days of cash outflows a business could pay from its cash balance if inflows stopped, estimated as average daily cash balance divided by average daily cash outflows [3]. It found that the typical small business carried only 27 cash buffer days, and the typical employer small business only 18 [3]. Payroll was a large and volatile outflow; the typical employer business had payroll outflows equal to 18 percent of all outflows [3].

## The thirteen-week forecast

A thirteen-week forecast covers one quarter week by week. It is short enough to be accurate and long enough to reveal problems while there is time to act.

1. Start with the reconciled bank balance.
2. List expected inflows by week: customer payments based on actual invoice dates and payment habits, deposits on new orders, and any loan proceeds.
3. List expected outflows by week: payroll, rent, supplier payments, card payments, loan payments, estimated taxes, sales tax remittances, owner draws, and planned purchases.
4. Calculate ending cash for each week.
5. Compare each week with a minimum cash target.
6. Update every week, replacing estimates with actuals.

## Timing levers

When the forecast shows a gap, the levers are about timing more than amounts:

- Collect faster: require deposits, invoice on delivery, offer card payment, follow up on day one past due.
- Pay on terms: pay suppliers when due, not early, unless there is a discount.
- Stage purchases: delay non-urgent equipment.
- Smooth large bills: set aside a weekly amount for quarterly taxes and annual insurance.
- Arrange credit before it is needed: a line of credit is easier to obtain when the business does not urgently need it.

:::case Cash buffer days across small businesses (real, cited)
The JPMorgan Chase Institute's 2017 report, The Ups and Downs of Small Business Employment, drew on a sample of over 45,000 small business customers [3]. Besides the 27-day typical cash buffer, it found that monthly payroll for the median employer small business grew at an annualized 8.5 percent, but month-to-month volatility around that growth was high, making cash management harder [3]. In its sample, 36.5 percent of employer firms experienced declining payroll outflows over a year [3]. These are research findings about other firms, not TTI results. They suggest that many small businesses operate with less than a month of cushion, and that a forecast is a practical defense against volatility that owners cannot control.
:::

# Chapter 6: Contribution and Break-Even

:::objectives
- Separate variable and fixed costs.
- Calculate contribution margin per unit and as a ratio.
- Calculate break-even volume and sales, including owner pay.
- Use contribution analysis to evaluate pricing and offers.
:::

Contribution margin answers a deceptively simple question: when I sell one more unit, how much money is left to cover fixed costs and profit? It is the most useful single concept for pricing, discounting, and deciding which products or services to emphasize.

## Variable and fixed costs

Variable costs change in proportion to volume: materials, outsourced production, direct labor paid by the job or hour, card processing fees, and shipping. Fixed costs stay roughly the same over a range of volume: rent, insurance, software, salaried staff, and loan payments. Some costs are mixed—a phone plan with a base fee and overage charges—and can be split into fixed and variable parts.

## Contribution margin

- Contribution margin per unit = price minus variable cost per unit.
- Contribution margin ratio = contribution margin per unit divided by price.

If contribution margin is negative, every sale loses money, and more volume makes things worse. No amount of growth can fix a negative contribution margin.

## Break-even

- Break-even units = fixed costs divided by contribution margin per unit.
- Break-even sales = fixed costs divided by contribution margin ratio.
- Target volume = (fixed costs plus target profit or owner pay) divided by contribution margin per unit.

Break-even analysis is only as good as its assumptions. Run it for a realistic range of prices and costs rather than as a single answer.

## Framework

1. Choose the unit: a label, a job, a customer-month, an hour.
2. List every cost that rises with one more unit.
3. Calculate contribution per unit and the ratio.
4. Total the monthly fixed costs.
5. Calculate break-even and target volumes.
6. Compare with realistic capacity and demand.
7. Test changes: a price increase, a cost reduction, a discount.

:::case MoviePass: growth with negative contribution (real, cited)
MoviePass offered moviegoers theater tickets through a subscription. In August 2017, after analytics firm Helios and Matheson acquired a controlling interest, it cut its monthly fee to under $10 for a plan that allowed one movie ticket per day [11]. According to The Verge, the company acted as an intermediary, buying tickets at full price and giving them to subscribers, and expected many subscribers to use the service rarely, as gym members do; instead, many used it often, and the company lost money on subscribers who watched frequently [11]. It amassed more than 1 million subscribers in the three months after introducing the plan, then cut benefits, shut down in September 2019, and its parent filed for bankruptcy [11]. The facts are drawn from published reporting; the outcome belongs to MoviePass and its parent. The lesson is arithmetic: when the variable cost of serving a customer exceeds the price, growth multiplies the loss.
:::

# Chapter 7: Pricing and Job Costing

:::objectives
- Build a price from direct costs, labor, overhead, and target profit.
- Calculate an overhead rate per labor hour.
- Prepare a job estimate and compare it with actual results.
- Use job-costing data to adjust prices and quoting.
:::

Pricing is where financial literacy pays off most directly. A price that ignores overhead, underestimates labor, or omits the owner's time may win work while slowly draining the business. Job costing—tracking the actual costs of each job and comparing them with the estimate—turns pricing from guesswork into a learning loop.

## The building blocks of a price

1. Direct materials: everything consumed by the job, at current replacement cost.
2. Direct labor: hours times the fully loaded hourly cost, including payroll taxes and benefits for employees.
3. Outsourced work: subcontracted printing, delivery, or installation.
4. Overhead: a share of fixed costs, allocated by labor hours, machine hours, or as a percentage of direct cost.
5. Profit: a deliberate margin above full cost, not whatever is left over.

## Overhead rate

A simple overhead rate divides monthly fixed costs by expected productive hours. If fixed costs are $3,150 a month and the shop expects 120 billable hours, the overhead rate is about $26.25 per billable hour. Each job then carries overhead in proportion to the hours it uses. Productive hours are almost always fewer than total hours; owners who assume 160 billable hours in a month usually underprice.

## Valuing the owner's time

Owners often leave their own time out of job costs. Entrepreneur Dan Martell recommends calculating a "buyback rate" from what you earn in a year—salary, or for an entrepreneur, roughly what you pay yourself plus profit and distributions—and converting it to an hourly figure to decide which tasks are worth delegating [12]. The same calculation gives an owner a floor for the value of an hour spent on a job. If the owner does the design work, that time belongs in the estimate.

## The job-costing framework

1. Estimate materials, labor hours, outsourced costs, and overhead for each job before quoting.
2. Add target profit to reach the price.
3. Open a job record when the order is accepted.
4. Track actual materials, hours, and outsourced costs against the job.
5. Close the job record when invoiced and compare estimate with actual.
6. Review variances monthly and adjust estimating standards.

:::case Dan Martell's buyback rate (real, cited)
Dan Martell is the founder of SaaS Academy, a coaching company for software CEOs, and author of Buy Back Your Time. In a 2023 Entrepreneur podcast interview, he described the buyback rate as starting with what an owner currently makes in a year—for an entrepreneur, what they pay themselves, profit, distributions, and certain expenses run through the business—and turning it into an hourly figure [12]. He argued that "we don't hire people to grow our business—we hire people to buy back time out of our calendar," so owners avoid "building a business we don't grow to hate" [12]. Martell's business results are his own. For pricing, the useful idea is that an owner's hour has a measurable value; leaving it out of job costs hides the true cost of work.
:::

# Chapter 8: Taxes and Payroll Records

:::objectives
- Explain self-employment tax and estimated tax obligations.
- Set aside money for taxes as income arrives.
- Describe payroll trust fund taxes and the risk of not paying them.
- Keep tax records in an orderly way.
:::

Taxes are a cash obligation that arrives on a schedule. Owners who treat tax money as available cash are often surprised by bills they cannot pay. This chapter is an orientation, not tax advice; rules depend on entity type, location, and circumstances, and a qualified professional should guide your decisions.

## Self-employment tax

Sole proprietors and partners generally pay self-employment tax on net earnings. The IRS explains that the self-employment tax rate is 15.3 percent, consisting of 12.4 percent for Social Security and 2.9 percent for Medicare; for 2026, the Social Security portion applies to the first $184,500 of combined wages, tips, and net earnings [14]. Self-employed individuals can deduct the employer-equivalent portion of self-employment tax when calculating adjusted gross income [14]. Self-employment tax is in addition to federal and state income taxes.

## Estimated taxes

Federal income tax is pay-as-you-go. The IRS explains that individuals, including sole proprietors, partners, and S corporation shareholders, generally must make estimated tax payments if they expect to owe $1,000 or more when their return is filed, and corporations if they expect to owe $500 or more; underpayment or late payment can result in a penalty [13]. Estimated payments are typically due four times a year; check current-year IRS dates.

## Payroll and trust fund taxes

When a business has employees, it withholds income tax and the employee's share of Social Security and Medicare from wages. The IRS calls these trust fund taxes because the employer holds the employee's money in trust until it is deposited [15]. If a business does not pay them, the IRS can assess the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty against any responsible person—someone with the duty and power to direct the collection and payment of these taxes—who willfully fails to pay; the penalty equals the unpaid trust fund tax, and the business does not have to have stopped operating for it to apply [15]. Using withheld payroll taxes to pay other bills is one of the most serious financial mistakes an owner can make.

## Framework for tax readiness

1. Ask a tax professional to estimate your annual tax obligations and set a percentage of profit to reserve.
2. Open a separate tax savings account.
3. Transfer the reserve percentage from each deposit or each week's profit.
4. Calendar estimated tax, sales tax, and payroll deposit dates.
5. Use a reputable payroll service if you have employees, and verify that deposits are made.
6. Keep returns, payroll reports, and supporting records organized by year.

:::case Marvin's Tow Service: unpaid payroll taxes (real, cited)
In February 2025, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Kansas announced that the owner of Marvin's Tow Service, Inc., in Gardner, Kansas, was sentenced to 17 months in prison for failing to forward more than one million dollars in employment tax collections to the IRS [16]. According to court documents, he failed to pay employment taxes for at least 23 calendar quarters between 2012 and 2017, and IRS agents were told by the company's office administrator that he would not allow her to pay the owed federal taxes [16]. He was ordered to pay $1,512,283 in restitution [16]. The facts are from the government's announcement. The case shows how payroll taxes accumulate quarter after quarter, and why the decision not to remit them carries personal consequences for the responsible owner.
:::

# Chapter 9: Debt and Financing Terms

:::objectives
- Distinguish common forms of small business financing.
- Calculate the true cost of a financing offer.
- Read key terms, including personal guarantees and repayment schedules.
- Match financing to the purpose and timing of the need.
:::

Debt is a tool. Used well, it smooths timing gaps or funds equipment that pays for itself. Used poorly, it turns a temporary problem into a permanent one. The Federal Reserve's 2025 Small Business Credit Survey found that 59 percent of firms with debt used a personal guarantee, and that 60 percent of borrowers from online lenders reported higher-than-expected borrowing costs, compared with 37 percent at small banks and 32 percent at large banks [2].

## Common financing types

- Business credit card: convenient for small purchases; high interest if balances are carried.
- Line of credit: borrow and repay as needed up to a limit; useful for timing gaps.
- Term loan: a fixed amount repaid over a set term; suited to equipment or long-lived investments.
- SBA-guaranteed loans: loans made by lenders with a partial government guarantee; the SBA describes several loan programs with different purposes and limits [20].
- Equipment financing: secured by the equipment itself.
- Merchant cash advance (MCA): an up-front sum in exchange for a larger amount repaid through daily or weekly automatic withdrawals or a share of card sales. MCAs are often structured as purchases of future receivables rather than loans, and their costs can be very high when expressed as an annual rate.

## Calculating true cost

Financing offers express cost in different ways: interest rates, factor rates, fees, and "total payback." To compare them, convert each to the total dollars repaid minus the dollars received, and consider how quickly repayment occurs. A factor rate of 1.3 on a $10,000 advance means $13,000 repaid—$3,000 of cost. If that is repaid in six months through daily withdrawals, the annualized cost is far higher than a 30 percent annual interest rate, because the average balance outstanding is small and falling.

## Key terms to read

1. Amount received after fees.
2. Total repayment amount.
3. Payment frequency and amount (daily, weekly, monthly).
4. Term and prepayment terms (is there a discount for early payoff?).
5. Collateral and liens (including blanket liens on all business assets).
6. Personal guarantee.
7. Default triggers and remedies.
8. Fees: origination, draw, late, and renewal.

## Matching purpose and timing

Short-term needs (a timing gap) are best met with short-term tools such as a line of credit; long-term investments (equipment) suit term loans. Using short-term, high-frequency repayment to fund long-term assets strains cash flow, because the repayment comes before the asset produces returns.

:::case Yellowstone Capital and the FTC (real, cited)
In April 2021, the Federal Trade Commission announced that Yellowstone Capital, a provider of merchant cash advances, would pay more than $9.8 million to settle charges that it took money from businesses' bank accounts without permission and deceived them about the amount of financing they would receive and other features of its products [17]. The FTC explained that merchant cash advances provide money up front in exchange for a larger amount repaid through daily automatic payments, and alleged that Yellowstone continued withdrawing money for days after businesses' balances had been repaid [17]. These are the FTC's allegations and the settlement terms; they are not a TTI result. The case underscores why owners should read the amount actually received, the repayment mechanism, and the payoff terms before signing, and should monitor withdrawals against the agreement.
:::

# Chapter 10: Owner Pay and Profit

:::objectives
- Distinguish owner pay, profit, and retained cash.
- Explain the Profit First allocation approach.
- Set allocation percentages based on current results.
- Build a routine for owner pay and reserves.
:::

Many small business owners pay themselves whatever is left at the end of the month—sometimes a great deal, often nothing. This "leftover" approach makes household finances unstable and hides whether the business is truly profitable after paying for the owner's labor. A deliberate system for owner pay, tax reserves, and profit treats these as obligations, not afterthoughts.

## Owner pay, profit, and retained cash

Owner pay compensates the owner for working in the business. Profit is the return on the owner's investment and risk, above and beyond pay. Retained cash funds reserves and growth. In a sole proprietorship, owner draws are not expenses for tax purposes, but for management purposes it is useful to treat a reasonable owner wage as a cost when judging whether the business works. Corporations and S corporations have specific rules about owner compensation; ask a tax professional.

## Profit First

Mike Michalowicz's Profit First system flips the usual formula. Instead of Sales minus Expenses equals Profit, it uses Sales minus Profit equals Expenses: a predetermined percentage of each deposit is taken as profit first, and only the remainder is available for expenses [18]. Michalowicz describes the method as working with owners' existing habits rather than against them. Many owners practice what he calls "bank balance accounting"—logging in, looking at the balance, and deciding what to do based on what they see—so Profit First pre-allocates money into separate accounts, so that every balance has a known purpose [23]. The foundational structure is five accounts—Income, Profit, Owner's Compensation, Tax, and Operating Expenses—with allocations twice a month (for example, on the 10th and 25th), starting with percentages the business can sustain, such as 1 percent to profit, and increasing them over time [24].

## Allocation framework

1. Calculate real revenue: revenue minus materials and subcontractor costs.
2. Determine current percentages: what share of real revenue currently goes to owner pay, taxes, operating expenses, and profit.
3. Choose starting target percentages modestly above current levels for profit and owner pay.
4. Open separate accounts and set allocation days (for example, the 10th and 25th).
5. On each allocation day, divide the income account balance by the percentages.
6. Pay yourself from the owner's pay account on a fixed schedule.
7. Review quarterly, and raise profit and owner pay targets by one or two points as efficiency improves.

:::case The Profit First method (real, cited)
Profit First was developed by entrepreneur and author Mike Michalowicz. On his website he writes that conventional accounting treats profit as a leftover, "something that is hopefully a nice surprise at the end of the year," and that "the profit is rarely there and the business continues on its check-to-check survival" [18]. He describes bank balance accounting candidly: "We don't have the time to do the complex stuff," so owners make collection calls when the balance is low and invest when it is high, yet "we never have enough money left to pay ourselves" [23]. His remedy is physical separation of money; when an account runs short, he advises owners to call their accountant and go through the statements and ratios [23]. The method is behavioral; it is not a substitute for accurate books or financial statements, and outcomes reported by users are their own, not TTI's. Used alongside the statements in this book, it gives owners a simple routine for honoring obligations they already know about.
:::

# Chapter 11: The Monthly Close

:::objectives
- Perform a monthly close in a consistent sequence.
- Confirm balances with independent evidence.
- Build a one-page financial dashboard.
- Turn the close into decisions.
:::

The monthly close is the routine that turns a month of transactions into reliable statements. Without it, the income statement and balance sheet drift from reality, errors accumulate, and fraud can go unnoticed. With it, the owner gets an accurate picture within days of month-end and a regular moment to decide.

## The close sequence

1. Record all sales, invoices, and payments received.
2. Record all bills and payments made.
3. Reconcile every bank, card, and loan account to its statement—confirming balances with documents from the bank or lender, not with internal records alone.
4. Review accounts receivable: follow up on overdue invoices.
5. Review accounts payable: confirm what is owed and when.
6. Record adjustments: depreciation, prepaid expenses, supplies used, and accrued liabilities.
7. Produce the income statement and balance sheet; check that equity ties.
8. Update the thirteen-week cash forecast.
9. Complete the dashboard and write down decisions.
10. Lock the month so past entries cannot be changed without a record.

## Independent confirmation

The most important principle of the close is that key balances are confirmed with evidence from outside the business: bank statements, lender statements, supplier statements, and customer confirmations. Internal records can be wrong by mistake or by design; independent evidence is much harder to falsify.

:::case Wirecard: cash that could not be confirmed (real, cited)
Wirecard, a German payments company that was once a stock market star, collapsed in June 2020 after its auditor, EY, refused to sign off on its 2019 accounts [19]. According to Reuters, EY was unable to confirm the existence of 1.9 billion euros in cash balances on trust accounts, representing around a quarter of the company's balance sheet; days later, Wirecard said the money likely never existed, and it filed for insolvency owing creditors almost $4 billion [19]. Earlier, an independent investigation by KPMG had found that the company did not provide sufficient documentation to address allegations of accounting irregularities [19]. The facts come from Reuters' timeline of public events. Wirecard was a large public company, but its failure turned on the same principle that governs a small business's monthly close: a cash balance is real only when it is confirmed by an independent statement.
:::

# Glossary

Accounting equation: Assets equal liabilities plus owner's equity.

Accounts payable: Amounts the business owes to suppliers.

Accounts receivable: Amounts customers owe the business.

Accrual method: Recording income when earned and expenses when incurred.

Balance sheet: A statement of assets, liabilities, and equity on a specific date.

Break-even: The volume or sales at which contribution equals fixed costs.

Cash buffer days: Average daily cash balance divided by average daily cash outflows.

Cash method: Recording income when received and expenses when paid.

Chart of accounts: The list of categories used to classify transactions.

Contribution margin: Price minus variable cost per unit.

Cost of goods sold: Direct costs of producing goods or delivering services.

Current ratio: Current assets divided by current liabilities.

Depreciation: Allocation of an asset's cost over its useful life.

Estimated tax: Periodic tax payments made during the year on income not subject to withholding.

Factor rate: A multiplier used in some financing to state total repayment, such as 1.3 times the amount advanced.

Gross margin: Gross profit divided by revenue.

Income statement: A statement of revenue and expenses for a period.

Internal controls: Practices that protect assets and ensure accurate records.

Job costing: Tracking actual costs of each job and comparing them with the estimate.

Merchant cash advance: Up-front funding repaid through automatic withdrawals or a share of future sales.

Operating margin: Operating income divided by revenue.

Overhead rate: Fixed costs divided by expected billable hours or another allocation base.

Owner's equity: The owner's residual interest in the business after liabilities.

Personal guarantee: A promise by the owner to repay a business debt personally if the business does not.

Real revenue: Revenue minus materials and subcontractor costs (a Profit First term).

Reconciliation: Comparing internal records with an outside statement and resolving differences.

Self-employment tax: Social Security and Medicare tax on net earnings from self-employment.

Trust fund taxes: Taxes withheld from employees and held by the employer until deposited.

Working capital: Current assets minus current liabilities.
