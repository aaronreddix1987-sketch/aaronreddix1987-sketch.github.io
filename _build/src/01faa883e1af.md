# How to Use This Book

This book is for working adults and small business owners who want more control over their financial lives: people who would like to reach a point where work is a choice rather than a necessity, or who simply want to stop feeling one surprise bill away from trouble. It assumes no prior training in finance. It does assume a willingness to collect your own numbers, do basic arithmetic, and revisit decisions as circumstances change.

The book's argument is that financial independence is better understood as a decision system than as a number. Popular writing often reduces the subject to a single target—"save twenty-five times your expenses"—as if one calculation settled the matter. In practice, the target depends on assumptions about spending, investment returns, inflation, taxes, health, and longevity, each of which is uncertain. A sound plan therefore works with ranges, makes its assumptions explicit, measures progress monthly, and includes rules for what to do when reality diverges from the plan.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this book you should be able to:

- Prepare a personal balance sheet and cash flow statement and interpret them.
- Build a spending system and an annual expense calendar that remove most financial surprises.
- Size reserves for different purposes and hold them in appropriate places.
- Compare debt repayment methods using both arithmetic and behavioral evidence.
- Explain diversification, risk, and the long-run effect of investment fees.
- Describe the main U.S. tax-advantaged account types and the 2026 contribution limits.
- Estimate a range for your financial independence target using published withdrawal-rate research, and understand that research's limits.
- Run a monthly review with explicit "stop rules" that trigger a reassessment.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, defines its key terms, explains the reasoning behind them, and presents a step-by-step framework. Every chapter includes at least one worked example with real arithmetic, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Where helpful, chapters include case studies drawn from published research and public sources, such as the Federal Reserve's household surveys, peer-reviewed studies on saving and debt, and the history of index investing.

The worked examples follow a fictional saver, Jordan, a 34-year-old with a salaried job and a small weekend side business. Jordan is illustrative only and is not a TTI customer; the numbers are not predictions. The people and organizations described in the case studies are real, and their results are their own, not TTI's.

:::callout A note on advice and evidence
This book is educational. It is not individualized investment, tax, legal, or insurance advice, and it promises no return, income, or outcome. Investments can lose value. Tax rules and contribution limits change every year; figures cited here were checked against official sources as of November 2026. Before acting on any decision with significant consequences, consult a qualified professional who is obligated to act in your interest, and verify current rules with the relevant agency.
:::

# Chapter 1: What Financial Independence Means

:::objectives
- Define financial independence, financial security, and financial freedom as distinct milestones.
- Explain why a financial independence target is a range, not a point.
- Identify the four variables that drive any financial independence plan.
- Write a personal purpose statement that guides financial trade-offs.
:::

Financial independence is commonly defined as the point at which income from your assets—investments, rental property, a business you no longer need to operate daily—can reliably cover your living expenses without your needing to work for pay. The definition is simple; applying it is not. Each word in it hides an assumption. "Reliably" implies a judgment about risk. "Living expenses" implies a choice about lifestyle. "Without needing to work" implies a time horizon that may be thirty or fifty years.

## Three milestones

It helps to separate the journey into milestones, because the distant goal can feel abstract and the intermediate ones are where most of the practical work happens.

Financial security is the first milestone: you have reserves to absorb ordinary shocks—a car repair, a month without income, a medical bill—without new high-interest debt. Many households have not yet reached this point. The Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking found that 63 percent of adults said they would cover a hypothetical $400 emergency expense exclusively with cash or its equivalent, meaning that a substantial minority would need to borrow, sell something, or could not pay at all [1].

Financial flexibility is the second: you carry no high-interest consumer debt, you save a meaningful share of income automatically, and you could change jobs or take a pay cut without hardship.

Financial independence is the third: your assets can plausibly fund your spending for the rest of your life under a range of reasonable assumptions.

## The four driving variables

Every financial independence plan, however sophisticated, rests on four variables:

1. Spending: How much you need each year, now and in the future.
2. Saving rate: The share of income you set aside, which determines how quickly assets accumulate.
3. Return: What your assets earn after fees, taxes, and inflation, which is uncertain.
4. Time: How long you will accumulate and how long you will draw down.

Spending matters twice. Lower spending both raises the amount you can save and lowers the amount you will need. This double effect is why many financial independence writers emphasize spending decisions above investment selection.

| Variable | You control | Partly control | Do not control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spending | Choices, habits | Housing, transport costs | Prices, inflation |
| Saving rate | Automation, priorities | Income growth | Job market |
| Return | Fees, diversification, behavior | Asset allocation | Market returns |
| Time | Start date | Retirement date | Health, longevity |

## Why the target is a range

Suppose you spend $40,000 a year. A widely cited guideline derived from William Bengen's 1994 study suggests that an initial withdrawal of about 4 percent of a portfolio, adjusted for inflation thereafter, would have lasted at least 30 years in every historical U.S. period he examined [2]. Dividing $40,000 by 0.04 yields $1,000,000. But if you assume a more conservative 3.5 percent withdrawal rate, the target rises to about $1,143,000; if you allow 4.5 percent, it falls to about $889,000. The choice of assumption moves the target by more than $250,000. Chapter 9 examines this research and its limitations in detail. For now, the point is that honest planning reports a range and the assumptions behind it.

:::example Jordan's first estimate (illustrative)
Jordan spends about $46,000 a year. Using withdrawal rates of 3.5, 4.0, and 4.5 percent, Jordan's target range is:

- 46,000 ÷ 0.035 ≈ $1,314,000 (conservative)
- 46,000 ÷ 0.040 = $1,150,000 (central)
- 46,000 ÷ 0.045 ≈ $1,022,000 (less conservative)

Jordan also notes that Social Security benefits, if available later in life, would reduce the amount the portfolio must cover after the age benefits begin, and that health insurance costs before Medicare eligibility might raise spending. Both adjustments are addressed in later chapters. Jordan writes the range, not a single number, at the top of the plan.
:::

## A purpose statement

Numbers do not motivate sustained behavior on their own. Vicki Robin and Joe Dominguez's Your Money or Your Life, an influential text in this field, frames money as something you trade your "life energy" for, and asks readers to evaluate spending by whether it brings fulfillment proportional to the hours of work it cost [3]. Whether or not you adopt their full program, the underlying exercise is useful: write down what financial independence would allow you to do or stop doing. That statement becomes the tiebreaker when a spending decision is close.

# Chapter 2: Measuring Your Starting Position

:::objectives
- Prepare a personal balance sheet (net worth statement).
- Prepare a personal cash flow statement from three months of records.
- Calculate saving rate, liquidity ratio, and debt-to-income ratio.
- Interpret these figures without judgment and use them to set priorities.
:::

You cannot plan a route without knowing where you are. The starting position is captured in two statements borrowed from business accounting. The balance sheet shows what you own and owe at a moment in time. The cash flow statement shows what came in and went out over a period. Together they reveal both your stock of wealth and the flow that is changing it.

## The personal balance sheet

Assets are things of value you own: cash, checking and savings balances, investment and retirement accounts, the market value of a home or vehicle, and the value of a business you own. Liabilities are what you owe: credit card balances, student loans, auto loans, mortgages, medical debt, and personal loans. Net worth equals assets minus liabilities.

Value assets conservatively. A car is worth what it would sell for today, not what you paid. A business is worth what a buyer would plausibly pay, which for a very small owner-dependent business may be little. Retirement accounts may be listed at their balance, with a note that taxes and possible penalties would apply to withdrawals.

## The personal cash flow statement

Gather three months of bank and card statements. Record income after taxes and deductions (take-home pay), and categorize every outflow. Three months smooths out timing quirks and reveals irregular spending that a single month hides. Then divide each total by three to get monthly averages.

## Three diagnostic ratios

Saving rate is the share of income not spent. One common definition is (take-home pay + retirement contributions taken from pay − spending) ÷ (take-home pay + retirement contributions). It is the single best predictor of how quickly you can progress.

Liquidity ratio is liquid reserves divided by monthly essential spending. It tells you how many months you could cover essentials without income.

Debt-to-income ratio is total monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. Lenders use it to judge borrowing capacity; you can use it to judge how much of your income is already committed.

:::example Jordan's starting statements (illustrative)
Balance sheet. Assets: checking $2,400; savings $3,100; workplace 401(k) $28,500; car (resale) $9,000; side-business equipment $1,200. Total assets $44,200. Liabilities: credit card A $4,800 at 24.9%; credit card B $1,300 at 19.9%; car loan $7,600 at 6.4%; student loan $18,900 at 5.0%. Total liabilities $32,600. Net worth: $11,600.

Cash flow (monthly averages over three months). Take-home pay $3,850 plus side-business profit $450, for $4,300 of spendable income. Payroll 401(k) contribution $310. Spending $3,830, including $410 of minimum debt payments on cards and $295 on the car loan and $200 on the student loan.

Saving rate: (4,300 + 310 − 3,830) ÷ (4,300 + 310) = 780 ÷ 4,610 ≈ 16.9%.
Essential monthly spending (housing, utilities, groceries, insurance, transport, minimum debt payments): $2,950. Liquidity ratio: 3,100 ÷ 2,950 ≈ 1.05 months.
Gross monthly income: about $5,170 from salary plus $450 side income, $5,620. Debt payments $905. Debt-to-income: 905 ÷ 5,620 ≈ 16%.

Interpretation: Jordan's saving rate is respectable, but reserves cover only about one month of essentials, and $6,100 of credit card debt carries rates near 20 to 25 percent. These two facts, not investment selection, should set the next priorities.
:::

| Ratio | Jordan | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Saving rate | 16.9% | Solid base; room to raise with automation |
| Liquidity ratio | 1.05 months | Reserves thin; build to first target |
| Debt-to-income | 16% | Moderate; high-rate cards are the concern |
| Net worth | $11,600 | Positive; mostly in retirement account |

## Interpreting without judgment

Students often find this exercise uncomfortable. Numbers that look worse than expected can trigger avoidance. It helps to treat the statements as a physician treats a blood test: information that guides the next decision, not a verdict on character. The point of measuring is to choose a sequence of actions. For most people, that sequence begins with a small reserve, proceeds to eliminating high-interest debt, and then builds larger reserves and long-term investments.

# Chapter 3: Spending Systems and the Expense Calendar

:::objectives
- Classify expenses as fixed, variable, irregular, and discretionary.
- Build an annual expense calendar that converts irregular costs into predictable monthly amounts.
- Choose a spending system suited to your temperament.
- Use value-based review to reduce spending without deprivation.
:::

Most financial surprises are not surprises. Car registration, insurance premiums, holiday gifts, annual software renewals, and dental cleanings arrive on a predictable schedule; they feel like emergencies only because they were not planned. This chapter converts that unpredictability into a system.

## Four kinds of expense

Fixed expenses are the same amount every month: rent or mortgage, loan payments, many subscriptions. Variable essentials recur monthly but fluctuate: groceries, utilities, fuel. Irregular expenses occur less than monthly but predictably: annual insurance, vehicle maintenance, gifts, professional dues, property taxes. Discretionary expenses are those you could stop without harm: dining out, entertainment, most shopping.

The category that wrecks budgets is irregular. A household that budgets carefully for monthly items and ignores irregular ones will experience several "bad months" each year and may conclude, wrongly, that budgeting does not work.

## The annual expense calendar

1. List every irregular expense you paid in the past twelve months, using bank and card statements.
2. Add expected items not in that history, such as a planned trip or a car that will need tires.
3. Record the month each falls due and its approximate amount.
4. Total the year and divide by twelve. That is your monthly sinking-fund contribution.
5. Transfer that amount every month to a dedicated savings account, and pay irregular expenses from it.

A sinking fund is money set aside in advance for a known future expense. It differs from an emergency reserve, which is for unknown expenses.

:::example Jordan's expense calendar (illustrative)
| Month | Item | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| January | Car registration | $240 |
| March | Tax preparation | $180 |
| April | Side-business software renewal | $120 |
| May | Car insurance (6-month premium) | $610 |
| June | Dental cleaning (after insurance) | $90 |
| August | Car maintenance | $450 |
| November | Car insurance (6-month premium) | $610 |
| December | Holiday gifts | $700 |
| Throughout | Clothing, household replacement | $600 |
| Total | | $3,600 |

Monthly sinking-fund contribution: 3,600 ÷ 12 = $300. Jordan's previous budget had no line for these items. Once the $300 transfer was automated, the "unexpected" months disappeared, and Jordan found that real discretionary spending was about $300 a month lower than it had felt.
:::

## Choosing a spending system

No single method suits everyone. The best system is the one you will follow.

| System | How it works | Suits people who | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero-based budget | Every dollar assigned a job each month | Like detail and control | Time-consuming; fatigue |
| Percentage framework | Fixed shares for needs, wants, savings | Want simplicity | Shares may not fit high-cost areas |
| Pay-yourself-first | Savings automated; spend the rest freely | Dislike tracking | Requires reliable irregular-expense fund |
| Envelope or category caps | Hard caps on a few problem categories | Overspend in specific areas | Can feel restrictive |

Many people combine approaches: automate savings and sinking funds first, then cap the one or two discretionary categories that tend to grow.

## Value-based review

Cutting spending through willpower alone tends to fail. A more durable approach asks of each category: does this spending deliver value proportional to its cost in hours of work? Robin and Dominguez's method converts spending into hours of "life energy" by dividing each expense by your real hourly wage, computed after deducting work-related costs such as commuting [3]. Seen this way, a $15-a-month subscription you rarely use may cost an hour of work every month. The goal is not minimal spending; it is spending that reflects what you value.

:::checklist Spending system setup
- I have listed every irregular expense from the past year.
- My annual expense calendar includes known future items.
- A monthly sinking-fund transfer is automated.
- I have chosen one primary spending system.
- I have capped no more than two problem categories.
- I have reviewed subscriptions and cancelled those I would not buy again today.
:::

:::summary
Expenses are fixed, variable, irregular, or discretionary; irregular expenses cause most budget failures. An annual expense calendar and a monthly sinking-fund transfer convert them into predictable amounts. Choose a spending system that fits your temperament and combine methods if useful. Value-based review, including converting costs into hours of work, reduces spending more durably than willpower.
:::

# Chapter 4: Reserves by Purpose and Liquidity

:::objectives
- Distinguish emergency reserves, sinking funds, opportunity funds, and long-term investments.
- Size an emergency reserve using essential spending and income stability.
- Match each reserve to an account type based on liquidity, safety, and return.
- Understand deposit insurance limits.
:::

A reserve is money held for a purpose. Treating all savings as a single pool invites two errors: spending emergency money on wants, and holding long-term money in low-return cash. Assigning each dollar to a purpose, and each purpose to an appropriate account, prevents both.

## Four kinds of reserve

| Reserve | Purpose | Time horizon | Typical home |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starter emergency fund | Small shocks, avoid new card debt | Immediate | Savings account |
| Full emergency fund | Job loss, major repair, medical | 0–12 months | High-yield savings, money market |
| Sinking funds | Known irregular expenses | 1–24 months | Separate savings account(s) |
| Opportunity fund | Career move, business, education | 1–5 years | Savings, CDs, Treasury bills |
| Long-term investments | Financial independence | 5+ years | Diversified portfolio in tax-advantaged accounts |

## Sizing the emergency reserve

Common guidance suggests three to six months of essential expenses, but the right size depends on income stability. A tenured employee in a dual-income household may need less; a single-income household, a commission salesperson, or a self-employed person typically needs more. One structured approach:

1. Calculate essential monthly spending (housing, utilities, food, insurance, transport, minimum debt payments).
2. Assign a stability score: stable salary with second household income (3 months), single stable salary (4–6 months), variable or self-employment income (6–12 months).
3. Multiply essential spending by the number of months.
4. Build first to a starter amount—often one month of essentials or $1,000 to $2,000—before aggressive debt repayment, then to the full target after high-interest debt is gone.

:::example Jordan's reserve plan (illustrative)
Jordan's essential spending is $2,950 a month. With a single salary and some side income, Jordan chooses five months as the full target: 5 × 2,950 = $14,750.

Starter target: one month, $2,950. Jordan already has $3,100 in savings, so the starter fund is met. Jordan decides to keep that amount untouched, direct extra cash flow to the credit cards (Chapter 5), and resume building the reserve to $14,750 once the cards are paid.

Jordan also opens a separate sinking-fund account for the $300 monthly irregular-expense transfer, so that those funds are not mistaken for emergency money.
:::

## Matching reserves to accounts

Three properties matter: liquidity (how quickly you can access funds without loss), safety (the risk of losing principal), and return (interest earned). Emergency reserves prioritize liquidity and safety. Longer-horizon reserves can accept some loss of liquidity for higher yield; Treasury bills and certificates of deposit are common choices. Money needed within a few years generally should not be in the stock market, because stock prices can fall substantially over short periods.

## Deposit insurance

Deposits at FDIC-insured banks are protected up to the standard maximum deposit insurance amount of $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category [4]. Credit union deposits are similarly insured by the National Credit Union Administration. Confirm that any institution you use is insured, and understand that investment products such as stocks, bonds, and mutual funds are not deposits and are not FDIC-insured, even when purchased through a bank.

:::case What the Federal Reserve's survey reveals about reserves
The Federal Reserve's annual household survey reports that 63 percent of adults in 2025 would cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or its equivalent, unchanged from the previous three years and below the 2021 high of 68 percent [1]. The same report found that 59 percent of adults had at least one major, unexpected expense in the prior 12 months; the most common were a major vehicle repair or replacement (30 percent of adults), a major house or appliance repair (22 percent), and unexpected major medical expenses (21 percent) [1]. For the individual planner, the lesson is twofold. First, "unexpected" expenses are, in aggregate, close to a certainty: more adults experienced one than did not. Second, the most common shocks—cars, homes, health—are categories you can partly anticipate with sinking funds, leaving the emergency reserve for genuinely unpredictable events such as job loss.
:::

# Chapter 5: Debt Repayment: Arithmetic and Behavior

:::objectives
- Calculate the true cost of carrying a balance at a given interest rate.
- Compare the avalanche and snowball repayment methods.
- Explain what research on debt repayment behavior has found.
- Evaluate refinancing, consolidation, and balance-transfer options cautiously.
- Build a repayment plan with a target payoff date.
:::

Debt is not inherently bad. A fixed-rate mortgage on a home you can afford, or a modest student loan that financed a marketable skill, may be reasonable. High-interest consumer debt is different: at rates near 20 to 25 percent, few investments can reliably earn more than the balance costs. Paying down such debt is, in effect, a guaranteed after-tax return equal to the interest rate.

## The cost of carrying a balance

Interest on credit cards is usually expressed as an annual percentage rate (APR) and charged monthly at roughly one-twelfth of that rate on the average daily balance. A $4,800 balance at 24.9 percent costs about $100 in interest in a single month (4,800 × 0.249 ÷ 12 ≈ $99.60). If the minimum payment is about $140, only about $40 reduces the principal. At that pace, repayment can take many years and cost thousands of dollars in interest. Federal law requires card statements to show how long it would take to pay off the balance making only minimum payments, and that disclosure is worth reading.

## Two repayment methods

Both methods start the same way: pay the minimum on every debt, then direct every extra dollar to one target debt. When that debt is gone, its payment "rolls" to the next target. They differ only in the order.

The avalanche method targets the highest interest rate first. Mathematically, it minimizes total interest paid.

The snowball method targets the smallest balance first, regardless of rate. It produces early victories—accounts closed—which can sustain motivation.

| Feature | Avalanche | Snowball |
|---|---|---|
| Order | Highest rate first | Smallest balance first |
| Total interest | Lowest | Usually somewhat higher |
| Early accounts closed | Possibly slower | Faster |
| Best for | Disciplined, numbers-motivated people | People who need visible progress |
| Risk | Abandoning plan before first win | Paying more interest on large high-rate debt |

:::case Research on paying off small debts first
Researchers David Gal and Blakeley McShane analyzed data from roughly 6,000 consumers enrolled in a debt-settlement program to ask whether the pattern of repayment predicted who eliminated their debts. They found that closing individual accounts—paying off debts entirely—was associated with a higher likelihood of eventually eliminating all debt, even controlling for the dollar amount repaid [5]. In plain terms, completing small goals appeared to help people persist. The authors and commentators caution that the snowball approach can cost more in interest, so the finding is best read as evidence that motivation matters, not as proof that the snowball method is superior for everyone [6]. Note that this was an observational study of a specific population, not a randomized experiment; the results are the researchers' own and not TTI's.
:::

A practical compromise: if your smallest balance can be paid off within two or three months, clear it first for the motivational benefit, then switch to the avalanche for the remaining debts.

:::example Jordan's repayment plan (illustrative)
Jordan's debts: card A $4,800 at 24.9% (minimum $140); card B $1,300 at 19.9% (minimum $45); car loan $7,600 at 6.4% ($295); student loan $18,900 at 5.0% ($200). By pausing extra reserve building and trimming two discretionary categories, Jordan frees $550 a month above minimums.

Card B is small enough to clear quickly, so Jordan uses the compromise approach. Month 1–2: $595 a month to card B (550 extra plus 45 minimum); it is paid off in about 2.3 months. Then $735 a month (550 + 45 + 140) goes to card A. Card A's balance after three months of minimum payments is about $4,680; at $735 a month it is paid off in roughly seven more months.

Result: both cards are cleared in about ten months. After that, the $735 can be split between rebuilding the emergency reserve toward $14,750 and raising retirement contributions. The car and student loans, at 6.4 and 5.0 percent, are paid on schedule; whether to prepay them is a closer judgment considered below.
:::

## Lower-rate debts: prepay or invest?

Once high-rate debt is gone, the question becomes whether to prepay moderate-rate loans or invest. Prepaying a 5 percent loan yields a certain 5 percent return; investing offers a higher expected return with risk. Considerations include whether you have an employer retirement match (usually take it first, because it is an immediate return), your emotional comfort with debt, the tax treatment of the interest, and the loan's protections (federal student loans, for example, may offer income-driven repayment options that private loans do not). There is no single correct answer; many people split extra cash between the two.

## Refinancing, consolidation, and balance transfers

A lower rate can accelerate repayment, but each option has pitfalls:

- Balance transfers offer a low introductory rate for a limited period, usually with a transfer fee. They help only if you can pay off the balance before the promotional period ends and stop adding new charges.
- Consolidation loans replace several debts with one fixed-rate loan. They help if the rate is genuinely lower and you do not run up the cleared cards again.
- Refinancing federal student loans into private loans can lower the rate but may forfeit federal protections.
- Debt-settlement companies may charge substantial fees and can damage credit; research them carefully and be wary of up-front fees.

:::checklist Debt plan
- I have listed every debt with balance, rate, and minimum payment.
- I know how much extra I can pay each month.
- I have chosen avalanche, snowball, or a compromise, and written why.
- I have set up automatic minimum payments on every account.
- I have a projected payoff date for each high-interest debt.
- I have stopped adding new charges to target cards.
- Any balance transfer has a payoff plan before the promotion ends.
:::

:::summary
High-interest debt usually deserves priority because repaying it earns a certain return equal to its rate. The avalanche method minimizes interest; the snowball method produces faster visible progress, and research on debt-settlement clients suggests closing accounts is associated with persistence. A compromise—clear a very small balance first, then switch to avalanche—captures much of both benefits. Evaluate refinancing and transfers by total cost and by the protections you may lose.
:::

# Chapter 6: Behavioral Design: Automation and Commitment

:::objectives
- Explain present bias and inertia and how they affect saving.
- Describe the Save More Tomorrow program and its reported results.
- Design automatic transfers that implement your plan by default.
- Use commitment devices and friction to protect long-term goals.
:::

Most people know they should save more; fewer do. Behavioral economics, the study of how real people make economic decisions, offers an explanation and a set of remedies. The explanation is that human decision-making is shaped by predictable tendencies. The remedy is to design your financial environment so that the right action happens by default.

## Key concepts

Present bias is the tendency to weight immediate rewards much more heavily than future ones. A dollar spent today feels more valuable than two dollars available at retirement.

Inertia, or status quo bias, is the tendency to stay with the current arrangement. If you are not enrolled in a retirement plan, you tend to stay unenrolled; if you are enrolled, you tend to stay enrolled.

Loss aversion is the tendency to feel losses more strongly than equivalent gains. A visible cut in take-home pay feels painful even when it funds a goal you value.

Mental accounting is the habit of treating money differently depending on its label or source—for example, spending a tax refund freely while carefully budgeting salary.

These tendencies are not failures of intelligence. They are features of human cognition that can be worked with rather than against.

:::case Save More Tomorrow
Economists Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi designed a program called Save More Tomorrow (SMarT) that used these tendencies in savers' favor. Employees were invited to commit in advance to allocate a portion of future pay raises to retirement saving. Because increases came out of raises, take-home pay never fell, sidestepping loss aversion; because the commitment was made in advance, present bias mattered less; and because participants stayed in the program unless they opted out, inertia worked for them.

In the first implementation, reported in the Journal of Political Economy in 2004, 78 percent of employees offered the plan joined, 80 percent of those remained through the fourth annual raise, and participants' average saving rate rose from 3.5 percent to 13.6 percent over 40 months [7]. A review by the U.S. Department of Labor's Clearinghouse for Labor Evaluation and Research notes that the study was not a randomized trial, so the results cannot be attributed to the program with full confidence [8]. The design principles—automatic increases timed to raises—have since been widely adopted in workplace plans. These results are the researchers' own and not TTI's.
:::

## Designing your automatic system

1. Schedule transfers for the day after each paycheck arrives, so money moves before it can be spent.
2. Order the transfers: retirement contributions through payroll; sinking fund; emergency reserve or extra debt payments; other goals.
3. Set an automatic escalation: raise your retirement contribution by one percentage point each year, or commit a fixed share—such as half—of every raise to saving.
4. Separate accounts by purpose and name them ("Car and insurance fund," "Emergency—do not touch"), using mental accounting to your advantage.
5. Add friction to spending temptations: remove saved card numbers from shopping sites, unsubscribe from promotional emails, and hold the emergency fund at a different bank from checking.
6. Review the system quarterly, not daily. Frequent checking invites tinkering.

:::example Jordan's automated pay-day sequence (illustrative)
Jordan is paid twice a month, about $1,925 each time after taxes and the 401(k) deduction. On the day after each paycheck:

| Transfer | Amount per paycheck | Monthly |
|---|---|---|
| Sinking fund | $150 | $300 |
| Extra debt payment (card target) | $275 | $550 |
| Remaining for bills and spending | $1,500 | $3,000 |

Jordan also enrolls in the employer plan's automatic escalation feature, raising the 401(k) contribution from 6 to 7 percent next January, and commits to directing half of any raise to saving. On a 3 percent raise on a $62,000 salary—about $1,860 a year—half, $930, goes to saving, and take-home pay still rises.
:::

## Commitment devices

A commitment device is an arrangement that makes it harder to abandon a goal. Examples include automatic escalation, accounts with withdrawal restrictions, public commitments to a friend or partner, and pre-scheduled reviews. Use them selectively: a device that makes emergencies harder to handle can backfire. Restrictive accounts suit long-term goals; reserves must remain liquid.

:::checklist Automation and commitment
- Retirement contributions come directly from payroll.
- Transfers run the day after payday.
- Each savings account is named for its purpose.
- Automatic escalation or a raise-sharing rule is in place.
- At least two spending frictions are active.
- A quarterly review date is on my calendar.
:::

:::summary
Present bias, inertia, loss aversion, and mental accounting shape financial behavior predictably. Save More Tomorrow used these tendencies by committing future raises to saving, and its first implementation reported large increases in saving rates, though it was not randomized. Automate transfers immediately after payday, name accounts by purpose, escalate contributions with raises, and add friction to temptations.
:::

# Chapter 7: Investing Fundamentals: Risk, Diversification, and Cost

:::objectives
- Define the main asset classes and their typical risk and return characteristics.
- Explain diversification and why it reduces risk without necessarily reducing expected return.
- Quantify the long-term effect of investment fees.
- Describe the origin and logic of index funds.
- Choose an asset allocation approach appropriate to time horizon and risk tolerance.
:::

Investing is the use of money to acquire assets expected to produce income or appreciate over time. For most people pursuing financial independence, the decisive questions are not which stock to buy, but how to divide money among broad asset classes, how to keep costs low, and how to behave sensibly when markets fall.

## Asset classes

Stocks (equities) represent ownership in companies. They have historically offered higher long-run returns than bonds or cash, with substantially greater short-run volatility, including declines of a third or more in severe markets.

Bonds (fixed income) are loans to governments or corporations that pay interest. They are generally less volatile than stocks but can lose value, especially when interest rates rise or the issuer's credit weakens.

Cash and cash equivalents—savings accounts, money market funds, Treasury bills—offer stability and liquidity with returns that may not keep pace with inflation over long periods.

Real estate and other assets can add diversification but often involve leverage, concentration, and management responsibilities.

## Risk and diversification

In finance, risk usually means the variability of returns: the chance that results will differ from expectations, including losses. Diversification spreads money across many investments whose prices do not move in lockstep, so that the poor performance of one is offset by others. A fund holding thousands of companies eliminates most company-specific risk—the risk that one firm fails—while leaving market risk, which cannot be diversified away. Because company-specific risk is not reliably rewarded, eliminating it improves the trade-off between risk and expected return.

## The arithmetic of cost

Costs reduce returns dollar for dollar, and their effect compounds. The SEC's investor education office illustrates the point with a $100,000 portfolio earning 4 percent annually for 20 years: with ongoing fees of 1 percent a year, the investor ends with nearly $30,000 less than with fees of 0.25 percent [9]. A former SEC director summarized the lesson plainly: small differences in fees can have a large effect on long-term savings [10].

:::example Fees on Jordan's portfolio (illustrative)
Jordan has $28,500 in a 401(k) and expects to contribute about $6,000 a year for 25 years. Assume, for illustration only, a 6 percent annual return before fees.

At a total fee of 0.10 percent (net 5.9%), the future value is approximately:
28,500 × 1.059^25 + 6,000 × [(1.059^25 − 1) ÷ 0.059] ≈ 28,500 × 4.19 + 6,000 × 54.1 ≈ $119,000 + $325,000 = $444,000.

At a total fee of 1.0 percent (net 5.0%):
28,500 × 1.05^25 + 6,000 × [(1.05^25 − 1) ÷ 0.05] ≈ 28,500 × 3.39 + 6,000 × 47.7 ≈ $97,000 + $286,000 = $383,000.

The difference, roughly $61,000, comes entirely from fees. The assumed return is not a forecast; the point is the gap between the two lines, which persists under any positive return assumption.
:::

| Fee level | Typical product | Annual cost on $100,000 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.03%–0.20% | Broad index funds and ETFs | $30–$200 |
| 0.50%–1.00% | Many actively managed funds | $500–$1,000 |
| 1.00% plus fund fees | Advisory account with funds inside | $1,000–$2,000 |
| Up-front loads or surrender charges | Some commission products | Varies; read disclosures |

## Index funds

An index fund is a mutual fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) that seeks to match the return of a market index, such as a broad U.S. stock index, by holding the securities in that index rather than trying to pick winners. Because it does not pay for research-intensive stock selection and trades infrequently, it can operate at very low cost.

:::case "Bogle's Folly" and the first index fund
On August 31, 1976, Vanguard, led by John C. Bogle, launched the First Index Investment Trust, a fund designed to track the S&P 500 index. Its underwriting aimed to raise $150 million and raised only $11.3 million; critics derided it as "Bogle's Folly" [11]. Bogle drew on academic work, including Paul Samuelson's call for a low-cost index fund, arguing that investors as a group cannot outperform the market before costs and therefore must underperform it after costs [12]. Index investing has since grown into one of the dominant approaches for individual and institutional investors. The case illustrates two points relevant to individual planners: low cost is a durable advantage, and a sound idea may take years to gain acceptance. Vanguard's history and Bogle's results are their own and not TTI's.
:::

## Choosing an allocation

Asset allocation—the division of a portfolio among stocks, bonds, and cash—is the primary driver of a portfolio's risk. A rough framework:

1. Separate money by time horizon: funds needed within five years belong mostly in reserves, not stocks.
2. For long-term money, choose a stock-bond mix you could hold through a decline of 30 to 50 percent in the stock portion without selling.
3. Consider a target-date fund, which holds a diversified mix and becomes more conservative over time, if you prefer a single, automatic choice.
4. Rebalance periodically—annually, or when allocations drift more than five percentage points—to keep risk at the intended level.
5. Write the allocation and your reasons in an investment policy statement (Appendix D) so that you can refer to it during market stress.

:::checklist Investment basics
- Money needed within five years is held outside stocks.
- I know the total annual fee (expense ratio plus any advisory fee) on every account.
- My long-term holdings are broadly diversified.
- My stock-bond mix is one I could hold through a large decline.
- I have a rebalancing rule.
- My allocation and reasons are written down.
:::

:::summary
Stocks, bonds, and cash have distinct risk and return profiles. Diversification eliminates company-specific risk without sacrificing expected return. Fees compound; on long horizons, a difference of less than one percentage point can cost tens of thousands of dollars. Index funds, pioneered for individual investors by Vanguard in 1976, offer diversified exposure at low cost. Set an allocation by time horizon and tolerance, write it down, and rebalance by rule.
:::

# Chapter 8: Tax-Advantaged Accounts

:::objectives
- Explain the difference between tax-deferred (traditional) and tax-free (Roth) treatment.
- State the 2026 contribution limits for 401(k) plans, IRAs, and health savings accounts.
- Prioritize contributions across account types.
- Identify account options for self-employed people.
:::

Taxes are among the largest costs an investor faces. U.S. law offers several account types that reduce or defer taxes on savings, usually in exchange for restrictions on withdrawals. Using them well can significantly accelerate progress toward financial independence. Because rules are detailed and change annually, verify current limits and eligibility with the IRS or a tax professional before acting.

## Traditional versus Roth

In a traditional (tax-deferred) account, contributions may reduce your taxable income now; investments grow without annual tax; withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.

In a Roth account, contributions are made with after-tax money; investments grow without annual tax; qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

The choice largely turns on whether you expect your tax rate to be higher now or in retirement. If you are in a relatively low bracket now—early in a career, or in a lean business year—Roth contributions may be attractive. If you are in a high bracket now and expect a lower one later, traditional contributions may be. Many people hold both to diversify their future tax exposure.

## Health savings accounts

A health savings account (HSA), available to people covered by a qualifying high-deductible health plan, can offer a triple tax advantage: contributions may be deductible, growth is untaxed, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free [14]. Some savers pay current medical costs out of pocket and let the HSA grow for future health expenses, which can be substantial in retirement.

## A common priority order

The following order is widely used as a starting point, though individual circumstances may change it:

1. Contribute enough to a workplace plan to receive the full employer match, if any. A match is an immediate return on contributions.
2. Pay off high-interest debt (Chapter 5) and build a starter reserve (Chapter 4).
3. If eligible, contribute to an HSA.
4. Contribute to an IRA (Roth or traditional, depending on your situation).
5. Increase workplace plan contributions toward the annual limit.
6. Invest additional savings in a taxable brokerage account.

:::example Jordan's account priorities (illustrative)
Jordan's employer matches 50 percent of contributions up to 6 percent of salary. On a $62,000 salary, a 6 percent contribution is $3,720 a year and earns a $1,860 match—an immediate 50 percent return before any investment gain. Jordan keeps this contribution throughout the debt payoff period.

A SIMPLE IRA suits small employers who want a low-administration plan for staff. Each has different contribution formulas, deadlines, and administrative requirements; the IRS small business and self-employed center offers guidance [15].

# Chapter 9: Planning With Ranges: Withdrawal Rates and the FI Number

:::objectives
- Summarize the findings of the Bengen and Trinity withdrawal-rate studies.
- Explain sequence-of-returns risk and why it dominates early retirement outcomes.
- Calculate a financial independence range that accounts for Social Security, health costs, and taxes.
- Describe flexible withdrawal strategies, such as spending guardrails.
- State the limitations of historical withdrawal-rate research.
:::

Chapter 1 introduced the financial independence target as a range. This chapter examines where the common withdrawal-rate figures come from, what they do and do not mean, and how to adapt them to your circumstances.

## Key definitions

A withdrawal rate is the amount withdrawn from a portfolio in a year, expressed as a percentage of the portfolio's value. A safe withdrawal rate, in the research literature, is the highest initial withdrawal rate that, when adjusted each year for inflation, would have sustained a portfolio for a given period in all or nearly all historical scenarios studied. The FI number (financial independence number) is the portfolio size at which planned spending equals a chosen withdrawal rate: FI number = annual spending from the portfolio ÷ withdrawal rate.

## The Bengen study

In October 1994, financial planner William Bengen published "Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data" in the Journal of Financial Planning. Using U.S. stock and bond returns from 1926 onward, he tested how long portfolios would last under different initial withdrawal rates, with each year's withdrawal increased for inflation. He found that a first-year withdrawal of 4 percent from a portfolio of roughly half stocks and half intermediate-term government bonds would have lasted at least 33 years in every historical starting year he examined, while higher rates failed in some periods [2]. Bengen later extended his research with additional asset classes and has said that his updated analysis supports a somewhat higher worst-case initial rate of about 4.7 percent [16].

## The Trinity study

In 1998, three finance professors at Trinity University—Philip Cooley, Carl Hubbard, and Daniel Walz—published a related analysis in the AAII Journal. Rather than identifying a single worst case, they reported the share of historical periods in which different withdrawal rates and stock-bond mixes would have sustained a portfolio for 15, 20, 25, and 30 years [17]. Their tables, often cited as the source of "the 4 percent rule," showed high success rates for 4 percent inflation-adjusted withdrawals over 30 years from stock-heavy portfolios, and declining success at higher rates and longer horizons.

## What the research does not say

The "4 percent rule" is frequently overstated. Several limitations deserve emphasis:

- The studies use historical U.S. returns, a period in which the U.S. market performed well by international standards. Future returns may be lower.
- The original analyses mostly considered 30-year horizons. Someone reaching independence at 45 may need 45 years or more, which generally implies a lower sustainable rate.
- They assume fixed, inflation-adjusted spending. Real retirees often spend flexibly.
- Fees and taxes reduce the amount available to spend. A 1 percent annual fee consumes a large share of a 4 percent withdrawal.
- Success in research means the portfolio did not reach zero; it does not mean the retiree was comfortable or that the ending balance was large.

These limitations are why this book recommends a range—commonly 3.25 to 4.5 percent depending on horizon, flexibility, and costs—rather than a single number.

## Sequence-of-returns risk

Sequence-of-returns risk is the danger that poor investment returns early in the withdrawal period permanently impair a portfolio, even if average returns over the whole period are acceptable. When you are withdrawing money, selling assets after a decline locks in losses and leaves fewer shares to participate in a recovery. Two retirees with identical average returns can have very different outcomes depending only on the order in which those returns arrive. The first five to ten years of withdrawals matter most.

| Mitigation | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Lower initial rate | Smaller withdrawals leave more margin | Requires larger portfolio or later date |
| Cash buffer | Hold one to two years of spending in cash | Cash earns less over time |
| Spending guardrails | Cut spending after declines; raise after gains | Requires budget flexibility |
| Part-time income | Earn some income in early years | Delays full independence |
| Delay Social Security | Larger lifetime benefit as inflation-adjusted income | Draws more from portfolio early |

## Spending guardrails

A guardrail strategy sets an initial withdrawal and then adjusts it within limits based on portfolio performance. A simple version: begin at 4 percent; if a market decline pushes your current withdrawal above 5 percent of the portfolio, cut spending by 10 percent; if strong returns push it below 3 percent, allow a 10 percent raise. Research on flexible strategies generally finds that willingness to cut spending modestly in bad years permits a higher initial rate, though the specifics depend on the rules chosen.

## Building your FI range

1. Estimate annual spending at independence, in today's dollars. Include health insurance if you plan to stop work before Medicare eligibility, and include taxes on withdrawals.
2. Subtract reliable income you expect: pension, rental income, or part-time work you intend to continue.
3. Account for Social Security separately. Obtain your benefit estimates from your my Social Security account, which shows projected benefits at different claiming ages [18]. Because benefits may not begin until years after you stop working, model a "bridge" period funded by the portfolio.
4. Divide the remaining portfolio-funded spending by withdrawal rates of 3.25, 3.75, and 4.25 percent (or a range suited to your horizon).
5. Write the resulting range and the assumptions behind it, and revisit it annually.

:::example Jordan's refined FI range (illustrative)
Jordan currently spends $46,000 a year. At independence, Jordan expects to drop commuting and work clothing ($2,500) but add private health insurance before Medicare ($7,000) and estimated taxes on withdrawals ($3,000). Expected spending: 46,000 − 2,500 + 7,000 + 3,000 = $53,500.

Jordan plans to keep the side business at a reduced level, netting about $6,000 a year. Portfolio-funded spending: 53,500 − 6,000 = $47,500.

Range:
- 47,500 ÷ 0.0325 ≈ $1,462,000
- 47,500 ÷ 0.0375 ≈ $1,267,000
- 47,500 ÷ 0.0425 ≈ $1,118,000

Jordan's my Social Security statement projects a benefit at full retirement age. Once benefits begin, required portfolio withdrawals would fall substantially, which argues for the middle or upper end of the withdrawal range being tolerable later in life. Jordan records the range, notes that it is in today's dollars, and schedules an annual update. Jordan's current net worth is $11,600, so the honest message is that independence is a long-term project, and the near-term milestones—security and flexibility—are where effort should go now.
:::

# Chapter 10: Protecting the Plan: Insurance, Estate Basics, and Fraud

:::objectives
- Identify the risks that can derail a financial independence plan.
- Explain the role of health, disability, life, property, and liability insurance.
- Complete basic estate documents and beneficiary designations.
- Recognize common investment fraud warning signs and verify professionals.
:::

A financial plan is a structure built over years. Insurance and legal planning are its foundation: they rarely produce visible gains, but without them, a single event—a disability, a lawsuit, a death, a scam—can undo a decade of progress. The principle is to insure against losses you cannot absorb and to self-insure losses you can.

## Risk management principles

Risk management involves four strategies: avoid the risk (do not engage in the activity), reduce it (take precautions), retain it (pay small losses yourself), or transfer it (buy insurance). Insurance is efficient for low-probability, high-cost events. It is inefficient for small, predictable costs, where premiums typically exceed expected claims. As your reserves grow, you can raise deductibles and retain more risk, lowering premiums.

## Coverage types

| Coverage | Protects against | Who usually needs it | Planning note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Health | Medical costs | Everyone | Plan coverage before leaving employment |
| Disability | Loss of earned income | Anyone relying on earnings | Often overlooked; check employer coverage |
| Term life | Income loss for dependents at death | People with dependents or shared debts | Need generally declines as assets grow |
| Property (home, renters, auto) | Damage, theft | Owners and renters | Review limits and deductibles annually |
| Liability / umbrella | Lawsuits | Those with significant assets or risks | Relatively inexpensive extra protection |
| Business liability | Claims arising from business activity | Business owners | Personal policies often exclude business |

Disability insurance deserves special attention. For a working person, the ability to earn income is often the largest asset on any honest balance sheet. A long-term disability could stop savings and drain reserves. Many employers provide some coverage; read the definition of disability and the benefit amount.

Term life insurance provides a death benefit for a fixed period at relatively low cost. As savings accumulate toward independence, the need for life insurance generally declines, because the portfolio itself can support dependents. Be cautious about complex insurance products sold primarily as investments; understand fees, surrender charges, and how the seller is compensated before buying.

## Estate basics

Estate planning is not only for the wealthy. Minimum steps include:

1. Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance, which usually override a will. Review them after any major life event.
2. A will that names guardians for minor children and directs the distribution of assets.
3. A durable power of attorney for finances, naming someone to act if you cannot.
4. A health care directive and health care proxy.
5. A record of accounts and passwords stored securely, with instructions for a trusted person.

State laws vary; an attorney can prepare documents appropriate to your state and circumstances.

## Fraud and verification

Financial fraud is common. The Federal Reserve's 2025 household survey found that 20 percent of adults experienced financial fraud or scams in the prior year, and estimated total non-credit-card fraud at about $100 billion, of which $56 billion was borne directly by consumers [1]. Investors pursuing independence are attractive targets because they have savings and are seeking returns.

Common warning signs include guaranteed high returns with little risk, pressure to act immediately, unregistered sellers, complex strategies the seller cannot explain, requests for payment by gift card, wire, or cryptocurrency, and unsolicited contact. Before working with any financial professional, check their background. FINRA's free BrokerCheck tool shows the registration history, employment history, and disclosures—including customer complaints and disciplinary actions—for brokers and investment advisers [19]. Ask how the professional is paid, whether they act as a fiduciary (legally obligated to act in your interest) for all of your accounts, and request the firm's relationship summary (Form CRS).

At a community event, Jordan meets a salesperson offering a "private income fund" paying "a guaranteed 12 percent a year." Jordan applies the verification steps:

# Chapter 11: Growing Income and Buying Back Time

:::objectives
- Explain why income growth complements spending control.
- Evaluate career, side-business, and skill investments by expected return.
- Calculate an effective hourly rate and a "buyback rate."
- Apply a time audit to decide what to delegate, automate, or eliminate.
:::

Saving more by spending less has a floor: you cannot cut below your essentials. Income has no comparable ceiling. For many people, especially early in their careers or running small businesses, the fastest route to a higher saving rate is to increase earnings while holding lifestyle growth below income growth. The key discipline is to direct a planned share of every income increase to saving, as Chapter 6 described.

## Three income levers

The first lever is career growth: raises, promotions, job changes, and credentials. Evaluate each investment—a certification, a degree, a move—by its cost, its likely effect on income, and its risk. A $2,000 certification that plausibly raises income by $5,000 a year has a payback period under six months; a $60,000 degree with uncertain payoff needs more scrutiny.

The second is a side business or self-employment. A small business can raise income and provide tax-advantaged retirement options (Chapter 8), but it carries risks and demands time. Test cheaply before investing heavily, and keep business finances separate from personal ones.

The third is investment income, which grows as the portfolio grows. It is the slowest lever early on and the dominant one late in the journey.

## The value of an hour

Your effective hourly rate is your annual income divided by the hours you work to earn it. Dan Martell, a software entrepreneur and author of Buy Back Your Time, popularized a related idea for business owners. He suggests dividing annual income by about 2,000 working hours to get an hourly value, then dividing that figure by four to find a "buyback rate": the most you should pay someone else to take a task off your plate, so that each dollar spent frees time worth about four times as much. Using his example, someone earning $100,000 a year has an hourly value of $50 and a buyback rate of $12.50 [20]. Martell argues that owners should hire to free their own calendar for higher-value work rather than simply to add capacity [21].

:::case The buyback principle in practice
Martell frames delegation as a cycle: audit two weeks of your calendar, mark each task by whether it drains or energizes you and by the value it creates, then eliminate, automate, or hand off the low-value, draining tasks, and reinvest the freed time in higher-value work [21]. He has described this as hiring for "calendar over capacity" [20]. The approach is designed for business owners, but it applies to households too: paying for a service that costs less than your buyback rate can be rational if, and only if, the freed time is redirected to something more valuable—earning, learning, health, or family. If the freed time is simply absorbed, the purchase is an expense, not an investment. Martell's methods and results are his own and not TTI's.
:::

:::example Jordan's time audit (illustrative)
Jordan's salary is $62,000 and side-business profit $5,400, totaling $67,400. Hourly value: 67,400 ÷ 2,000 ≈ $33.70. Buyback rate: 33.70 ÷ 4 ≈ $8.40.

A two-week audit shows that Jordan spends about four hours a week on side-business bookkeeping and invoicing, which Jordan finds draining. A bookkeeping software subscription costs $30 a month and would cut the task to about one hour a week—saving roughly 13 hours a month at an effective cost of about $2.30 an hour, well below the buyback rate. Jordan subscribes and redirects three of the freed hours each week to marketing the side business, with a target of one additional client a month.

By contrast, a weekly house-cleaning service at $120 a visit would save about three hours a week at $40 an hour—far above Jordan's buyback rate. Jordan declines for now, noting the decision can be revisited as income rises.
:::

| Decision | Cost per hour saved | Compared to buyback rate ($8.40) | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bookkeeping software | ~$2.30 | Below | Buy; redirect time to marketing |
| Grocery delivery | ~$10.00 | Slightly above | Use only in busy weeks |
| House cleaning | ~$40.00 | Well above | Not yet |
| Meal planning (do it myself) | $0 | — | Keep; low cost, saves money |

## Lifestyle creep

Lifestyle creep (or lifestyle inflation) is the gradual rise in spending as income grows, often without deliberate choice. It is the main reason higher earners do not automatically save more. The defenses are the ones already described: automate a fixed share of raises to saving, review spending by value, and refer to the purpose statement from Chapter 1 before major lifestyle upgrades.

# Chapter 12: The Monthly Review and Stop Rules

:::objectives
- Run a 30-minute monthly financial review.
- Track a small set of leading and lagging indicators.
- Define stop rules that trigger reassessment.
- Conduct an annual review that updates assumptions and the FI range.
:::

A plan that is never reviewed decays. Prices change, incomes change, laws change, and life changes. The monthly review is the operating rhythm that keeps the plan current without consuming your life. Its companion, the stop rule, is a commitment made in advance about what you will do when something goes wrong.

## Leading and lagging indicators

Lagging indicators measure results: net worth, total debt, portfolio value. They move slowly and are affected by markets you do not control. Leading indicators measure behaviors that drive results: saving rate, whether automatic transfers ran, spending versus plan in capped categories. They respond quickly and are mostly within your control. A good review emphasizes leading indicators monthly and lagging indicators quarterly or annually.

| Indicator | Type | Frequency | Target example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saving rate | Leading | Monthly | 20% or higher |
| Transfers completed | Leading | Monthly | All |
| Capped categories vs. plan | Leading | Monthly | Within 10% |
| High-interest debt balance | Lagging | Monthly | Falling to zero |
| Emergency reserve months | Lagging | Monthly | Toward target |
| Net worth | Lagging | Quarterly | Rising over the year |
| Allocation drift | Lagging | Quarterly | Within 5 points |
| FI range and assumptions | Lagging | Annually | Updated |

## The 30-minute monthly review

1. Minutes 0–5: Confirm all automatic transfers and bill payments ran.
2. Minutes 5–15: Compare spending to plan by category; note any variance above 10 percent and its cause.
3. Minutes 15–20: Update debt balances and reserve balances.
4. Minutes 20–25: Calculate the month's saving rate.
5. Minutes 25–30: Choose one adjustment for next month and check whether any stop rule has been triggered.

Keep a one-page log (Appendix E). Over a year, the log reveals patterns no single month can show.

## Stop rules

A stop rule is a predefined condition that triggers a pause and reassessment. Writing stop rules in advance protects you from the two most common errors under stress: doing nothing while a problem grows, and overreacting to short-term noise. Examples:

- If the emergency reserve falls below one month of essentials, pause extra debt and investment contributions until it is restored.
- If spending exceeds plan by more than 10 percent for two consecutive months, conduct a full category review before the next month begins.
- If any new high-interest debt appears, stop discretionary spending in the category that caused it until it is paid.
- If the stock market falls 20 percent or more, do not sell; review the investment policy statement and rebalance only according to its rule.
- If income falls by more than 15 percent, switch to an essential-only budget and reassess the FI timeline.
- If you are offered any investment promising guaranteed high returns, stop and verify before discussing further.

:::example Jordan's month-seven review (illustrative)
| Item | Plan | Actual | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transfers | All | All | On track |
| Dining out (cap $250) | $250 | $340 | 36% over; two work dinners |
| Card A balance | $2,150 | $2,150 | On schedule |
| Emergency reserve | $3,100 | $2,500 | Used $600 for a car repair |
| Saving rate | 17% | 15.8% | Below plan |

Stop rule check: the reserve is still above one month of essentials ($2,950)? No—it is $2,500, below the threshold. Jordan's stop rule applies: the extra $550 card payment is redirected to the reserve for one month, restoring it to $3,050. The dining-out overrun is noted; it was a single month, so no category review is required yet. Adjustment for next month: bring lunch on workdays and reimburse work dinners through the employer's expense process.

Jordan also notes that the car repair should have come from the sinking fund, which had $900 in it. Jordan moves $600 from the sinking fund to the reserve, recognizing the repair belonged to the "car" category in the expense calendar. With that correction, the reserve is restored without delaying the debt plan.
:::

## The annual review

Once a year, set aside two to three hours to:

1. Rebuild the balance sheet and cash flow statement.
2. Update the expense calendar for the coming year.
3. Verify current contribution limits and tax rules on IRS.gov.
4. Review insurance coverage and beneficiary designations.
5. Rebalance according to the investment policy statement.
6. Update the FI range with current spending and assumptions.
7. Revisit the purpose statement and decide whether the plan still serves it.

:::checklist Review system
- My monthly review is on the calendar.
- I track at least three leading indicators.
- I have written at least four stop rules.
- I keep a monthly log.
- My annual review date is set.
- A trusted person knows where my plan and records are kept.
:::

:::summary
The monthly review keeps the plan current in about 30 minutes, emphasizing leading indicators you control. Stop rules, written in advance, convert stressful situations into predefined actions and protect against both neglect and overreaction. An annual review updates statements, limits, insurance, allocation, and the FI range, and checks the plan against its purpose.
:::

## Rules for using a balance transfer

1. Calculate the transfer fee in dollars and compare it with the interest you would otherwise pay over the promotional period.
2. Divide the transferred balance by the number of promotional months. That is the monthly payment needed to clear it before the rate rises.
3. Set that amount as an automatic payment.
4. Do not use the card for new purchases; new purchases may not receive the promotional rate.
5. Record the promotion end date in your calendar two months early.

# Appendix F: Questions to Ask a Financial Professional

Many people benefit from professional help, especially for taxes, insurance, estate documents, and complex decisions. Use these questions before engaging anyone.

1. Are you registered, and with whom? (Verify the answer in FINRA BrokerCheck [19].)
2. Are you a fiduciary at all times, for all accounts you would manage for me? Will you put that in writing?
3. How are you paid: hourly or flat fee, a percentage of assets, commissions, or a combination? What would I pay in dollars per year?
4. What are the total annual costs of the investments you recommend, including fund expense ratios?
5. Do you or your firm receive any payments from the products you recommend?
6. May I have your firm's relationship summary (Form CRS) and advisory brochure?
7. What is your investment philosophy, and how would you respond if markets fell 30 percent?
8. Have you ever been the subject of a customer complaint or disciplinary action?
9. How often will we meet, and what will each review include?
10. Who holds my money? (It should be a third-party custodian, not the adviser personally.)

Professionals who decline to answer these questions clearly, or who pressure you to decide quickly, are not good fits regardless of their credentials.

# Appendix G: Formula Reference

| Measure | Formula |
|---|---|
| Net worth | Total assets − total liabilities |
| Saving rate | (Income + payroll retirement contributions − spending) ÷ (income + payroll retirement contributions) |
| Liquidity ratio | Liquid reserves ÷ monthly essential spending |
| Debt-to-income | Monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income |
| Monthly interest (approx.) | Balance × APR ÷ 12 |
| Sinking-fund transfer | Annual irregular expenses ÷ 12 |
| Emergency reserve target | Monthly essential spending × months chosen |
| Future value of a lump sum | PV × (1 + r)^n |
| Future value of annual contributions | C × [((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r] |
| FI number | Annual portfolio-funded spending ÷ withdrawal rate |
| Current withdrawal rate | Annual withdrawals ÷ current portfolio value |
| Effective hourly value | Annual income ÷ hours worked (Martell uses 2,000) |
| Buyback rate (Martell) | Effective hourly value ÷ 4 |
| Real return (approx.) | Nominal return − inflation rate |

In these formulas, PV is present value, r is the annual rate of return expressed as a decimal, n is the number of years, and C is the annual contribution.

# Glossary

Asset allocation: The division of a portfolio among asset classes such as stocks, bonds, and cash.

Avalanche method: A debt repayment method that directs extra payments to the highest-interest debt first.

Balance sheet: A statement of assets, liabilities, and net worth at a point in time.

Buyback rate: Dan Martell's term for the maximum hourly cost worth paying to delegate a task: annual income ÷ 2,000 ÷ 4.

Cash flow statement: A record of income and spending over a period.

Commitment device: An arrangement that makes it harder to abandon a goal.

Diversification: Spreading investments across many holdings to reduce company-specific risk.

Expense ratio: The annual operating cost of a fund, expressed as a percentage of assets.

FI number: The portfolio size at which planned portfolio-funded spending equals a chosen withdrawal rate.

Fiduciary: A professional legally obligated to act in a client's best interest.

Financial independence: The point at which assets can reliably fund living expenses without required work.

Guardrails: Rules that adjust withdrawals within limits based on portfolio performance.

Health savings account (HSA): A tax-advantaged account for medical expenses, available with a qualifying high-deductible health plan.

Index fund: A fund that seeks to match the return of a market index by holding its securities.

Inertia (status quo bias): The tendency to stay with the current arrangement.

Investment policy statement (IPS): A written description of how and why you invest.

Lifestyle creep: Gradual spending growth as income rises.

Liquidity: How quickly an asset can be converted to cash without loss.

Loss aversion: The tendency to feel losses more strongly than equivalent gains.

Net worth: Total assets minus total liabilities.

Present bias: The tendency to overweight immediate rewards relative to future ones.

Rebalancing: Restoring a portfolio to its target allocation.

Roth account: A retirement account funded with after-tax money, with tax-free qualified withdrawals.

Safe withdrawal rate: In research, the highest initial inflation-adjusted withdrawal rate that sustained portfolios in historical tests over a given period.

Saving rate: The share of income not spent.

Sequence-of-returns risk: The risk that poor returns early in a withdrawal period permanently impair a portfolio.

Sinking fund: Money set aside in advance for a known future expense.

Snowball method: A debt repayment method that directs extra payments to the smallest balance first.

Stop rule: A predefined condition that triggers reassessment.

Traditional (tax-deferred) account: A retirement account in which contributions may be deductible and withdrawals are taxed.

Withdrawal rate: Annual withdrawals as a percentage of portfolio value.
