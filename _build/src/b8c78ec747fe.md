# How to Use This Book

This book is for owners and marketers of small businesses who want a disciplined way to think about how customers find them, decide to buy, and come back. It treats the sales funnel not as a marketing slogan but as a system with stages, measurable conversion rates, and design choices that can be tested. The reader needs no prior training in marketing; the reader does need a willingness to count.

Each chapter opens with learning objectives and closes with a summary, a checklist or worksheet, and review questions. In between, concepts are defined plainly, a step-by-step framework is presented, and at least one worked example applies the framework to a fictional business with numbers you can verify. Where a real company has publicly documented an experience that illustrates the point, a case study box summarizes what was reported, with a citation in the References. Those companies' results are their own, not TTI's, and are not predictions of yours.

## What you will be able to do

- Describe any funnel as a series of stages and calculate the conversion rate at each.
- Define a specific customer segment and the problem you solve for it.
- Construct an offer using an explicit value framework.
- Choose a funnel architecture suited to your type of business.
- Design capture, follow-up, and checkout steps that respect the law and the customer.
- Measure a funnel, find its constraint, and run honest experiments to improve it.

## How the book is organized

Chapters 1–4 establish the foundations: funnel mathematics, the customer, the offer, and the overall architecture. Chapters 5–10 work through the stages in order: attention, capture, follow-up, trust, purchase, and retention. Chapter 11 covers measurement and experimentation, and Chapter 12 turns the method into a ninety-day build plan. Appendices supply templates: a funnel map, an offer worksheet, email sequences, a landing-page checklist, a metrics dashboard, and an experiment log.

:::callout A word about hype
Many funnel courses promise "automated income." This book does not. A funnel is a description of how your customers already behave and a set of decisions about how to serve them better at each step. Good architecture can reduce waste and improve conversion; it cannot create demand for an offer nobody wants.
:::

# Chapter 1: What a Funnel Is and Why the Arithmetic Matters

:::objectives
- Define a sales funnel and its typical stages.
- Calculate stage conversion rates and overall conversion.
- Explain why improving a constrained stage matters more than improving the others.
- Distinguish a funnel from a customer journey and a flywheel.
:::

A **sales funnel** is a model of the stages a person passes through on the way from not knowing your business exists to becoming a customer, and often to buying again. The word "funnel" captures the essential fact: at every stage, some people drop out. That is normal and unavoidable. The architect's job is not to prevent all drop-off but to understand where and why it happens and to remove the drop-off that is caused by poor design rather than poor fit.

## The canonical stages

Different authors use different labels. This book uses six:

1. **Attention**: the person notices your business (sees a post, hears a recommendation, finds a search result).
2. **Interest**: the person engages (visits your site, watches a video, reads a page).
3. **Capture**: the person gives you a way to continue the conversation (email, phone, booking request, follow).
4. **Consideration**: the person evaluates whether you are the right choice (reads reviews, compares, asks questions).
5. **Purchase**: the person buys.
6. **Retention and referral**: the person buys again, stays subscribed, or tells others.

Not every business uses every stage. A walk-in coffee shop compresses attention, interest, and purchase into seconds. A consulting firm may spend months in consideration.

## Conversion arithmetic

The **stage conversion rate** is the share of people at one stage who reach the next. The **overall conversion rate** is the product of all stage rates. This multiplication is the most important idea in the book.

:::example Illustrative: a home-organizing service
In a typical month, 4,000 people see the business's posts or ads. Of those, 10 percent (400) visit the website. Of visitors, 8 percent (32) request a free consultation. Of those, 75 percent (24) attend. Of attendees, 33 percent (8) hire the service.

| Stage | People | Stage rate |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | 4,000 | — |
| Interest (site visit) | 400 | 10% |
| Capture (consult request) | 32 | 8% |
| Consideration (attend consult) | 24 | 75% |
| Purchase | 8 | 33% |

Overall conversion = 0.10 × 0.08 × 0.75 × 0.33 ≈ 0.2 percent of people who saw the business.

Now suppose the owner improves the website so that 12 percent of visitors request a consultation instead of 8 percent. With nothing else changed, purchases rise from 8 to about 12, a 50 percent increase from changing one stage. Doubling ad spend would also roughly double the top of the funnel, but at real cost; improving the capture rate cost a few hours of rewriting.
:::

## The constraint

Because stage rates multiply, every stage matters, but they do not matter equally at every moment. The **constraint** is the stage whose improvement would most increase the final output for the least effort. It is often the stage with the most unusual drop-off compared with similar businesses, or the one that has never been deliberately designed. Chapter 11 provides a method for finding it.

## Related models

A **customer journey map** describes the same path from the customer's point of view, including emotions and touchpoints. A **flywheel** emphasizes that satisfied customers feed new attention through referrals and reviews. These models complement the funnel. This book uses the funnel for measurement and borrows from the others when it helps.

:::checklist Funnel basics
- I can name the stages my customers pass through.
- I know, or can estimate, how many people reach each stage in a month.
- I have calculated the conversion rate between each pair of stages.
- I understand that stage rates multiply.
- I have a first guess at which stage is my constraint.
:::

:::summary
A funnel is a staged model of how strangers become customers. Stage conversion rates multiply, so a modest improvement at one weak stage can raise output substantially. The architect's task is to find the constraint and design it better, not to eliminate all drop-off.
:::

:::review
1. Define stage conversion rate and overall conversion rate.
2. Why do improvements at one stage have a multiplied effect on output?
3. In the worked example, what happens to purchases if attendance rises from 75 to 90 percent?
4. What is a constraint in a funnel?
5. How does a flywheel model complement the funnel model?
:::

# Chapter 2: Knowing Exactly Whom You Serve

:::objectives
- Define a customer segment precisely enough to guide design decisions.
- Describe the customer's problem using the "job to be done" lens.
- Gather evidence about customers through interviews and existing data.
- Write a one-paragraph customer profile grounded in evidence.
:::

Every funnel decision, from which channel to use to what words appear on a button, depends on who the customer is. A funnel built for "everyone" converts no one in particular. Precision here is not a marketing luxury; it is the foundation.

## Segments, not demographics alone

A **segment** is a group of potential customers who share a problem, a context, and a way of buying. Demographics (age, income, location) are sometimes useful, but behavior and circumstance usually matter more. "Homeowners aged 35–55" is a demographic. "Homeowners who have just bought an older house and are discovering repair problems they did not expect" is a segment. The second tells you what they search for, what they fear, and when they buy.

## The job to be done

A useful lens is to ask what **job** the customer is hiring your product or service to do. People do not want a quarter-inch drill bit; they want a quarter-inch hole, and behind that, a shelf on the wall, and behind that, an organized room. Describing the job in the customer's terms reveals what they value and what competes with you, including doing nothing.

For each segment, write:

- **Situation**: when does the need arise?
- **Motivation**: what are they trying to achieve?
- **Desired outcome**: how will they know it worked?
- **Obstacles**: what makes it hard today?
- **Alternatives**: what else could they do, including nothing?

## Gathering evidence

Opinions about customers are cheap; evidence is better. Three sources are available to almost any business:

1. **Customer interviews.** Ten to fifteen short conversations with recent customers or near-customers. Ask about the situation that led them to look, what they tried before, what almost stopped them, and what made them choose. Avoid leading questions ("Wasn't our price great?").
2. **Existing records.** Inquiry forms, emails, reviews, and sales notes contain the customer's own words. Collect recurring phrases.
3. **Lost deals.** People who considered you and chose otherwise are among the most informative sources. A short, polite question ("Would you share what led you to go another way?") often gets candid answers.

:::example Illustrative: refining a segment for a bookkeeping firm
A two-person bookkeeping firm initially describes its customer as "small businesses." After twelve interviews it finds that its best clients share a pattern: service businesses with two to ten employees, in their second to fourth year, whose owners did their own books until a tax-season crisis. Their trigger is usually a letter from a tax authority or a lender's request for financial statements. Their main fear is being judged for messy records. The alternative they consider most is "keep doing it myself and hope."

Profile: "Owners of service businesses with two to ten employees, two to four years old, who have just experienced a bookkeeping-related scare and want someone to clean up and maintain their books without judgment, so they can answer a lender or tax authority with confidence."

This profile immediately suggests funnel decisions: content addressing "behind on books" situations, a no-judgment cleanup offer, and outreach timed to tax season and loan-application periods.
:::

## Common errors

- **Defining by product, not problem.** "People who need bookkeeping" describes your service, not their situation.
- **Too many segments at once.** Start with the segment that is most profitable and easiest to reach.
- **Ignoring non-buyers.** The people who almost bought tell you what to fix.

# Chapter 3: Constructing the Offer

:::objectives
- Define an offer and distinguish it from a product.
- Apply a four-variable value framework to strengthen an offer.
- Design risk reversal and bonuses responsibly.
- Write a one-sentence offer statement.
:::

An **offer** is the complete proposition you put in front of a customer: what they get, what it costs, how it is delivered, what happens if it does not work, and why they should act now rather than later. Two businesses can sell the same product with very different offers, and the offer, more than the product, often determines how well a funnel converts.

## A four-variable view of value

Alex Hormozi, an entrepreneur and investor, sets out a **value equation** in his 2021 book *$100M Offers* [1]. In his formulation, perceived value increases with the **dream outcome** and the **perceived likelihood of achievement**, and decreases with the **time delay** before results and the **effort and sacrifice** required [1][2]. He presents the equation as a thinking tool rather than a measured formula, and his company makes a video lesson on it freely available [2].

The framework is useful because it gives four separate levers:

| Lever | Question the buyer asks | Ways to improve it honestly |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome (increase) | What do I actually get? | Describe the result in the customer's words, not your features |
| Perceived likelihood (increase) | Will it work for me? | Show your process; offer real, permitted testimonials; explain qualifications |
| Time delay (decrease) | How long until I see something? | Deliver an early, visible first win |
| Effort and sacrifice (decrease) | What will this cost me besides money? | Do more of the work for the customer; simplify steps |

Hormozi argues that reducing the bottom of the equation (time and effort) is often a stronger lever than inflating the top [1]. For small businesses, this is good news: making a service faster or easier to use is usually within your control, while promising bigger outcomes invites exaggeration.

:::case Where the framework comes from
Hormozi presents the value equation in *$100M Offers* as a lesson drawn from building and selling service businesses, beginning with gyms, and his company teaches it in a free video course [1][2]. His central argument is that sellers usually compete by promising a bigger outcome, when buyers are often more moved by faster results and less effort. Treat the framework as a structured checklist, not a law of nature. Any results he or his companies report are their own, not TTI's.
:::

## Risk reversal

A **guarantee** shifts risk from the buyer to the seller. Guarantees can raise perceived likelihood, but they must be honest and honored. A guarantee you cannot afford to keep, or one buried in conditions, is a liability and, if misleading, may violate consumer-protection law. Simple forms include a satisfaction guarantee on a first session, a redo guarantee on workmanship, or a clear refund window on a product.

## Bonuses and scarcity

**Bonuses** are additional items that address a specific obstacle (a checklist, a follow-up call, a template). They work best when each solves a real problem the customer will face. **Scarcity and urgency** (limited spots, deadlines) are legitimate only when they are true. A countdown timer that resets on every visit is deceptive.

:::example Illustrative: rebuilding a bookkeeping offer
Original offer: "Monthly bookkeeping, $400 per month."

Applying the four levers:

- Dream outcome: "Books you can hand to a lender or tax preparer without embarrassment."
- Perceived likelihood: a one-page description of the three-step cleanup process; a sample (anonymized) monthly report; the owner's credentials.
- Time delay: a first deliverable within 10 business days, a clear "where you stand" report, before the full cleanup is complete.
- Effort: the firm connects directly to bank feeds with the client's permission; the client answers one short weekly question list instead of sending piles of receipts.

Rebuilt offer: "We clean up your books and keep them current, with a 'where you stand' report in 10 business days and a 10-minute weekly check-in instead of shoeboxes of receipts. Cleanup from $900; then $400 per month. If the first report does not give you a clear picture, we refund the cleanup fee."

The price did not fall. The perceived value rose because time and effort fell and likelihood became visible. Whether it converts better must be tested (Chapter 11).
:::

## The one-sentence offer statement

Write your offer in one sentence: "For [segment], we [deliver outcome] in [time frame] with [what makes it easy], backed by [risk reversal]." If you cannot write the sentence, the offer is not yet clear enough to build a funnel around.

:::worksheet Offer construction
Segment: ___
Dream outcome in the customer's words: ___
How I show likelihood (process, proof, credentials): ___
First visible win and when it arrives: ___
Effort I remove from the customer: ___
Risk reversal (and can I afford to honor it?): ___
Bonuses (each tied to a specific obstacle): ___
One-sentence offer statement: ___
[[box 3]]
:::

:::summary
An offer is the complete proposition, not just the product. Hormozi's value equation provides four levers: raise dream outcome and perceived likelihood; lower time delay and effort. For small firms, reducing time and effort is often the most honest and controllable lever. Guarantees, bonuses, and urgency must be truthful and honored.
:::

# Chapter 4: Choosing a Funnel Architecture

:::objectives
- Describe four common funnel architectures and their typical uses.
- Match an architecture to a business's price point, sales cycle, and delivery model.
- Draw a funnel map showing stages, assets, and handoffs.
- Identify the minimum viable funnel for launch.
:::

With a segment and an offer defined, you can choose the structure that will carry customers from attention to purchase. Most small-business funnels follow one of four patterns.

## Four architectures

**1. Direct-response (product) funnel.** Attention leads straight to a product page and checkout. Common for lower-priced physical or digital products where the buyer can decide quickly. Key stages: ad or post → product page → cart → checkout → confirmation → post-purchase follow-up.

**2. Lead-capture (nurture) funnel.** Attention leads to a free resource in exchange for an email or phone number, followed by a sequence of helpful messages that build toward an offer. Common when the buyer needs education or time. Key stages: content → landing page → lead magnet → email sequence → offer → purchase.

**3. Consultation (service) funnel.** Attention leads to a booking for a call or visit, where a person diagnoses the need and proposes a solution. Common for higher-priced or customized services. Key stages: content or referral → booking page → pre-call questionnaire → consultation → proposal → follow-up → agreement.

**4. Subscription or membership funnel.** Attention leads to a trial or low-commitment first purchase, followed by ongoing billing. Retention is as important as acquisition. Key stages: offer → trial or first order → onboarding → first value → renewal.

| Architecture | Typical price | Sales cycle | Main human touchpoint | Key metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct-response | Low | Minutes to days | Little or none | Checkout conversion |
| Lead-capture | Low to mid | Days to weeks | Optional | Lead-to-customer rate |
| Consultation | Mid to high | Weeks to months | Consultation | Consult-to-close rate |
| Subscription | Recurring | Ongoing | Onboarding | Retention or churn |

## Matching architecture to business

Ask three questions. How much does the customer need to learn before buying? How customized is the delivery? How much is a customer worth over time? A high-learning, highly customized, high-value service suits a consultation funnel; a low-learning, standardized, low-price product suits direct response. Many businesses combine architectures, for example a lead-capture funnel that ends in a consultation.

## The funnel map

A **funnel map** is a diagram, or simply a table, listing each stage, the asset that serves it (a post, a page, an email, a script), the action you want, the metric you will track, and the person responsible. The map makes gaps visible: a stage with no asset, an asset with no owner, a handoff with no follow-up.

:::example Illustrative: funnel map for a residential window-cleaning company
| Stage | Asset | Desired action | Metric | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Attention | Neighborhood social posts; door hangers after each job | Visit site or call | Visits; calls | Owner |
| Interest | Service page with prices from, photos, service area | Request quote | Quote requests ÷ visits | Owner |
| Capture | Two-minute quote form (address, window count, photos) | Submit form | Form completions | Website |
| Consideration | Quote sent within 2 business hours, with reviews | Accept quote | Acceptance rate | Office manager |
| Purchase | Online booking and deposit | Book date | Bookings | Office manager |
| Retention | Reminder at 6 months; referral card | Rebook; refer | Rebook rate; referrals | Office manager |

The map revealed one gap: no one followed up on quotes not accepted within a week. The company added a single follow-up message on day 5 (Chapter 7).
:::

## The minimum viable funnel

Do not build every asset before launching. The **minimum viable funnel** is the smallest set of assets that lets a real customer move from attention to purchase: one traffic source, one page, one capture method, one follow-up, and one way to pay. Launch that, measure it, and improve the constraint. Elaborate funnels built before any customer has passed through them usually contain expensive guesses.

:::checklist Architecture decision
- I have chosen a primary architecture and can explain why.
- My funnel map lists an asset, action, metric, and owner for every stage.
- Every handoff (form to person, quote to follow-up) has an owner.
- I have identified my minimum viable funnel.
- I have not built assets for stages no customer has reached yet.
:::

:::summary
Four architectures cover most small businesses: direct-response, lead-capture, consultation, and subscription. Choose based on how much the customer must learn, how customized delivery is, and customer value over time. A funnel map exposes gaps; a minimum viable funnel gets real customers moving so you can measure before elaborating.
:::

# Chapter 5: Attention: Earning the First Look

:::objectives
- Distinguish owned, earned, and paid attention.
- Select one or two primary attention channels based on where the segment already is.
- Apply the principle of message-market match to content and ads.
- Estimate the cost of attention and judge whether a channel is affordable.
:::

Attention is the top of every funnel. Without it, the best offer and the cleanest checkout produce nothing. Yet attention is also where many small businesses waste the most money, by spreading thin across every platform or buying traffic before the rest of the funnel can convert it.

## Three kinds of attention

- **Owned** attention comes from assets you control: your website, your email list, your storefront, your vehicle signage.
- **Earned** attention comes from others choosing to talk about you: referrals, reviews, press coverage, shares.
- **Paid** attention is bought: ads, sponsorships, paid placements.

Owned and earned attention compound over time; paid attention stops when spending stops. A healthy funnel usually uses paid attention to test and accelerate, while building owned and earned sources for durability.

## Choosing channels

The right channel is the one where your segment already spends time with the relevant problem on their mind. A homeowner with a leaking roof searches; a parent looking for weekend activities scrolls local groups; a procurement manager reads industry newsletters. Start with one or two channels and do them well. Adding channels before the first one works multiplies effort without multiplying learning.

## Message-market match

A message converts attention only if it speaks to the segment's situation in their words. The job-to-be-done profile from Chapter 2 is the raw material. A useful test: if a member of the segment saw the message, would they think "that's me"? Clever messages that miss the situation underperform plain messages that hit it.

:::case Dollar Shave Club's launch video
In March 2012, Dollar Shave Club released a launch video in which founder Michael Dubin, speaking directly to camera, mocked overpriced razors in plain, comic language. The *New York Times* reported that the video cost about $4,500 to make and that the company received 12,000 orders in the first 48 hours, more than its servers could handle [3]. *Inc.* later recounted Dubin's background in improv comedy and the company's 2016 sale to Unilever, reported at $1 billion [4]. Two lessons are relevant to funnel design: the message named a specific frustration (paying too much for razors) in the customer's voice, and the downstream funnel (servers, fulfillment) was not ready for the attention it received. Their results are their own, not TTI's, and viral outcomes of this scale are rare and unpredictable.
:::

## The cost of attention

For paid channels, three numbers decide affordability:

- **Cost per click (CPC)** or cost per thousand impressions (CPM).
- **Conversion rate** from click to customer (the product of downstream stage rates).
- **Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** = total spend ÷ customers acquired.

CAC must be compared with **gross profit per customer** over a sensible horizon. If acquiring a customer costs more than the gross profit they generate, paid attention loses money no matter how many clicks it produces.

:::example Illustrative: is this ad channel affordable?
A meal-prep company pays an average of $1.20 per click. Of people who click, 3 percent place a first order. The first order yields $28 of gross profit; on average customers place 4 orders over six months.

- Cost per first order: $1.20 ÷ 0.03 = $40.
- Gross profit on the first order: $28. The channel loses $12 per customer on the first order.
- Gross profit over six months: 4 × $28 = $112. The channel earns $72 per customer over six months, before overhead.

The channel is affordable only if the company can finance the gap until repeat orders arrive and if the repeat estimate is real. If the click-to-order rate were raised from 3 to 4 percent, cost per first order would fall to $30, a reminder that downstream conversion directly changes what attention costs.
:::

| Channel type | Speed to results | Cost profile | Durability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Search ads | Fast | Ongoing per click | Stops when spending stops |
| Social ads | Fast | Ongoing per impression | Stops when spending stops |
| Organic social content | Slow | Time | Moderate |
| Search-optimized content | Slow | Time up front | High |
| Referrals and reviews | Moderate | Service quality | High |
| Local partnerships | Moderate | Relationship time | High |

:::checklist Attention plan
- I know where my segment already looks when the problem is on their mind.
- I have chosen no more than two primary channels.
- My message would make a segment member think "that's me."
- I know my CAC and my gross profit per customer.
- The rest of my funnel can handle a sudden increase in attention.
:::

:::summary
Attention comes in owned, earned, and paid forms; owned and earned compound while paid stops with spending. Choose one or two channels where the segment already looks, and write messages in the segment's words. A paid channel is affordable only when CAC is below gross profit per customer over a horizon you can finance.
:::

# Chapter 6: Capture: Landing Pages and Lead Magnets

:::objectives
- Define a landing page and its essential components.
- Design a lead magnet that solves a narrow, real problem.
- Reduce form friction without losing needed information.
- Collect contact information lawfully and transparently.
:::

**Capture** is the stage at which an interested person gives you a way to continue the conversation. For most funnels, it happens on a **landing page**, a page built for one purpose and one action, as opposed to a general website page with many links.

## Anatomy of a landing page

A landing page that converts well typically contains, in this order:

1. **A headline** that names the outcome or problem in the segment's words.
2. **A short explanation** of what the visitor will receive and why it helps.
3. **Evidence**: a brief description of your process, credentials, or genuine reviews.
4. **A single call to action**, repeated if the page is long.
5. **A form** asking for only what you need.
6. **A privacy statement** explaining what you will do with the information.

Remove navigation menus and competing links where possible. Every additional option is an invitation to leave.

## Lead magnets

A **lead magnet** is something of value offered in exchange for contact details: a checklist, a short guide, a calculator, a sample, a free assessment, or a consultation. The best lead magnets share three traits. They solve a narrow problem quickly. They relate directly to the paid offer, so the people they attract are potential buyers. And they demonstrate competence, so the recipient's perceived likelihood of success (Chapter 3) rises.

:::warning Lead magnets that attract the wrong people
A general giveaway, such as a prize drawing for a tablet, attracts people who want a tablet, not your service. Lists built this way look large and convert poorly. Measure lead magnets by the number of customers they eventually produce, not the number of email addresses.
:::

## Form friction

Each field on a form reduces completions somewhat, but removing fields you genuinely need just moves the work to later. The rule is to ask only for what you need at this stage. A newsletter needs an email address. A quote may need an address and a few details. A consultation booking may need a short questionnaire, which also qualifies the lead and prepares the conversation.

:::example Illustrative: reducing friction on a quote form
A landscaping company's quote form had 14 fields, including budget, preferred plants, and how the visitor heard about the company. Of 600 monthly visitors to the page, 18 (3 percent) completed it.

The owner reduced the form to five fields (name, phone or email, address, project type from a short list, optional photo upload) and moved the remaining questions to the follow-up call. The next month, 600 visitors produced 42 completions (7 percent). The owner then checked downstream: the share of leads who became customers fell slightly, from 33 percent to 29 percent, because some less-serious leads came through. Net customers rose from about 6 to about 12. The change was worth keeping, and the owner added a short qualifying question to the call script.
:::

## Lawful, transparent collection

When you collect contact information, say plainly what you will send and how often, and honor that. The Federal Trade Commission's guidance on protecting personal information recommends keeping only what you need, protecting what you keep, and disposing of what you no longer need [5]. If you will send marketing text messages or make automated calls, U.S. law imposes consent requirements; obtain clear consent and consult current guidance or counsel before launching text campaigns. For email, the CAN-SPAM requirements in Chapter 7 apply to every commercial message.

| Form purpose | Fields needed now | Fields to defer |
|---|---|---|
| Newsletter or guide | Email (name optional) | Everything else |
| Quote request | Name, contact, address, project type | Budget, timing, source |
| Consultation booking | Name, contact, time slot, 2–4 qualifying questions | Detailed history |
| Purchase | Payment, shipping, email | Marketing preferences (optional, unchecked) |

:::checklist Landing page review
- One page, one purpose, one call to action.
- Headline uses the segment's words.
- The lead magnet solves a narrow problem related to the paid offer.
- The form asks only for what is needed now.
- A plain privacy statement explains what happens to the information.
- I measure customers produced, not just leads.
:::

:::summary
A landing page serves one purpose with one action. Effective lead magnets solve narrow problems related to the paid offer. Shorter forms usually raise completions, but downstream quality must be checked. Collect only what you need, say what you will do with it, and follow consent rules for texts and calls.
:::

# Chapter 7: Speed and Follow-Up

:::objectives
- Explain the evidence on lead response time.
- Design a speed-to-lead process for a small team.
- Write a follow-up sequence that is helpful rather than pushy.
- Comply with the CAN-SPAM Act's core requirements for commercial email.
:::

The moment after someone raises their hand is the most valuable and the most neglected moment in many funnels. A lead who requests a quote at 10 a.m. is thinking about the problem at 10 a.m. By the next day, they may have called a competitor, solved the problem themselves, or simply moved on.

:::case The short life of online sales leads
In a 2011 *Harvard Business Review* article, James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington reported an audit of how 2,241 U.S. companies responded to web-generated leads. Only 37 percent responded within an hour, 23 percent never responded at all, and the average response time among those that responded was 42 hours [6]. Analyzing data on 1.25 million leads, they found that firms contacting a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead (have a meaningful conversation with a decision maker) as those that waited even one hour longer, and more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited 24 hours or more [6]. These are research findings on other firms, not TTI results, but they make a strong case that response speed is a design variable, not an afterthought.
:::

## Designing for speed

A small team cannot answer every lead in five minutes around the clock, but it can design a process that makes fast responses normal:

1. **Route every lead to one place.** Forms, calls, social messages, and emails should land in a single inbox or list.
2. **Send an immediate acknowledgment.** An automatic reply confirms receipt, states when a person will respond, and offers one useful next step (a booking link or a short guide).
3. **Set a response standard.** For example, a personal reply within one business hour during business hours, and by 9 a.m. for after-hours leads.
4. **Assign an owner and a backup.** Someone must be responsible each hour of the business day.
5. **Measure response time weekly.** What is not measured drifts.

## The follow-up sequence

Most people do not buy on the first contact. A **follow-up sequence** is a planned series of messages that continues the conversation helpfully. A good sequence:

- Answers the questions the segment usually has, one per message.
- Provides evidence (a process explanation, a genuine review, a short example).
- Makes the next step easy (a reply, a booking link).
- Ends politely with a clear "closing the loop" message, rather than continuing forever.

:::example Illustrative: a five-touch quote follow-up
A window-cleaning company sends quotes within two business hours. If the quote is not accepted:

| Day | Channel | Message purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Email | Quote, what is included, how to book, two short reviews |
| 2 | Email | Answers the most common question ("Do I need to be home?") |
| 5 | Phone or text (with consent) | Personal check-in: "Any questions about the quote?" |
| 10 | Email | Seasonal note: booking calendar for the month is filling; true availability only |
| 21 | Email | Closing the loop: "We'll stop following up; reply anytime if timing changes." |

Before the sequence, 30 percent of quotes were accepted. With it, suppose 38 percent are accepted. On 50 quotes a month at an average job of $260, that is 4 more jobs and about $1,040 more revenue a month. Illustrative figures only; measure your own.
:::

## CAN-SPAM essentials

In the United States, the CAN-SPAM Act applies to commercial email, including business-to-business messages. The FTC's compliance guide lists core requirements: do not use false or misleading header information; do not use deceptive subject lines; identify the message as an advertisement where required; include a valid physical postal address; tell recipients how to opt out; honor opt-out requests within 10 business days; and monitor what others do on your behalf [7]. The FTC states that each separate email in violation may be subject to penalties of up to $53,088 [7]. Penalty amounts are adjusted periodically, so check the current figure.

:::warning Follow-up versus harassment
Persistence is not the same as pressure. Never imply false urgency, never ignore a request to stop, and never use deceptive subject lines such as "Re: your order" when there is no order.
:::

:::checklist Follow-up system
- All leads arrive in one place.
- An automatic acknowledgment goes out immediately.
- A written response-time standard exists, with an owner and a backup.
- A follow-up sequence of three to six helpful messages ends with a close-the-loop message.
- Every commercial email has a postal address and a working opt-out.
- Opt-outs are honored promptly, and well within 10 business days.
:::

:::summary
Research reported in HBR found that firms contacting web leads within an hour were far more likely to qualify them than slower firms, yet many companies responded slowly or never. Design for speed by routing, acknowledging, and owning every lead. Follow up with helpful, finite sequences and comply with CAN-SPAM.
:::

# Chapter 8: Trust and Proof

:::objectives
- Explain why proof is necessary at the consideration stage.
- Identify forms of proof that are both persuasive and lawful.
- Describe key provisions of the FTC's 2024 rule on fake reviews and testimonials.
- Build a system for collecting genuine reviews.
:::

At the consideration stage, the buyer asks: "Will this work for someone like me, and can I trust these people?" **Proof** is anything that answers that question with evidence rather than assertion.

## Forms of proof

- **Process proof**: a clear explanation of how you work, step by step. Often underrated; it shows competence without relying on others' words.
- **Credentials**: licenses, certifications, insurance, years in business, professional memberships. Only state what is true and current.
- **Demonstrations**: samples, before-and-after photos of your own work (with permission), short videos of the process.
- **Reviews and testimonials**: statements from real customers about their real experience.
- **Guarantees**: discussed in Chapter 3; a seller's willingness to bear risk is itself evidence.

## The law on reviews and testimonials

In August 2024, the Federal Trade Commission announced a final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials; the rule took effect in October 2024 [8]. According to the FTC's announcement, the rule prohibits, among other things: writing, selling, or buying fake reviews, including reviews generated by AI that misrepresent the reviewer or their experience; offering compensation or incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular sentiment; certain undisclosed reviews by company insiders; using threats or intimidation to suppress negative reviews, or misrepresenting that a review section shows all reviews when negative ones have been suppressed; and selling or buying fake social-media indicators such as followers or views [8].

The practical implications for a small business are direct: never write reviews for yourself, never pay only for positive reviews, disclose material connections (for example, an employee or relative who reviews), and do not hide negative reviews from a section you present as complete.

:::case Operation AI Comply
In September 2024, the FTC announced a set of law-enforcement actions it called Operation AI Comply. Among them was an action against Rytr, the maker of an AI writing tool that, according to the FTC, offered a service capable of generating large numbers of customer reviews with specific, often false details [9]. The FTC also acted against several business-opportunity schemes that promised earnings from AI-powered online stores [9]. The lesson for funnel builders is that both fabricated proof and inflated earnings claims draw regulatory attention, whether or not AI is involved.
:::

## Collecting genuine reviews

A review system is part of the funnel's retention stage feeding back into consideration:

1. **Ask at the moment of value**: right after a job is completed well, or after the customer reaches the first visible win.
2. **Ask everyone**, not only customers you expect to be happy. Selective solicitation can distort the picture and invite legal risk.
3. **Make it easy**: one link, one sentence of instruction.
4. **Respond to all reviews**, positive and negative, professionally. A thoughtful response to a complaint is itself proof of how you handle problems.
5. **Never condition incentives on sentiment.** If you offer anything for a review, it must be offered for any honest review, and the arrangement should be disclosed.

:::example Illustrative: proof audit for a consultation funnel
A family-law mediation practice audits its booking page. It finds: no description of the mediation process, an old certification logo, and three undated testimonials. Changes:

| Gap | Fix |
|---|---|
| No process proof | Added a five-step "what happens in mediation" section |
| Outdated credential | Replaced with current certification and membership, verified |
| Undated testimonials | Requested permission from recent clients; added first name, month and year, and context; removed any statement implying a guaranteed outcome |
| No complaint handling visible | Added a short note on how concerns are handled and replied to all public reviews |

Consultation-booking rate on the page went from 4 percent to 5.5 percent over the next two months, an illustrative result; proof changes often work slowly because they affect trust rather than clicks.
:::

:::checklist Proof and compliance
- My process is explained step by step.
- Credentials shown are true and current.
- Every testimonial is from a real customer, with permission.
- No incentive is conditioned on positive sentiment.
- Material connections are disclosed.
- I ask all customers for reviews and respond to all reviews.
:::

:::summary
Proof answers the buyer's question "will this work for me?" with evidence. Process explanations, credentials, demonstrations, genuine reviews, and guarantees all serve. The FTC's 2024 rule bans fake and AI-fabricated reviews, sentiment-conditioned incentives, and review suppression. A deliberate review system turns satisfied customers into lawful proof.
:::

# Chapter 9: Making It Easy to Buy

:::objectives
- Explain why purchase-stage friction causes avoidable losses.
- Use published research on checkout abandonment to prioritize fixes.
- Design pricing presentation and payment options clearly.
- Comply with basic rules on subscriptions and automatic renewals.
:::

By the time a customer reaches the purchase stage, you have paid for their attention, captured their interest, followed up, and earned their trust. Losing them now, to a confusing price, a surprise fee, or a broken form, is the most expensive drop-off in the funnel.

:::case What checkout research shows
The Baymard Institute, which conducts large-scale usability research on e-commerce, compiles published studies of online shopping-cart abandonment. Its summary reports an average documented abandonment rate of about 70 percent across 50 studies, with individual studies ranging from about 55 to 84 percent [10]. Baymard's own surveys of U.S. shoppers list reasons such as extra costs (shipping, taxes, fees) that were too high, being required to create an account, concerns about trusting the site with card information, and a checkout process that was too long or complicated. Baymard estimates that large e-commerce sites could achieve a substantial increase in conversion rate, about 35 percent, through better checkout design [10]. Some abandonment is just browsing and can never be recovered; the point is that a meaningful portion is caused by design.
:::

## Principles of an easy purchase

1. **Show the full price early.** Surprise fees at the last step are among the most-cited reasons for abandonment. State shipping, taxes, and fees, or a clear estimate, before checkout.
2. **Allow guest checkout.** Offer account creation after purchase, not as a condition of it.
3. **Ask for the minimum.** Remove optional fields; use address lookup where available.
4. **Signal security plainly.** Use a reputable payment processor and say so; avoid cluttered "trust badge" collections that look suspicious.
5. **Offer the payment methods your segment uses.** Cards plus one or two common digital wallets cover most needs.
6. **Make the next step obvious.** A confirmation page and email that say exactly what happens next and when.

For service businesses, the "checkout" is the proposal and agreement. The same principles apply: a clear price, no surprises, an easy way to sign and pay a deposit, and a clear explanation of what happens next.

## Presenting price

Clarity beats cleverness. Common, honest techniques include:

- **Tiered options** (for example, Basic, Standard, Complete) that let customers self-select, with the differences stated plainly.
- **Anchoring with real comparisons**: showing what the problem costs the customer, or what the alternative costs, without inventing a "regular price" you never charge.
- **Payment plans** for larger purchases, with total cost disclosed.

:::warning Fake reference prices
Advertising a "was $199, now $99" price when the item was never genuinely offered at $199 can be deceptive. Use only true former prices or honest comparisons.
:::

## Subscriptions and automatic renewal

If your funnel ends in a subscription, extra care is needed. The federal Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA) requires sellers of online negative-option plans, where silence or failure to cancel is treated as acceptance, to clearly disclose material terms before collecting billing information, to obtain the consumer's express informed consent before charging, and to provide a simple mechanism to stop recurring charges [11]. Many states have additional automatic-renewal laws. Design subscriptions so that cancellation is as easy as signing up, and send renewal reminders. Beyond compliance, easy cancellation builds the trust that keeps good customers.

:::example Illustrative: fixing a service proposal
A painting contractor sends proposals as PDF attachments. Customers must print, sign, scan, and email them back, then mail a deposit check. Of 40 proposals a month, 14 (35 percent) are signed.

Changes: proposals sent as a web page with three tiers (one coat, two coats, two coats plus trim), each with a total price including materials; e-signature and card or bank deposit on the same page; a "what happens next" section with the scheduling process.

Next quarter's average: 40 proposals, 18 signed (45 percent). At an average job of $3,200 with 35 percent gross margin, four more jobs add about $12,800 of revenue and $4,480 of gross profit a month. Illustrative only; tiered proposals also shifted about a quarter of customers to the middle tier, raising average job size, which the owner tracked separately to avoid double-counting.
:::

| Friction point | Typical symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Surprise costs | Drop-off at final step | Show totals early |
| Forced account | Drop-off at login screen | Guest checkout |
| Long form | Slow completion, errors | Fewer fields, autofill |
| Unclear next step | Anxious emails after purchase | Confirmation page and email with timeline |
| Paper agreements | Proposals stall | E-signature and online deposit |
| Hard cancellation | Chargebacks, complaints | Simple online cancellation |

:::checklist Purchase stage audit
- The total price, or a clear estimate, appears before the final step.
- Guest checkout is available.
- Only necessary fields are required.
- Payment methods fit my segment.
- Confirmation explains what happens next and when.
- Subscription terms are disclosed clearly, consent is express, and cancellation is simple.
:::

:::summary
Purchase-stage losses are the most expensive in the funnel. Research compiled by Baymard finds high average cart abandonment, much of it tied to surprise costs, forced accounts, and complex checkouts. Show prices early, ask for the minimum, use honest price presentation, and make subscriptions easy to understand and cancel, as ROSCA requires for online negative-option plans.
:::

# Chapter 10: Retention and Referral

:::objectives
- Explain why retention and referral are part of funnel architecture.
- Calculate customer lifetime value with simple inputs.
- Design onboarding that delivers an early win.
- Structure a referral program that rewards both parties honestly.
:::

A funnel that ends at the first purchase leaves most of its value uncollected. Customers who return cost far less to serve than new customers cost to acquire, and satisfied customers bring others. Retention and referral turn a funnel into a loop.

## Customer lifetime value

**Customer lifetime value (LTV)** estimates the gross profit a customer generates over their relationship with you. A simple version:

LTV = average gross profit per purchase × average number of purchases per customer.

For subscriptions: LTV ≈ monthly gross profit per customer ÷ monthly churn rate. If gross profit is $30 a month and 5 percent of customers cancel each month, LTV ≈ $30 ÷ 0.05 = $600.

LTV sets the ceiling on what you can afford to spend acquiring a customer. Raising retention raises LTV, which in turn allows more spending on attention.

## Onboarding and the first win

**Onboarding** is everything that happens between purchase and the customer experiencing value. The goal is a **first win**: a visible, early result that confirms the decision (the "time delay" lever from Chapter 3). For a cleaning service, it might be a short checklist of what was done, with photos. For software, a completed first task. For a course, a finished first exercise with feedback. Customers who reach a first win quickly are more likely to stay and to refer.

## Referral programs

A **referral program** invites existing customers to introduce others, usually with a reward. The best programs share three features: the reward is meaningful and relevant to the product; both the referrer and the new customer benefit; and the request comes at a moment of satisfaction.

:::case Dropbox's two-sided referral program
In an April 2010 presentation titled "Startup Lessons Learned," Dropbox founder Drew Houston described how the company grew from about 100,000 registered users in September 2008 to about 4 million in January 2010 [12]. Houston reported that paid search advertising had been far too expensive relative to the value of a customer, and that the company instead built a referral program offering extra free storage to both the person referring and the person joining. According to the presentation, the referral program permanently increased signups by 60 percent, and at the time referrals accounted for about 35 percent of daily signups, with shared folders contributing another 20 percent [12][13]. The reward was the product itself (storage) and was given to both sides. Dropbox's results are its own, not TTI's; a free-storage reward works because it costs the company little and directly increases the product's value to the user.
:::

## Designing a small-business referral program

1. **Choose a reward tied to your offer**: a service credit, an upgrade, a free add-on. Avoid cash rewards that could create disclosure obligations or attract the wrong motives, unless you have reviewed the rules for your industry.
2. **Make it two-sided**: the new customer gets something too, which makes referring feel like a favor, not a sales pitch.
3. **Ask at the moment of value**: right after the first win or a successful job.
4. **Make it easy**: a personal link or a simple card with a code.
5. **Track it**: record who referred whom and fulfill rewards promptly.
6. **Disclose relationships**: if referrers post publicly about you in exchange for rewards, the FTC's Endorsement Guides call for clear disclosure of that material connection [14].

:::example Illustrative: LTV and referrals for a lawn-care company
Average gross profit per visit: $22. Average visits per customer per season: 18. Average seasons retained: 2.5.
LTV = $22 × 18 × 2.5 = $990.

The company introduces a two-sided referral reward: one free mowing ($35 retail, about $13 cost) for the referrer and the first mowing half price for the new customer (about $17 forgone). Total cost per referred customer: about $30. If 10 percent of 200 customers refer one new customer per season, that is 20 new customers at about $30 each, compared with an estimated $90 CAC from local ads. Even if referred customers had the same LTV as others, the program would be far cheaper acquisition; companies often find referred customers stay longer, but measure that rather than assume it.
:::

| Retention lever | Example | What to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Onboarding first win | Photo report after first visit | Second-purchase rate |
| Scheduled reminders | Six-month service reminder | Rebooking rate |
| Proactive check-ins | "How is it going?" at day 30 | Churn, complaints |
| Easy rebooking | One-click rebook link | Time between purchases |
| Referral program | Two-sided service credit | Referrals per customer |

:::checklist Retention and referral
- I know my approximate LTV.
- Onboarding delivers a first win quickly.
- I remind customers when they are likely to need me again.
- My referral reward is relevant, two-sided, and promptly fulfilled.
- Referrals are tracked by source.
- Material connections in public endorsements are disclosed.
:::

:::summary
Retention and referral convert a one-way funnel into a loop. LTV sets the ceiling on acquisition spending, and onboarding that delivers an early win improves retention. Dropbox's 2010 account shows how a two-sided, product-based referral reward can drive growth more cheaply than paid ads. Small businesses can apply the same principles at their own scale.
:::

# Chapter 11: Measurement and Experimentation

:::objectives
- Build a simple funnel dashboard with weekly stage counts and rates.
- Find the constraint using benchmarks and judgment.
- Design a valid A/B test or before-and-after comparison.
- Avoid the most common errors in interpreting funnel data.
:::

A funnel you cannot measure cannot be improved, only changed. Measurement need not be elaborate. A spreadsheet with weekly counts for each stage, kept consistently, is more useful than a sophisticated analytics tool that nobody checks.

## The dashboard

Track, weekly:

- Count of people at each stage (visits, leads, consultations, proposals, customers).
- Stage conversion rates.
- Spend by channel and CAC.
- Response time to leads (Chapter 7).
- Revenue and gross profit from new and returning customers.

Keep definitions stable. If "lead" means a completed form one month and any inquiry the next, the trend is meaningless.

## Finding the constraint

Three methods, used together:

1. **Compare with your own history.** Which rate has fallen or never improved?
2. **Compare with plausible benchmarks.** Published averages vary widely by industry and should be used only as rough guides; a rate far below anything plausible signals a design problem.
3. **Watch real customers.** Ask five people from your segment to use your page or process while thinking aloud. Where they hesitate or get confused is often the constraint.

## Experiments

An **A/B test** shows two versions (A and B) to comparable visitors at the same time and compares results. It is the most reliable way to learn whether a change caused an improvement. Small businesses often lack the traffic for formal A/B tests, so a **before-and-after comparison** is a practical alternative, with caution about seasonal and other changes.

Rules for honest experiments:

1. **Write the hypothesis first**: "Shortening the form from 14 to 5 fields will raise completions from 3 to at least 5 percent without lowering lead-to-customer rate below 25 percent."
2. **Change one thing at a time** when possible.
3. **Decide the run length in advance**: for example, until each version has at least a few hundred visitors, or a fixed number of weeks.
4. **Check downstream effects**: more leads of lower quality may not be an improvement.
5. **Record everything** in an experiment log (Appendix F).

:::example Illustrative: is the difference real?
A landing page has two headline versions. Version A: 500 visitors, 25 sign-ups (5.0 percent). Version B: 500 visitors, 35 sign-ups (7.0 percent).

Is B genuinely better? A rough check: with conversion rates around 5–7 percent and 500 visitors each, the random variation in each rate is about plus or minus 2 percentage points (approximately twice the square root of p × (1 − p) ÷ n, which for p = 0.06 and n = 500 is about 0.021). The observed difference of 2 points is about at the edge of that noise. The owner decides to run the test for another two weeks. With 1,000 visitors each and the same rates (50 vs. 70), the uncertainty narrows to about plus or minus 1.5 points, and the difference becomes more convincing. Many free online calculators perform this test formally; the lesson is to avoid declaring winners from small numbers.
:::

## Common errors

| Error | Example | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Declaring a winner too early | Stopping a test after 2 days | Set run length in advance |
| Ignoring downstream effects | More leads, fewer sales | Track to purchase |
| Changing many things at once | New page, offer, and price together | One change at a time, or accept you won't know which mattered |
| Vanity metrics | Celebrating followers, not customers | Tie every metric to purchases or gross profit |
| Shifting definitions | "Lead" changes meaning | Write definitions down |
| Seasonal confusion | Comparing December to June | Compare like periods or run simultaneous tests |

:::worksheet Constraint finder
Stage with lowest rate relative to history or benchmark: ___
Evidence (numbers): ___
What five segment members said when observed: ___
[[box 3]]
Hypothesis for improvement: ___
Metric and target: ___
Run length decided in advance: ___
Downstream metric to check: ___
:::

:::summary
A simple, consistent weekly dashboard is the foundation of improvement. Find the constraint by comparing with history and plausible benchmarks and by watching real customers. Test changes with written hypotheses, adequate sample sizes, and downstream checks, and avoid vanity metrics and early conclusions.
:::

# Appendix D: Landing Page and Checkout Checklist

:::checklist Landing page
- Headline names the outcome or problem in the segment's words.
- Two to four sentences explain what the visitor gets.
- Process proof appears (how it works, in steps).
- Credentials shown are current and verifiable.
- Reviews are genuine, permitted, and representative.
- One call to action; navigation minimized.
- Form asks only for what is needed now.
- Privacy statement explains use of information.
- Page loads quickly on a phone and is readable without zooming.
- Acknowledgment message is sent immediately after submission.
:::

:::checklist Checkout or proposal
- Full price or a clear estimate is visible before the final step.
- No surprise fees appear at the end.
- Guest checkout is available.
- Payment methods match the segment.
- Former or reference prices, if shown, are true.
- Subscription terms are clear before billing details are collected.
- Express consent is obtained before recurring charges.
- Cancellation is simple and explained.
- Confirmation page and email explain what happens next and when.
- Someone tests the full purchase path monthly.
:::

# Glossary

**A/B test**: An experiment showing two versions to comparable audiences at the same time to see which performs better.

**Attention**: The funnel stage at which a person first notices a business.

**Before-and-after comparison**: Comparing results before and after a change; less reliable than an A/B test because other factors may change too.

**CAN-SPAM Act**: The U.S. law setting rules for commercial email, including opt-out requirements.

**Capture**: The stage at which a person provides a way to continue contact.

**Churn**: The share of customers who stop buying or cancel in a period.

**Constraint**: The stage whose improvement would most increase output for the least effort.

**Conversion rate**: The share of people at one stage who move to the next (stage rate) or from the top to purchase (overall rate).

**Customer acquisition cost (CAC)**: Total acquisition spending divided by new customers acquired.

**Customer lifetime value (LTV)**: Estimated gross profit a customer generates over the relationship.

**Endorsement Guides**: FTC guidance on honest endorsements and disclosure of material connections.

**First win**: An early, visible result that confirms a customer's purchase decision.

**Funnel map**: A table or diagram listing each stage with its asset, desired action, metric, and owner.

**Job to be done**: The underlying progress a customer is trying to make by buying a product or service.

**Landing page**: A web page built for one purpose and one action.

**Lead magnet**: Something of value offered in exchange for contact information.

**Material connection**: A relationship between an endorser and a marketer that could affect how people weigh the endorsement.

**Minimum viable funnel**: The smallest set of assets allowing a customer to move from attention to purchase.

**Negative option**: A sales arrangement in which a customer's silence or failure to cancel is treated as acceptance of continued charges.

**Offer**: The complete proposition presented to a customer, including deliverables, price, terms, and risk reversal.

**ROSCA**: The Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act, a U.S. law governing certain online charges and negative-option plans.

**Segment**: A group of potential customers sharing a problem, context, and way of buying.

**Value equation**: Hormozi's framework relating perceived value to dream outcome, perceived likelihood, time delay, and effort.
