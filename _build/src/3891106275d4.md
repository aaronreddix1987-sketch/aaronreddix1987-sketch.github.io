# How to Use This Book

Small businesses rarely fail because the owner cannot do the work. More often, trouble starts in the back office: a missed step on a job, a tense customer conversation, an invoice sent late or never, a bill paid to a scammer, or a cash shortfall nobody saw coming. This workbook treats the back office as a system that can be designed, documented, and reviewed, with artificial intelligence (AI) tools used as assistants rather than decision-makers.

Each chapter introduces a concept, defines key terms, presents a framework, works through an illustrative example with real arithmetic, examines a case from public sources, and provides a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. A fictional running example, Rivera Home Repair, a two-person handyman business, shows how the tools fit together. Its numbers are invented for teaching.

The cases come from published research, court and tribunal decisions, government agencies, and company announcements. The organizations involved are named only as public examples; their results are their own, not TTI's.

## What you will be able to do

- Design kickoff and delivery checklists for paid work.
- Turn voice notes and recordings into one-page standard operating procedures.
- Write calm, accurate customer-support replies, with AI drafting and human review.
- Send invoices the same day work is completed.
- Track open invoices and send four polite payment reminders.
- Run a 15-minute weekly cash check.
- Screen vendor invoices and payment-change requests for fraud.
- Keep bank and payment access under human control.

## The human-only rule

Throughout this book, one rule applies: AI tools may draft, summarize, organize, and remind, but a person approves anything that moves money, makes a promise to a customer, or changes access to accounts. Do not give AI tools your banking passwords, payment authority, or the ability to send money.

:::warning Scope of this book
This workbook provides general operations guidance. Laws on consumer protection, collections, privacy, taxes, and recordkeeping vary by jurisdiction and change over time. Consult a qualified accountant, enrolled agent, or attorney for your specific situation.
:::

# Chapter 1: The Back Office as a System

:::objectives
- Explain why small businesses benefit from written systems.
- Describe the evidence that checklists reduce errors in complex work.
- Map the five parts of the back-office cycle.
- Identify where AI tools help and where they should not act alone.
:::

The front office wins work: marketing, sales, quotes. The back office delivers it and gets paid: scheduling, checklists, customer updates, invoicing, collections, bill payment, and cash review. In many small businesses, the back office lives in the owner's head. That works until the owner is sick, busy, or growing.

## Key terms

- **System**: a repeatable sequence of steps that produces a consistent result.
- **Checklist**: a written list of critical steps, used during work to prevent omissions.
- **Standard operating procedure (SOP)**: a document describing how a recurring task is done.
- **Back-office cycle**: the sequence from job kickoff to delivery, invoicing, collection, and cash review.
- **Human-in-the-loop**: a design in which a person reviews or approves AI output before it takes effect.

## The back-office cycle

| Stage | Main question | Tool in this book |
|---|---|---|
| Kickoff | Do we have everything we need to start? | Kickoff gate (Chapter 3) |
| Delivery | Did we complete every step and confirm with the customer? | Delivery sign-off (Chapter 3) |
| Support | Are customers informed and calm? | Support scripts (Chapters 5–6) |
| Billing | Was the invoice sent the same day? | Invoice quality check (Chapter 7) |
| Collection | Who owes us, and what is the next reminder? | Invoice tracker (Chapter 8) |
| Cash and protection | What is our cash position, and is every payment legitimate? | Friday cash check and scam shield (Chapters 9–11) |

:::case The surgical safety checklist
In a study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2009, Alex Haynes, Atul Gawande, and colleagues introduced a 19-item surgical safety checklist in eight hospitals in eight cities around the world [1]. Among nearly 7,700 patients studied before and after the change, the in-hospital death rate fell from 1.5 percent to 0.8 percent, and inpatient complications fell from 11.0 percent to 7.0 percent [1]. The authors described the results as associated with the checklist; the study used a before-and-after design, not a randomized trial, and later studies in other settings have found smaller or no effects. The lesson for a small business is modest but useful: in complex, repeated work, a short list of critical steps, used consistently, can reduce preventable errors.
:::

## Where AI helps

| Task | AI role | Human role |
|---|---|---|
| Turning voice notes into an SOP draft | Draft and format | Check accuracy, approve |
| Drafting customer replies | Draft in a calm tone | Verify facts, edit, send |
| Summarizing open invoices | Sort and summarize | Decide on follow-up |
| Drafting payment reminders | Draft from templates | Approve and send |
| Moving money or changing bank details | None | Human only |

:::example Illustrative: the cost of a missed step
Rivera Home Repair completes about 40 jobs a month at an average invoice of $420, for monthly revenue of 40 × $420 = $16,800. Owner Luis Rivera estimates that about 1 job in 20 needs a return trip because a step was missed, such as forgetting to confirm a part or skipping a final walkthrough.

Return trips per month: 40 ÷ 20 = 2. Each return trip takes about 1.5 hours of labor and 30 minutes of driving, for 2 hours total. At an internal labor cost of $45 an hour, that is 2 × 2 × $45 = $180 a month, or $2,160 a year, before counting customer frustration. If a checklist cut return trips by half, the business would save about $1,080 a year in labor alone. These figures are illustrative.
:::

## Why small businesses resist systems

Owners often resist writing things down for understandable reasons. The work feels too varied to standardize, documentation seems slower than doing the task, and the owner already knows the steps. These objections have some truth, but they overlook three costs. First, knowledge held only in the owner's head cannot be delegated, which caps the business at the owner's available hours. Second, memory fails under pressure, especially on busy days. Third, without written steps, there is no baseline to improve.

The answer is not a thick manual. It is a small number of short documents for the tasks that happen most often or cost the most when they go wrong. Start with one checklist, use it for a month, and add another only when the first has become a habit.

:::checklist Back-office starting audit
- Is there a written list of steps for our most common job?
- Do customers receive a confirmation and a completion message?
- Are invoices sent the same day work is completed?
- Do we know today who owes us money and how much?
- Do we review cash at least once a week?
- Do we verify any request to change vendor payment details?
- Is bank access limited to named people with multi-factor authentication?
:::

:::summary
The back office delivers work and collects payment. Treating it as a system, with checklists, SOPs, and regular reviews, reduces errors and stress. Evidence from surgery suggests that short checklists of critical steps can reduce preventable errors, though effects vary by setting. AI can draft and organize, but humans approve anything that moves money, makes promises, or changes access.
:::

# Chapter 2: Setting Up the Desk

:::objectives
- Organize folders, records, and tools for the back office.
- Explain basic federal recordkeeping expectations.
- Define access levels for people and AI tools.
- Apply a simple AI risk framework.
:::

Before writing checklists and scripts, set up the desk: where documents live, which tools you use, and who can access what. A small amount of setup work prevents a large amount of searching later.

## Key terms

- **Source document**: an original record of a transaction, such as an invoice, receipt, or bank statement.
- **Access level**: what a person or tool is allowed to see or do.
- **Least privilege**: giving each person or tool only the access needed for its job.
- **Audit trail**: a record of who did what and when.

## Recordkeeping basics

The IRS explains that businesses should keep records that support the income, deductions, and credits shown on a tax return, including gross receipts, purchases, expenses, assets, and employment taxes, and that supporting documents include items such as sales slips, invoices, receipts, deposit information, and canceled checks [2]. How long to keep records depends on the situation, and the IRS provides guidance on retention periods [2]. A consistent folder structure makes it easier to find these documents when you need them.

## The folder map

| Folder | Contents | Who has access |
|---|---|---|
| 01 Jobs | One subfolder per job: quote, photos, sign-off | Owner, crew |
| 02 Invoices sent | PDF of each invoice, by month | Owner, bookkeeper |
| 03 Receipts and bills | Vendor bills and receipts, by month | Owner, bookkeeper |
| 04 Bank statements | Monthly statements | Owner, bookkeeper |
| 05 SOPs | One-page procedures | Everyone |
| 06 Templates | Scripts, reminders, checklists | Everyone |

## Access levels for AI tools

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published its AI Risk Management Framework to help organizations manage the risks of AI systems, organized around four functions: govern, map, measure, and manage [3]. A small business does not need a formal program, but the four functions translate into simple questions.

| NIST function | Small-business question |
|---|---|
| Govern | Who decides which AI tools we use and for what? |
| Map | What tasks will each tool touch, and what could go wrong? |
| Measure | How will we check that outputs are accurate? |
| Manage | What do we do when a tool makes a mistake? |

:::example Illustrative: Rivera's access table
Luis lists four roles and their access:

Luis (owner): everything, including the bank.
Maria (office manager, part-time): invoices, receipts, scheduling; can view the bank but cannot send payments.
Crew member: job folders and SOPs only.
AI assistant: can read SOPs and templates and draft replies and reminders; no access to the bank, payment apps, or customer payment details.

Luis counts 6 folders and 4 roles, so he fills in a 6 × 4 = 24-cell access grid. Only 1 cell, Luis and the bank, includes payment authority.
:::

:::case Records that support the return
The IRS's recordkeeping guidance for small businesses explains that good records help a business monitor its progress, prepare financial statements, identify sources of receipts, keep track of deductible expenses, prepare tax returns, and support items reported on those returns [2]. These are government guidelines, not a statement about any particular business's results. The guidance supports a practical point of this book: the same folders and habits that make the Friday cash check easy also make tax time less stressful.
:::

# Chapter 3: Job Kickoff and Delivery Checklists

:::objectives
- Design a kickoff gate that confirms readiness before work begins.
- Build a delivery sign-off that closes each job cleanly.
- Distinguish read-do and do-confirm checklists.
- Connect delivery promises to customer expectations and legal duties.
:::

Most delivery problems begin before the work starts: unclear scope, missing deposits, unconfirmed parts, or wrong addresses. A kickoff gate stops the job from starting until the basics are confirmed. A delivery sign-off ensures the job ends with the customer's agreement and an immediate invoice.

## Key terms

- **Kickoff gate**: a short checklist that must be completed before work begins.
- **Delivery sign-off**: a closing checklist completed with the customer at the end of a job.
- **Read-do checklist**: a list followed step by step, for unfamiliar tasks.
- **Do-confirm checklist**: a list used after doing the work from memory, to confirm nothing was missed.
- **Scope**: a written description of what the job includes and excludes.

## The kickoff gate

| Item | Confirmed? |
|---|---|
| Written scope agreed by the customer | |
| Price or estimate agreed in writing | |
| Deposit received, if required | |
| Address, access instructions, and parking confirmed | |
| Materials and parts ordered or in stock | |
| Date and arrival window sent to the customer | |
| Permits checked, if required | |

## The delivery sign-off

| Item | Confirmed? |
|---|---|
| All scoped work completed | |
| Work area cleaned | |
| Before-and-after photos saved to the job folder | |
| Walkthrough with the customer | |
| Customer questions answered | |
| Any extra work documented and approved in writing | |
| Invoice sent before leaving or the same day | |

## Designing good checklists

Good checklists are short and focus on the steps most likely to be missed or most costly if missed. They use plain words and fit on one page. They are revised when mistakes reveal a missing step. Long checklists that list every obvious action are often ignored.

:::example Illustrative: Rivera's kickoff gate in practice
In one month, Rivera Home Repair scheduled 42 jobs. The kickoff gate caught 5 problems before the crew arrived: 2 missing parts, 1 wrong address, 1 customer who had not approved the price, and 1 job needing a permit.

If each problem had been discovered on site, Luis estimates a wasted trip of 1.5 hours at $45 an hour: 5 × 1.5 × $45 = $337.50 in labor. The gate took about 4 minutes per job: 42 × 4 = 168 minutes, or 2.8 hours, costing 2.8 × $45 = $126. Net estimated savings: $337.50 − $126 = $211.50 for the month, plus fewer frustrated customers. These numbers are illustrative.
:::

:::case Delivery promises and the FTC Mail Order Rule
For businesses that sell goods by mail, internet, or telephone, the Federal Trade Commission's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule requires sellers to have a reasonable basis for stated shipping times, to ship within the time stated or within 30 days if no time is stated, and to notify customers of delays and offer them the option to cancel [4]. Service businesses like Rivera are not covered by that rule, but the principle generalizes: make delivery promises you have a reason to believe you can keep, and tell customers promptly when something changes. A kickoff gate gives a business that reasonable basis.
:::

## Change orders during delivery

Scope often changes mid-job. A customer asks for an extra outlet, or the crew discovers rotted wood behind a fixture. Unrecorded changes are a common source of billing disputes. A simple change-order habit prevents most of them: describe the extra work, state the added price, and get the customer's written approval, even by text message, before doing it. Save the approval to the job folder and list it on the invoice with its date.

| Change-order field | Example |
|---|---|
| Description | Replace rotted subfloor under sink, about 4 square feet |
| Added price | $280 |
| Approved by and how | Customer, by text, 2:14 p.m. |
| Date | Mar 4 |

:::checklist Building your first checklist
- Choose your most common job type.
- Write the steps from memory, then compare with a recent job.
- Circle the steps most often missed or most costly to miss.
- Keep only the circled steps plus kickoff and sign-off basics.
- Test it on the next five jobs.
- Revise after each mistake that the checklist did not catch.
:::

:::summary
A kickoff gate confirms scope, price, deposit, access, materials, schedule, and permits before work begins. A delivery sign-off confirms completion, cleanup, photos, walkthrough, change approvals, and same-day invoicing. Keep checklists short and focused on critical steps. Make delivery promises you can support and communicate changes promptly, a principle reflected in the FTC's Mail Order Rule for goods.
:::

# Chapter 4: Writing SOPs with AI Assistance

:::objectives
- Explain the purpose and structure of a one-page SOP.
- Capture a task using recordings or voice notes.
- Use AI prompts to draft an SOP and check it for accuracy.
- Maintain and version SOPs over time.
:::

An SOP captures how a recurring task is done so that someone else can do it, or so you can do it the same way every time. Many owners avoid writing SOPs because it feels slow. Recording yourself doing the task and asking an AI tool to draft the document can make the process much faster, as long as a person checks the result.

## Key terms

- **SOP**: a written procedure for a recurring task.
- **Trigger**: the event that starts the procedure.
- **Definition of done**: a clear description of what a completed task looks like.
- **Version**: a dated revision of a document.
- **Prompt**: instructions given to an AI tool.

## The one-page SOP structure

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Title and version | Name of task, date, owner |
| Purpose | Why this task matters, in one sentence |
| Trigger | When the task starts |
| Tools and materials | What you need |
| Steps | Numbered, one action each, 5 to 15 steps |
| Definition of done | What "finished" looks like |
| Common mistakes | Two or three things that often go wrong |

:::case Dan Martell's Camcorder Method
In Buy Back Your Time (2023), entrepreneur and coach Dan Martell describes a "Camcorder Method" for delegation: record yourself doing a task, then use the recording to create a checklist and training material so someone else can take the task over [5]. The approach captures knowledge once instead of explaining the task repeatedly. Martell's book describes his own experience and that of clients he has coached; their results are their own. The method is cited here because it pairs well with AI tools that can turn recordings or transcripts into a first draft.
:::

## Master prompts for SOP drafting

Prompt 1, from transcript to draft: "Here is a transcript of me doing a task. Write a one-page SOP with these sections: title, purpose, trigger, tools, numbered steps (one action per step), definition of done, and common mistakes. Use plain language. Do not add steps I did not describe; mark anything unclear with [CHECK]."

Prompt 2, gap check: "Review this SOP. List any steps that seem missing, unclear, or out of order. Ask me questions rather than guessing."

Prompt 3, simplification: "Rewrite these steps for someone on their first week. Keep every step but use shorter sentences."

The instruction to mark uncertain items with [CHECK] and not add steps matters. AI tools can produce confident text that sounds right but is wrong. A person who knows the task must review every step.

:::example Illustrative: the faucet replacement SOP
Luis records a 14-minute video replacing a kitchen faucet and asks an AI tool for a transcript and SOP draft. The draft has 12 steps and 3 items marked [CHECK]. Luis corrects 2 steps (the tool had the order of shutting off water and opening the faucet reversed) and resolves the 3 checks.

Time spent: 14 minutes recording, 5 minutes prompting, 20 minutes reviewing, total 39 minutes. Luis estimates that writing it from scratch would have taken 90 minutes. Savings: 90 − 39 = 51 minutes. More important, the review caught an ordering error that could have caused a leak. The numbers are illustrative.
:::

## Keeping SOPs alive

An SOP that no one updates becomes a source of errors. Put a review date on every SOP, typically every six or twelve months, and update it whenever a mistake reveals a missing or wrong step. Keep the old version in an archive folder, marked with its date, so you can see what changed. Ask the person who does the task most often to suggest edits; they usually know where the document is out of date. AI tools can help by comparing an old SOP with a new transcript and listing differences, but a person decides which changes to accept.

:::worksheet SOP builder
Task name: ___
Trigger (when does it start?): ___
Tools and materials: ___
Recording or notes location: ___
Definition of done: ___
Reviewer and date: ___
Next review date: ___
:::

:::summary
A one-page SOP includes purpose, trigger, tools, steps, definition of done, and common mistakes. Dan Martell's Camcorder Method, recording yourself doing the task, pairs well with AI tools that draft from transcripts. Prompts should tell the tool not to invent steps and to mark uncertainties. A knowledgeable person reviews every SOP, and SOPs are dated and revised as the work changes.
:::

# Chapter 5: Calm Customer-Support Replies

:::objectives
- Explain the principles of calm, accurate support communication.
- Use six core reply scripts for common situations.
- Avoid promises that the business cannot keep.
- Recognize when to escalate to the owner.
:::

Customers contact small businesses with questions, worries, and complaints. A calm, accurate reply can preserve a relationship; a defensive or careless one can damage it. Scripts help staff respond consistently, but they must be adapted to each situation and must never promise things the business has not agreed to.

## Key terms

- **Acknowledgment**: recognizing the customer's concern before explaining.
- **Status update**: a factual message about where a job stands.
- **Policy boundary**: a limit the business maintains, explained politely.
- **Escalation**: passing an issue to someone with more authority.
- **Commitment**: a specific promise, such as a date or a refund, which the business must keep.

## The calm reply framework

| Step | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge | Show you understand | "Thanks for letting us know about the drip." |
| State facts | Share what you know | "Our records show the faucet was installed Tuesday." |
| Next step | Say what happens next | "We can come back Thursday between 9 and 11." |
| Confirm | Close the loop | "Does that window work for you?" |

## Six core scripts

1. Status update: "Hi [Name], a quick update on your [job]. We're on schedule for [date] and will text 30 minutes before arrival."
2. Delay: "Hi [Name], I'm sorry, but the [part] has been delayed by the supplier. The new expected date is [date]. If that doesn't work, reply and we'll find another option."
3. Fix request: "Thanks for telling us about [issue]. We stand behind our work. Can we come back on [date and window] to take a look?"
4. Price question: "Your estimate was [amount] for [scope]. The final invoice is [amount] because [approved change]. Here is the approval from [date]."
5. Policy boundary: "I understand. Our policy is [policy], which we explained in the estimate. Here's what we can do: [option]."
6. Escalation: "I want to make sure this is handled right, so I'm passing it to Luis, the owner. He'll contact you by [time]."

:::case Moffatt v. Air Canada
In a 2024 decision, the British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal considered a claim by Jake Moffatt, who said Air Canada's website chatbot told him he could apply for a bereavement fare discount after travel, which conflicted with the airline's actual policy [6]. Air Canada argued, in effect, that the chatbot was responsible for its own statements. The tribunal rejected that argument, found that Air Canada was responsible for all information on its website, including the chatbot, and ordered the airline to pay damages plus interest and fees [6]. The case is a clear reminder that customer-facing statements, whether written by a person or an AI tool, can be treated as the business's own.
:::

:::example Illustrative: response time and escalation
Rivera Home Repair receives about 60 customer messages a month. Maria tracks them for a month: 36 are status questions, 12 are scheduling changes, 8 are fix requests, and 4 are complaints about price.

With scripts, Maria answers status and scheduling messages in about 3 minutes each: (36 + 12) × 3 = 144 minutes. Fix requests take about 8 minutes: 8 × 8 = 64 minutes. Price complaints are escalated to Luis, who spends about 15 minutes on each: 4 × 15 = 60 minutes. Total: 144 + 64 + 60 = 268 minutes, about 4.5 hours a month. Before the scripts, Maria estimated about 7 hours. These numbers are illustrative.
:::

## De-escalating an upset customer

When a customer is angry, the first goal is to lower the temperature, not to win the argument. Listen fully before responding. Repeat back what you heard: "So the faucet is still dripping, and you had to take time off work for our visit." Apologize for the experience without admitting fault you have not confirmed: "I'm sorry this has been frustrating." Then move to a concrete next step with a time. If the customer becomes abusive, it is acceptable to end the conversation politely and escalate to the owner.

AI tools can help draft a de-escalation reply. A useful prompt is: "Draft a calm reply to this customer message. Acknowledge their frustration, do not admit fault, do not promise refunds or discounts, and offer to schedule a visit. Keep it under 100 words." The reviewer then checks every fact and commitment.

:::checklist Before sending a support reply
- Did I acknowledge the customer's concern?
- Are all facts (dates, prices, scope) checked against our records?
- Am I making any commitment? If so, is it approved?
- Is the tone calm and free of blame?
- Does the message say what happens next?
- Should this be escalated instead?
:::

:::summary
Calm support replies acknowledge, state facts, name the next step, and confirm. Six scripts cover status updates, delays, fix requests, price questions, policy boundaries, and escalation. Never make commitments the business has not approved. The Moffatt v. Air Canada decision shows that statements on a business's website, including those from a chatbot, can be treated as the business's own.
:::

# Chapter 6: Using AI in Customer Support Responsibly

:::objectives
- Summarize research on AI assistance in customer support.
- Describe how a large company reported using an AI assistant.
- Design a draft-review-send workflow.
- Set rules for what AI may and may not say.
:::

AI tools can draft replies, summarize long threads, and suggest next steps. Used well, they save time and help newer staff. Used carelessly, they can invent facts, make unauthorized promises, or mishandle a sensitive complaint.

## Key terms

- **Draft-review-send**: a workflow in which AI drafts, a person reviews and edits, and the person sends.
- **Hallucination**: an AI output that sounds plausible but is false.
- **Grounding**: giving the AI tool approved source material, such as policies and job records, to base its reply on.
- **Guardrail**: a rule limiting what the tool may say or do.

## What the research says

In a study published as a National Bureau of Economic Research working paper, Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond examined the rollout of an AI conversational assistant to more than 5,000 customer support agents and found that it increased productivity, measured as issues resolved per hour, by 14 percent on average, with the largest gains among novice and lower-skilled workers [7]. The study looked at one large company's support operation; results in other settings may differ.

:::case Klarna's AI assistant
In February 2024, the payments company Klarna announced that its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million customer service conversations in its first month, two-thirds of its customer service chats, and said the assistant was doing work equivalent to 700 full-time agents [8]. Klarna also reported that customers resolved their errands in less than 2 minutes, compared with 11 minutes previously [8]. These are the company's own reported figures, made in a press release, and they come from a large firm with substantial engineering resources. A small business should take a narrower lesson: AI can handle routine, well-documented questions, while unusual or sensitive cases still need a person.
:::

## Guardrails for AI-drafted replies

| AI may | AI may not |
|---|---|
| Draft replies using approved scripts and job records | Promise refunds, discounts, or dates without approval |
| Summarize long message threads | Give legal, medical, or safety advice |
| Suggest a tone or wording | Share other customers' information |
| Flag messages that sound angry or urgent | Send messages without human review |
| Translate a reply for review | Request payment details by message |

:::example Illustrative: review time with AI drafts
Maria tests AI drafting on Rivera's 48 routine monthly messages (status and scheduling). Writing from scripts took about 3 minutes each. With AI drafts grounded in the job schedule, she spends about 1.5 minutes reviewing and editing each one.

Time saved: 48 × (3 − 1.5) = 72 minutes a month. During the test, she corrected 4 drafts with wrong dates, a rate of 4 ÷ 48 = about 8 percent. That error rate is why every draft is reviewed before sending. The numbers are illustrative.
:::

## Privacy in AI-assisted support

Customer messages often contain names, addresses, phone numbers, and sometimes sensitive details. Before using an AI tool, review its data-handling terms: whether it stores inputs, whether they are used for training, and who can access them. Share only what the tool needs to draft a reply. Never paste payment card numbers, bank details, or government identification numbers into an AI tool. If a customer sends such information by message, delete it and ask them to use a secure payment link instead.

# Chapter 7: Same-Day Invoicing

:::objectives
- Explain why invoice timing affects cash flow.
- Summarize survey data on late payments to small businesses.
- Apply an invoice quality check before sending.
- Set payment terms and methods that make paying easy.
:::

An invoice not sent is a payment not received. Many small businesses delay invoicing until the weekend or month-end, which adds days or weeks to the time it takes to get paid. Sending the invoice the same day the work is completed, ideally before leaving the site, is one of the simplest cash-flow improvements available.

## Key terms

- **Invoice**: a request for payment listing work done, amounts, terms, and how to pay.
- **Payment terms**: when payment is due, such as "due on receipt" or "net 15."
- **Days sales outstanding (DSO)**: the average number of days it takes to collect payment after a sale.
- **Accounts receivable**: money owed to the business by customers.

## What the data say

The 2025 Intuit QuickBooks Small Business Late Payments Report, based on a January 2025 survey of 2,487 U.S. small businesses with up to 100 employees, found that 56 percent of respondents were owed money from unpaid invoices, averaging $17,500 per business, and that 47 percent had some invoices overdue by more than 30 days [9]. The survey was commissioned by a company that sells invoicing software, so readers should note the potential interest, but the findings are consistent with the general experience that late payment is common.

## The invoice quality check

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Customer name and billing address correct | Avoids rejected or misrouted invoices |
| Job address and date | Helps the customer recognize the work |
| Clear description of work and materials | Reduces questions |
| Any approved changes listed with approval date | Prevents disputes |
| Total, taxes, and deposit applied | Avoids errors |
| Due date and payment terms | Sets expectations |
| Payment methods and a payment link | Makes paying easy |
| Invoice number | Enables tracking |

:::example Illustrative: the value of invoicing faster
Rivera Home Repair used to send invoices every Friday. On average, work was completed 3 days before the invoice went out, and customers paid 12 days after receiving it, so cash arrived about 15 days after completion.

After switching to same-day invoicing, cash arrives about 12 days after completion. Average daily revenue: $16,800 ÷ 30 = $560. Reducing collection time by 3 days means about 3 × $560 = $1,680 arrives sooner and stays in the bank rather than in receivables. This is a one-time improvement in cash on hand, not additional revenue. The figures are illustrative.
:::

:::case Late payment as a common problem
Beyond the QuickBooks survey, a 2025 study by the credit-monitoring firm Creditsafe, based on a poll of over 200 U.S. finance and accounting professionals and reported by CFO Dive, found that 81 percent of businesses typically chase a customer between one and four times to get a single overdue invoice paid [10]. Both surveys come from companies with commercial interests in invoicing and credit services, and their samples differ. Taken together, they suggest that late payment is routine enough to plan for, which is why this book pairs same-day invoicing with a tracker and a reminder sequence.
:::

## Deposits and progress billing

For larger jobs, waiting until completion to bill can strain cash. Two common practices reduce that strain. A deposit, collected at kickoff, covers materials and signals the customer's commitment. Progress billing, invoicing at agreed milestones, matches cash inflows to costs incurred. Both should be described in the written estimate. Some states regulate deposits for home improvement contracts, so check the rules that apply where you work.

| Job size | Possible structure |
|---|---|
| Under $500 | Full payment on completion |
| $500 to $3,000 | Deposit for materials; balance on completion |
| Over $3,000 | Deposit; milestone invoices; final invoice at sign-off |

:::checklist Same-day invoicing habit
- Prepare an invoice template for each common job type.
- Complete the invoice quality check during the delivery sign-off.
- Send the invoice from the job site or the same evening.
- Include a payment link and clear due date.
- Log the invoice in the tracker immediately.
- Save a PDF copy to the invoices folder.
:::

:::summary

Surveys report that more than half of small businesses are owed money from unpaid invoices and that many have invoices more than 30 days overdue. # Chapter 8: The Open-Invoice Tracker and Four Polite Reminders

:::objectives
- Maintain a simple open-invoice tracker.
- Calculate days outstanding and aging buckets.
- Use a four-step reminder sequence with a pause rule.
- Keep collection communications respectful and lawful.
:::

Tracking open invoices turns a vague feeling ("some people owe us") into a list with names, amounts, and next actions. A polite, consistent reminder sequence collects many late payments without damaging relationships.

## Key terms

- **Open invoice**: an invoice sent but not yet paid in full.
- **Aging**: grouping open invoices by how long they have been outstanding.
- **Reminder sequence**: a planned series of messages before and after the due date.
- **Pause rule**: a point at which automated reminders stop and the owner decides what to do.

## The tracker columns

| Column | Example |
|---|---|
| Invoice number | 1047 |
| Customer | Patel |
| Date sent | Mar 4 |
| Amount | $640 |
| Due date | Mar 18 |
| Days past due | 6 |
| Last reminder sent | Reminder 2, Mar 21 |
| Next action and date | Reminder 3, Mar 25 |
| Notes | Asked about payment plan |

## Aging buckets

| Bucket | Typical action |
|---|---|
| Not yet due | Pre-due reminder |
| 1–7 days past due | Friendly reminder |
| 8–14 days past due | Firm reminder and call |
| 15+ days past due | Pause rule: owner review |

## The four polite reminders

Reminder 1, three days before due: "Hi [Name], a friendly note that invoice [number] for [amount] is due on [date]. You can pay here: [link]. Thank you for choosing us."

Reminder 2, one day after due: "Hi [Name], invoice [number] for [amount] was due yesterday. If you've already paid, thank you, and please ignore this. If not, here's the link: [link]."

Reminder 3, seven days after due: "Hi [Name], invoice [number] for [amount] is now a week past due. Please let us know if there's a problem with the work or the invoice; we're glad to help. Otherwise, we'd appreciate payment by [date]: [link]."

Reminder 4, fourteen days after due: "Hi [Name], invoice [number] for [amount] is two weeks past due. I'd like to resolve this with you directly. Could we talk by [date]? Luis Rivera, owner."

After Reminder 4, the pause rule applies: no more automated messages. The owner decides whether to call, offer a payment plan, pause future work, or consult an attorney about further options.

:::warning Collection practices
Collection communications are regulated in many places. Third-party debt collectors are subject to federal rules for consumer debts, and some states also regulate how businesses collect their own debts. Never threaten, harass, misrepresent, or contact third parties about a customer's debt. Seek legal advice before taking formal collection action.
:::

:::example Illustrative: aging Rivera's receivables
On a Friday, Rivera's tracker shows 9 open invoices totaling $4,860:

Not yet due: 4 invoices, $2,100.
1–7 days past due: 3 invoices, $1,420.
8–14 days past due: 1 invoice, $640.
15+ days past due: 1 invoice, $700.

Share past due: ($1,420 + $640 + $700) ÷ $4,860 = $2,760 ÷ $4,860 = 56.8 percent. The $700 invoice triggers the pause rule; Luis calls the customer, who explains a cash-flow problem, and they agree on two payments of $350.
:::

:::case Reminders as normal practice
The Creditsafe survey reported by CFO Dive found that most businesses chase a customer between one and four times to collect a single overdue invoice [10]. That finding supports designing a reminder sequence in advance rather than improvising. A planned sequence keeps tone consistent, removes emotion from the decision of when to follow up, and makes clear when the owner should step in. The survey reflects a sample of finance professionals at businesses of various sizes, so small-business experience may differ.
:::

## Using AI for reminders safely

AI tools and invoicing software can draft and schedule reminders from the tracker. The human-only rule still applies: a person approves the sequence, checks that the invoice is truly unpaid before each reminder goes out, and handles every reply. Payments sometimes arrive between the tracker update and the reminder, and few things irritate a customer more than a past-due notice for an invoice they paid yesterday. A quick check of the payment record before sending prevents this.

# Chapter 9: The 15-Minute Friday Cash Check

:::objectives
- Explain cash buffer days and why they matter.
- Summarize research on small-business cash reserves.
- Conduct a 15-minute weekly cash review.
- Build a simple 4-week cash forecast.
:::

Profit and cash are different. A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash if customers pay late and bills come due. A short weekly review keeps the owner aware of the cash position and upcoming needs.

## Key terms

- **Cash position**: the total cash available in business accounts today.
- **Cash buffer days**: how many days of typical outflows the business could pay from its cash balance if inflows stopped.
- **Cash forecast**: an estimate of cash coming in and going out over the next few weeks.
- **Reserve**: cash set aside for taxes, emergencies, or planned expenses.

## What the research shows

The JPMorgan Chase Institute analyzed more than 470 million transactions from 597,000 small businesses in 2015 and found that the median small business held enough cash to cover 27 days of typical outflows, with a quarter holding 13 days or fewer [11]. Repair and maintenance businesses had a median of 18 cash buffer days [11]. In a 2025 analysis of 2.1 million small businesses with Chase Business Banking accounts, the Institute reported that the typical firm in its nationwide sample held 17.6 cash buffer days [12]. Both analyses cover the bank's own customers, so they may not represent all small businesses.

:::case Cash buffer days
The JPMorgan Chase Institute defines cash buffer days as the ratio of a business's average daily cash balance to its average daily cash outflows, a measure of how long it could continue paying its bills if revenue stopped [11]. Its finding that half of small businesses held less than a month of buffer, and fewer in some industries, highlights how quickly a slow-paying customer or an unexpected expense can strain a small firm [11]. These are research findings about a large sample of bank customers; they describe typical conditions rather than any particular business.
:::

## The 15-minute agenda

| Minutes | Step |
|---|---|
| 0–3 | Record balances in all business accounts |
| 3–6 | Review the open-invoice tracker; note expected receipts this week and next |
| 6–9 | List bills and payroll due in the next 14 days |
| 9–11 | Check the tax reserve and other set-asides |
| 11–13 | Calculate cash buffer days |
| 13–15 | Decide actions: reminders to send, bills to schedule, spending to pause |

:::example Illustrative: Rivera's Friday cash check
Balances: operating account $9,400; tax reserve account $3,200 (not counted as available).

Monthly outflows average $13,500, so average daily outflows are $13,500 ÷ 30 = $450.

Cash buffer days: $9,400 ÷ $450 = about 20.9 days.

Next 14 days: expected receipts $4,200 (from the tracker, counting only invoices likely to be paid); bills and payroll due $6,900. Projected balance in 14 days: $9,400 + $4,200 − $6,900 = $6,700, or $6,700 ÷ $450 = about 14.9 buffer days.

Decision: send reminders on two overdue invoices, delay a $600 tool purchase until the projected balance recovers, and keep the tax reserve untouched.
:::

## The 4-week forecast

| Week | Starting cash | Expected in | Expected out | Ending cash |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $9,400 | $2,300 | $3,600 | $8,100 |
| 2 | $8,100 | $1,900 | $3,300 | $6,700 |
| 3 | $6,700 | $4,100 | $3,200 | $7,600 |
| 4 | $7,600 | $3,800 | $3,400 | $8,000 |

## Setting aside taxes

Self-employed people and many small businesses do not have taxes withheld automatically, so tax money collected through sales arrives mixed with operating cash. A separate tax reserve account, funded with a fixed percentage of each deposit, prevents the common problem of spending money that will be owed later. The right percentage depends on the business and the owner's situation; an accountant can help set it. Count the reserve as unavailable in the Friday cash check.

## Improving the cash buffer

If buffer days are consistently low, the main levers are faster collection, slower or smaller outflows, and retained profit. Faster collection comes from deposits, same-day invoicing, easy payment links, and consistent reminders. Outflows can sometimes be timed: scheduling bills on their due dates rather than early, negotiating vendor terms, and delaying discretionary purchases when the forecast is tight. Retained profit, leaving some earnings in the business rather than withdrawing everything, builds the buffer over time. A target such as 30 or 45 buffer days can guide decisions, but the right level depends on the business's volatility and obligations.

:::checklist Friday cash check
- Record all account balances.
- Separate the tax reserve from available cash.
- Review open invoices and expected receipts.
- List bills and payroll due in 14 days.
- Calculate cash buffer days.
- Update the 4-week forecast.
- Write down this week's three actions.
:::

:::summary
Cash and profit differ. The JPMorgan Chase Institute found that the median small business held about 27 days of cash buffer in 2015, and its 2025 analysis reported about 17.6 days, with lower figures in some industries. A 15-minute Friday review of balances, receivables, upcoming bills, reserves, and buffer days, plus a 4-week forecast, keeps the owner ahead of cash problems.
:::

# Chapter 10: The Vendor Scam Shield

:::objectives
- Recognize fake invoice, unordered merchandise, and payment-change scams.
- Summarize FTC guidance on verifying invoices.
- Apply a three-check rule before paying any bill.
- Train everyone who answers the phone or opens the mail.
:::

Scammers target small businesses because they often lack purchasing departments and formal approval processes. Fake invoices, unordered supplies, and emails requesting changes to payment details are common tactics. A few simple rules, followed every time, block most of them.

## Key terms

- **Fake invoice**: a bill for goods or services the business never ordered.
- **Unordered merchandise**: goods sent without an order, followed by a demand for payment.
- **Payment-change request**: a message asking to send future payments to a new account.
- **Out-of-band verification**: confirming a request through a separate, known channel, such as a phone number already on file.
- **Approved vendor list**: a list of companies the business actually buys from.

## What the FTC warns about

The Federal Trade Commission warns that scammers send small businesses fake invoices for products or services they never ordered, sometimes appearing to come from well-known companies and sometimes marked "past due" to create urgency [13][14]. It advises businesses to make procedures clear for approving purchases and invoices, to check all invoices closely, to limit the number of people who can place orders and pay invoices, and to refuse payment requested by wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or gift cards [13]. The FTC also notes that if a business receives merchandise it did not order, it has a legal right to keep it for free [13].

:::case Unordered office supplies
In 2017, the FTC announced settlements with Maryland-based companies it charged with sending small businesses unordered office supplies and then invoicing them for amounts the businesses did not owe [15]. According to the FTC, one operation offered "free" light bulbs or cleaning supplies and then billed well above retail; another used telemarketers to "verify" a previous order or offer a free sample, then shipped goods and sent invoices [15]. The FTC advised businesses to train staff, designate one person to handle supply inquiries, and investigate every invoice before paying [15]. These were allegations resolved through settlements.
:::

## The three-check rule

| Check | Question | Where to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Ordered | Did someone here actually order this? | Purchase records, job folder |
| Received | Did we receive the goods or service? | Delivery records, crew confirmation |
| Known vendor | Is this a vendor on our approved list, at the address and account on file? | Approved vendor list |

Any invoice failing a check is held until the owner reviews it.

:::example Illustrative: a month of bills
Rivera receives 31 bills in a month. The three-check rule flags 3: a $389 "directory listing renewal" from an unfamiliar company, a $247 invoice for toner the business never ordered, and an email from a known supplier asking to send payment to a new bank account.

Luis searches the directory company's name with "scam" and "complaint" and finds many reports; he does not pay. He calls the toner company's listed number, confirms there was no order, and does not pay. He calls the supplier at the number on file, not the number in the email, and learns the email was fraudulent.

Potential loss avoided: $389 + $247 = $636, plus the supplier payment that would have gone to a fraudster, which was $2,150. Total: $2,786. The numbers are illustrative.
:::

## Training the people who answer the phone

Many supply and directory scams begin with a phone call to whoever answers. The caller asks to "confirm" the address, "verify" an existing order, or speak to the person who handles supplies. Train everyone who answers the phone, including part-time staff and family members, to avoid confirming any order or business detail for an unknown caller and to pass such calls to the one person who handles purchasing. The FTC recommends designating a single person for supply inquiries and training staff in short sessions [15].

:::checklist Vendor scam shield
- Keep an approved vendor list with phone numbers and account details on file.
- Limit who can place orders and approve bills.
- Apply the three-check rule to every bill.
- Verify any payment-change request by calling a known number.
- Never pay by gift card, wire to an unknown account, or cryptocurrency on request.
- Train everyone who answers the phone not to "confirm" orders or details for callers.
- Report fake invoices to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
:::

:::summary
Fake invoices, unordered merchandise, and payment-change requests target small businesses. The FTC advises clear approval procedures, close invoice review, limited payment authority, and refusal of unusual payment methods. The three-check rule, ordered, received, known vendor, plus out-of-band verification of payment changes, blocks most of these scams.
:::

# Chapter 11: Keeping Bank Access Human-Only

:::objectives
- Explain the scale of business email compromise.
- Apply access controls to bank and payment accounts.
- Describe the protective value of multi-factor authentication.
- Combine all tools into a weekly back-office rhythm.
:::

As AI tools become more capable, it can be tempting to let them pay bills or move money. This book recommends against it. Payment authority should remain with named people, protected by strong authentication and a second check for unusual payments.

## Key terms

- **Business email compromise (BEC)**: fraud in which criminals impersonate executives, vendors, or partners by email to redirect payments.
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**: a login that requires something beyond a password, such as a code or security key.
- **Dual control**: requiring two people to approve certain payments.
- **Payment threshold**: an amount above which extra verification is required.

## The scale of the problem

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center reported in its 2025 annual report that it received 1,008,597 complaints with reported losses of about $20.9 billion, and that business email compromise accounted for about $3.05 billion of reported losses [16]. These are reported losses only; many incidents go unreported.

:::case Multi-factor authentication
Microsoft researchers studied the effectiveness of multi-factor authentication across a large set of accounts and found that MFA reduced the risk of account compromise by more than 99.2 percent in the population studied [17]. MFA does not stop every attack, and the results come from one company's data, but it is among the most effective and least expensive protections available. The FTC's small-business cybersecurity resources also recommend multi-factor authentication [18].
:::

## Bank access rules

| Rule | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Only named owners or managers can send payments | Limits who can move money |
| MFA on all bank, payment, and email accounts | Blocks most password-based attacks |
| No AI tool has bank or payment credentials | Keeps money movement human-only |
| Payments above a threshold require a second check | Catches errors and fraud |
| New payees verified by phone at a known number | Blocks payment-change fraud |
| Bank alerts on for all outgoing payments | Enables quick detection |

:::example Illustrative: setting a payment threshold
Rivera's typical vendor payments range from $50 to $1,200. Luis sets a threshold of $1,500: any payment above it, or any payment to a new payee, requires him to call the payee at a known number and wait one business day before sending.

In a year, Rivera makes about 380 payments. Luis estimates 12 exceed the threshold or go to new payees. At 10 minutes per check, the annual time cost is 12 × 10 = 120 minutes, or 2 hours. A single prevented fraudulent payment of $2,150 would be worth far more than 2 hours of the owner's time. The numbers are illustrative.
:::

## The weekly back-office rhythm

| Day | Task | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Daily | Kickoff gate and delivery sign-off; same-day invoices | Built into jobs |
| Daily | Review and send AI-drafted support replies | 15–30 minutes |
| Tuesday | Send scheduled payment reminders | 10 minutes |
| Thursday | Apply three-check rule to bills; schedule payments | 15 minutes |
| Friday | 15-minute cash check and forecast | 15 minutes |
| Monthly | Review SOPs, AI corrections, and access list | 1 hour |

## What to do if fraud happens

Speed matters. If you suspect a fraudulent payment, call your bank immediately using the number on your card or statement and ask about recalling or stopping the payment. Change passwords on affected accounts and check that MFA is enabled. Preserve emails and records. Report business email compromise to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov and report scams to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Afterward, review which control failed and update the relevant SOP.

## Prompt-writing principles

Good prompts state the task, the source material to use, the format, and the limits. Limits matter most: "do not change the date," "no discounts," "mark unclear items." Ask the tool to flag uncertainty rather than guess. Keep a short log of prompts that work well and of errors the reviewer caught, and refine the prompts monthly.

# Glossary

**Accounts receivable**: money owed to the business by customers.

**Aging**: grouping open invoices by how long they have been outstanding.

**Business email compromise (BEC)**: email fraud that impersonates executives, vendors, or partners to redirect payments.

**Cash buffer days**: the number of days of typical outflows a business could pay from its cash balance if inflows stopped.

**Cash forecast**: an estimate of cash coming in and going out over the next few weeks.

**Checklist**: a written list of critical steps used during work to prevent omissions.

**Delivery sign-off**: a closing checklist completed with the customer at the end of a job.

**Draft-review-send**: a workflow in which AI drafts, a person reviews, and the person sends.

**Dual control**: requiring two people to approve certain payments.

**Grounding**: giving an AI tool approved source material to base its output on.

**Hallucination**: an AI output that sounds plausible but is false.

**Human-in-the-loop**: a design in which a person approves AI output before it takes effect.

**Kickoff gate**: a checklist completed before work begins.

**Least privilege**: giving each person or tool only the access it needs.

**Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**: a login requiring more than a password.

**Out-of-band verification**: confirming a request through a separate, known channel.

**Pause rule**: the point at which automated reminders stop and the owner decides next steps.

**Payment terms**: when an invoice is due.

**Standard operating procedure (SOP)**: a written procedure for a recurring task.

**Unordered merchandise**: goods sent without an order, followed by a payment demand.
