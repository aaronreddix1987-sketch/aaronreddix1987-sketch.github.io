# How to Use This Book

Consulting is one of the oldest knowledge businesses and one of the easiest to start. A person with expertise, a telephone, and a willing client can be in business by the end of the week. That low barrier is both the attraction and the danger. Because almost anyone can call themselves a consultant, clients are cautious, prices are contested, and many new practices stall after a handful of projects done for friends at a discount.

This book is written for experienced professionals who want to turn their expertise into an independent advisory practice: former managers, engineers, marketers, operations leaders, accountants, technologists, and specialists of every kind. It is also useful for owners of small agencies who want to add higher-value advisory work. The book treats consulting the way a university course in professional services management would. It explains why clients buy advice, how to define and validate a focused offer, how to price, how to write proposals and contracts that protect both parties, how to set up the business properly, how to deliver engagements, and how to build systems so the practice does not depend on the consultant's every hour.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this book you should be able to:

- Explain what clients are actually buying when they hire a consultant.
- Choose a niche defined by a specific client, a specific problem, and a specific outcome.
- Validate demand through structured discovery interviews before investing heavily.
- Design an offer with a clear diagnostic, a defined deliverable, and an implementation path.
- Compare hourly, project, value-based, and retainer pricing and choose deliberately.
- Write a proposal and statement of work with scope, exclusions, assumptions, and change control.
- Set up the legal, tax, banking, and record-keeping foundations of the practice.
- Market the practice honestly, in compliance with federal rules on endorsements and reviews.
- Deliver an engagement from kickoff to close-out and capture lessons and referrals.
- Build leverage through documented processes and delegation.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, explains its concepts in plain language, and gives a step-by-step framework. A worked example applies the framework with numbers; worked examples are illustrative and hypothetical, and the people and firms in them are invented. Case studies describe the publicly documented ideas and practices of real consultants and entrepreneurs, with sources listed in the References chapter. Where a real person is discussed, their results are their own, not TTI's, and are not a prediction of yours. Each chapter includes a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Appendices provide usable templates.

Some fields, such as law, accounting, engineering, investment advice, and health care, are regulated, and advising in them may require a license. Consult a licensed attorney, a CPA, and an insurance professional before you act. No income, client, or result is promised.

# Chapter 1: What Clients Buy When They Buy Advice

:::objectives
- Define consulting and distinguish it from employment, contracting, and coaching.
- Describe the market for management advice using public labor data.
- Explain the four things clients buy: expertise, perspective, capacity, and certainty.
- Identify the consultant's main risks and how this book addresses them.
:::

## A definition

A consultant is an independent professional who is paid to help a client understand a problem and decide or implement a better course of action. The essential features are independence (the consultant is not the client's employee), expertise (the consultant knows something the client needs), and accountability for a defined outcome or deliverable rather than for being present a certain number of hours.

Consulting overlaps with several neighboring roles. A contractor or freelancer typically performs defined tasks the client has already specified. A coach typically helps an individual develop their own capabilities. An employee works under the employer's direction and control. In practice, many independent practitioners blend these roles, but clarity matters: the role you claim shapes the price you can charge, the contract you sign, and the tax treatment of the relationship.

## The market for management advice

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics classifies people who "recommend ways to improve an organization's efficiency" as management analysts, a category that includes many management consultants [1]. BLS reports that there were about 1,077,100 management analyst jobs in 2025, that the median annual wage was $101,860 in May 2025, and that employment is projected to grow 10 percent from 2025 to 2035, much faster than average [1]. The lowest 10 percent earned less than $60,640 and the highest 10 percent more than $171,640 [1].

These figures describe wage and salary employment, not independent practice, and they say nothing about what any individual will earn. They are useful for two reasons. First, they confirm that organizations spend substantial sums on outside and inside advisory work. Second, they offer a reference point when you think about pricing: if an organization would pay an employee in a comparable role roughly $100,000 a year plus benefits and overhead, a short engagement that solves a specific problem can be valued against that alternative.

## What clients are really buying

| What the client buys | What it means | Implication for the consultant |
|---|---|---|
| Expertise | Knowledge or skill the client lacks | Be specific about what you know and how you know it |
| Perspective | An outside, unbiased view | Protect your independence; say unwelcome things tactfully |
| Capacity | Extra hands for a defined period | Define scope tightly; capacity work invites scope creep |
| Certainty | Reduced risk of a costly mistake | Use evidence, methods, and references to lower perceived risk |

Most engagements involve more than one of these. A company hiring a consultant to redesign its sales process may want expertise in sales operations, an outside view of its leadership's assumptions, extra capacity to document processes, and confidence that the redesign will not damage current revenue. A consultant who recognizes all four can design an offer and a proposal that address each.

## The consultant's main risks

New consultants commonly encounter five problems: an offer so broad that no one understands it; too few qualified conversations; prices set by guesswork; scope that expands without additional payment; and a practice that collapses when the consultant is busy delivering and stops marketing. The chapters that follow address each in turn: niche (Chapter 2), validation (Chapter 3), offer design (Chapter 4), pricing (Chapter 5), proposals and contracts (Chapter 6), setup (Chapter 7), marketing (Chapter 8), delivery (Chapter 9), ethics (Chapter 10), leverage (Chapter 11), financial management (Chapter 12), and a launch plan (Chapter 13).

:::example Illustrative: four motives in one engagement
A hypothetical regional dental group with six offices hires an operations consultant to reduce missed appointments. The group's executives want expertise in scheduling systems (expertise), an independent view of why front-desk practices differ by office (perspective), help documenting a standard confirmation process (capacity), and assurance that the change will not alienate patients (certainty). The consultant's proposal therefore includes a two-week diagnostic, a comparison across offices, a written standard operating procedure, and a pilot in one office before rollout. All details are hypothetical.
:::

:::checklist Self-assessment: am I offering consulting?
- I can name a specific problem I help clients solve.
- I can explain the expertise or experience that qualifies me.
- I am accountable for an outcome or deliverable, not just hours present.
- I work independently and control how the work is done.
- I know whether my field requires a professional license.
:::

:::summary
Consulting is independent, expert work accountable for a defined outcome. Public labor data show a large and growing market for management advice, but they predict nothing about an individual practice. Clients buy expertise, perspective, capacity, and certainty; a strong practice designs its offer around all four and guards against the common risks of vagueness, thin pipelines, guesswork pricing, scope creep, and dependence on the consultant's time.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choosing a Niche and a Narrow Outcome

:::objectives
- Explain why specialization improves marketing, pricing, and delivery.
- Define a niche using client, problem, and outcome.
- Evaluate candidate niches with a weighted scoring method.
- Write a one-sentence positioning statement.
:::

## The economics of specialization

A generalist must persuade every prospect that they understand that prospect's situation. A specialist begins with that understanding. Specialization shortens the sales conversation, makes referrals easier ("you should talk to the person who fixes scheduling for dental groups"), allows reuse of tools and templates, and supports higher prices because the client perceives lower risk. The cost of specialization is a smaller addressable market and the discomfort of saying no to work outside it. For most new practices, the benefits outweigh the costs.

## Three dimensions of a niche

A useful niche has three parts:

1. **A specific client.** Industry, size, role of the buyer, and sometimes geography. "Owner-operated home service businesses with 10 to 50 employees" is specific; "small businesses" is not.
2. **A specific problem.** Something the client recognizes, can describe, and is already spending money or time on. "Leads are not followed up within a day" is a problem; "growth" is not.
3. **A specific outcome.** A measurable improvement the client would value. "Every new inquiry contacted within 15 minutes during business hours" is an outcome; "better processes" is not.

## Sources of niche ideas

The best niches usually come from the consultant's own history: industries where you have worked, problems you have solved repeatedly, results you can document from past employment, and networks that already trust you. Avoid choosing a niche solely because it appears lucrative; without credibility and access, a lucrative niche is simply expensive to enter.

## The niche evaluation framework

**Step 1: List candidates.** Write five to ten client-problem-outcome combinations drawn from your experience.

**Step 2: Define criteria.** Typical criteria are credibility (your evidence and experience), access (your existing relationships), urgency (how painful the problem is), ability to pay, and repeatability (whether the same solution can be reused).

**Step 3: Weight the criteria** according to what matters most for your situation.

**Step 4: Score each candidate** from 1 to 5 on each criterion.

**Step 5: Choose the top one or two** and test them through discovery interviews (Chapter 3).

| Criterion | Weight | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Credibility | 25% | Can I show evidence that I have solved this problem before? |
| Access | 25% | Do I know at least 20 people who fit or can introduce me? |
| Urgency | 20% | Is the problem costing the client money or risk now? |
| Ability to pay | 15% | Do these clients have budgets for outside help? |
| Repeatability | 15% | Will the same method work across many clients? |

:::example Illustrative: scoring three niches
A hypothetical former operations director, Elena, scores three candidates.

| Candidate | Credibility | Access | Urgency | Ability to pay | Repeatability | Weighted score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lead follow-up for home service firms (10–50 staff) | 4 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 5 | 4.25 |
| Supply-chain redesign for mid-size manufacturers | 5 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 3 | 3.60 |
| General "business coaching" for startups | 2 | 3 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2.25 |

The first candidate's score is (4 × 0.25) + (5 × 0.25) + (4 × 0.20) + (3 × 0.15) + (5 × 0.15) = 1.00 + 1.25 + 0.80 + 0.45 + 0.75 = 4.25. Elena chooses it and keeps manufacturing as a possible second niche. Scores are illustrative.
:::

## The positioning statement

Summarize the chosen niche in one sentence: "I help [specific client] [achieve specific outcome] by [method or approach], so they can [ultimate benefit]." For example: "I help owner-operated home service companies respond to every new inquiry within 15 minutes by redesigning their intake process and tools, so fewer paid leads go to waste." The sentence should be understandable to a prospect without explanation.

Candidate 3 (client / problem / outcome): ___
Weighted scores: ___

Positioning statement: ___
:::

:::summary
Specialization reduces sales friction and supports better pricing and delivery. A niche is defined by a specific client, a specific problem, and a measurable outcome. A weighted scoring method makes the choice explicit, and a one-sentence positioning statement communicates it. The choice is a hypothesis to be tested, not a permanent commitment.

# Chapter 3: Validating Demand Through Discovery Interviews

:::objectives
- Explain why validation should precede heavy investment in branding or content.
- Conduct structured discovery interviews that surface real problems and budgets.
- Distinguish evidence of demand from polite interest.
- Decide whether to proceed, adjust, or abandon a niche.
:::

## Why validate first

Many new consultants spend weeks on logos, websites, and social media before speaking to a single potential client. Validation reverses the order. A few dozen structured conversations will tell you whether the problem is real, how clients describe it, what they have tried, what they spend, and who decides. That information makes every later investment, from the website to the proposal, more effective.

## Interview principles

- **Ask about the past, not the future.** "What happened the last time a lead went unanswered?" yields better evidence than "Would you buy a service that fixes lead response?"
- **Listen more than you speak.** Aim for the interviewee to talk at least 70 percent of the time.
- **Seek specifics.** Numbers, dates, tools, people, and costs.
- **Do not pitch.** The goal is learning. If the interviewee asks for help, note it and schedule a separate conversation.
- **Ask for referrals.** End by asking who else experiences the problem.

## The discovery interview framework

**Step 1: Recruit** 15 to 25 people who fit the niche, starting with your existing network.

**Step 2: Request 20 to 30 minutes** and explain that you are researching the problem, not selling.

**Step 3: Use a consistent guide** (Appendix A) so answers can be compared.

**Step 4: Record notes immediately**, using the interviewee's own words where possible.

**Step 5: Code the results** by problem severity, current spending, decision maker, and buying triggers.

**Step 6: Decide** using predefined thresholds.

## Signals of real demand

| Strong signal | Weak signal |
|---|---|
| The client describes a recent, specific incident | The client agrees the problem "sounds important" |
| The client has already spent money or staff time trying to fix it | The client has never tried to solve it |
| The client can estimate the cost of the problem | The client cannot quantify it |
| The client asks how you would solve it or requests a follow-up | The client offers general encouragement |
| The client introduces you to others with the problem | The client offers no referrals |

:::example Illustrative: coding twenty interviews
Elena, the hypothetical consultant from Chapter 2, interviews 20 home service owners. She sets thresholds in advance: proceed if at least 10 describe a recent incident, at least 6 have spent money on a fix, and at least 3 request a follow-up.

| Signal | Count of 20 |
|---|---|
| Recent specific incident described | 14 |
| Already spent money (software, answering service, staff) | 9 |
| Could estimate cost of the problem | 7 |
| Requested follow-up conversation | 5 |
| Offered at least one referral | 8 |

All thresholds are met. Several owners estimated that each missed lead represented $300 to $1,200 in lost gross profit. Elena also learns that owners describe the problem as "phones ringing out while we're on jobs," a phrase she adopts in her marketing. Figures are illustrative.
:::

## When the evidence says no

If the thresholds are not met, resist the temptation to explain the results away. Either adjust a dimension of the niche (a different client size, a narrower problem) and interview again, or move to the next candidate. A niche abandoned after twenty conversations is far cheaper than one abandoned after a year.

:::checklist Validation readiness
- Thresholds for proceeding were written down before interviews began.
- At least 15 interviews completed with people who fit the niche.
- Notes capture the interviewees' own words.
- Results coded by severity, spending, decision maker, and triggers.
- Decision recorded: proceed, adjust, or abandon.
:::

:::summary
Validation through structured discovery interviews tests whether a niche's problem is real, costly, and funded before major investment. Interviews focus on past behavior and specifics, avoid pitching, and end with referral requests. Predefined thresholds turn conversations into a disciplined decision.
:::

# Chapter 4: Designing the Offer

:::objectives
- Structure an offer as a sequence of diagnostic, prescription, and implementation.
- Apply a value framework to increase perceived value without increasing hours.
- Define deliverables, timelines, and success measures precisely.
- Create a small entry offer that lowers the risk of a first engagement.
:::

## From expertise to a product

Clients find it hard to buy "expertise." They find it much easier to buy a defined service with a name, a scope, a timeline, and a price. Productizing your expertise means packaging it so a client knows exactly what they will receive. This does not make the work generic; it makes the structure predictable while the content remains tailored.

## The three-stage offer

| Stage | Purpose | Typical deliverable | Typical length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diagnostic | Understand the current state and quantify the problem | Findings report with prioritized recommendations | 1–3 weeks |
| Prescription | Design the solution | Process maps, plans, specifications, or a roadmap | 2–6 weeks |
| Implementation | Install the solution and transfer ownership | Working process, trained staff, measured results | 1–6 months |

A paid diagnostic is often the best entry point. It is small enough for a client to approve quickly, it gives the consultant real data for pricing later stages, and it produces value on its own even if the client does not continue.

## A value framework

Entrepreneur and author Alex Hormozi popularized a "value equation" that expresses perceived value as the product of the dream outcome and the perceived likelihood of achieving it, divided by the product of the time delay and the effort and sacrifice required [2]. The framework is a heuristic, not a scientific law, but it is useful for consultants because it shows four levers for raising value:

- **Increase the dream outcome** by tying the work to results the client cares about most.
- **Increase perceived likelihood** with evidence, methods, references, and guarantees on what you control.
- **Reduce time delay** by delivering early wins and shortening the path to first value.
- **Reduce effort and sacrifice** by doing more of the work for the client, providing templates, and minimizing disruption.

:::case The value equation applied to advisory work
In a podcast episode discussing his book on offers, Hormozi described value as driven by four factors: two that should be increased (the dream outcome and the perceived likelihood of achievement) and two that should be decreased (time delay and effort and sacrifice) [2]. For a consultant, the practical lesson is that perceived value can often be raised without adding hours: a clearer statement of outcomes, stronger evidence, an early quick win, and done-for-you templates all move the levers. Hormozi's results are his own, not TTI's, and the framework does not guarantee any sale.
:::

## Defining deliverables

Every deliverable should be described so that both parties can tell whether it has been delivered. Weak: "Process improvement recommendations." Strong: "A written report of no more than 20 pages containing a current-state process map, a quantified estimate of lost leads, and no fewer than five prioritized recommendations with estimated cost and effort, delivered within 15 business days of receiving data."

## Success measures

Distinguish between what you control and what the client controls. You can commit to delivering a process, training staff, and configuring tools. You usually cannot guarantee that the client's staff will follow the process or that the market will respond. Define leading indicators you can influence (for example, percentage of inquiries logged in the new system) and lagging indicators the client will track (for example, booked jobs).

## The offer design framework

**Step 1: Name the offer** in client language.

**Step 2: Define the stages** and which are included.

**Step 3: Specify deliverables** for each stage.

**Step 4: Set timelines** and client responsibilities.

**Step 5: Apply the four value levers** to strengthen the offer.

**Step 6: Define success measures** and separate controllable from uncontrollable factors.

:::example Illustrative: Elena's entry offer
Elena designs a "Lead Response Diagnostic" for home service businesses. Deliverables: a review of 60 days of call and form data; a map of the current intake process; secret-shopper tests of five inquiries; a findings report with an estimate of missed inquiries and their likely cost; and a 60-minute presentation with prioritized recommendations. Timeline: 10 business days after data access. Client responsibilities: export of call logs and form submissions, one 45-minute interview with the owner and one with the office manager. Price: a fixed fee (Chapter 5). The diagnostic leads naturally to an implementation offer: "Lead Response Installation," a six-week engagement. Details are hypothetical.
:::

Deliverable 3: ___

:::summary
A productized offer gives clients a named, scoped, priced service. The diagnostic-prescription-implementation sequence lowers buying risk, and a paid diagnostic is often the best entry point. Hormozi's value equation identifies four levers that raise perceived value without necessarily adding hours. Deliverables and success measures should be verifiable and should separate what the consultant controls from what the client controls.

# Chapter 5: Pricing Advisory Work

:::objectives
- Compare hourly, daily, project, value-based, and retainer pricing.
- Calculate a floor price from costs, taxes, and utilization.
- Apply value-based reasoning to set prices above the floor.
- Present prices in options that make decisions easier.
:::

## Pricing models

| Model | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly or daily | Client pays for time | Simple; low risk for consultant | Rewards slowness; caps income; invites scrutiny of hours |
| Fixed project fee | Single price for defined scope | Predictable for client; rewards efficiency | Consultant bears overrun risk |
| Value-based fee | Price tied to value of outcome to client | Aligns with client results; higher ceiling | Requires trust and clear value conversation |
| Retainer | Recurring fee for access or ongoing work | Predictable revenue | Must define what is included |
| Performance or contingency | Fee depends on results | Attractive to some clients | Consultant bears risks outside their control |

## The floor price

Before thinking about value, know your floor: the minimum price below which the practice loses money. Start with annual targets and costs.

1. **Target owner compensation** before taxes.
2. **Self-employment tax.** Self-employed individuals generally pay self-employment tax as well as income tax [15]. The IRS describes the self-employment tax rate as 15.3 percent, consisting of 12.4 percent for Social Security and 2.9 percent for Medicare, applied to net earnings subject to limits [9].
3. **Business expenses**: insurance, software, professional fees, equipment, marketing, travel, and benefits you must now fund yourself.
4. **Billable capacity.** Independent consultants spend substantial time on marketing, sales, administration, and learning. Utilization of 50 to 60 percent of working hours is a common planning assumption for a solo practice.

:::example Illustrative: computing a floor rate
Elena targets $110,000 in pre-tax owner compensation. She estimates business expenses of $24,000 (insurance, software, accounting, health insurance premiums, marketing) and sets aside a planning allowance for self-employment tax. She plans to work 46 weeks × 40 hours = 1,840 hours and assumes 55 percent utilization, or about 1,012 billable hours.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Target compensation | $110,000 |
| Business expenses | $24,000 |
| Self-employment tax allowance (approximately 15.3% of 92.35% of $110,000) | $15,543 |
| Required annual revenue | $149,543 |
| Billable hours | 1,012 |
| Floor rate per billable hour | $147.77 |

Her floor is about $148 per hour. Income tax, retirement savings, and a cash reserve would push the real requirement higher. These figures are illustrative planning numbers, not tax advice; a CPA should confirm the treatment for her situation.
:::

## Value-based pricing

Consultant and author Alan Weiss, who has written more than 60 books, has argued for decades that consultants should price according to their contribution to the value the client receives rather than by time [3]. He defines a value-based fee as "a fee that is based on your contribution to the results the client achieves," and argues that time-based billing creates a conflict, because the client benefits from fast resolution while the hourly provider benefits from longer engagements [3].

:::case Alan Weiss on value-based fees
Weiss has published a formula in which monetized tangible outcomes are multiplied by how long the benefit lasts, intangible outcomes are weighed by their emotional impact, and peripheral benefits are added; the total, divided by the fee, should represent a strong return on investment for the buyer [4]. In a podcast interview he described aiming for at least a ten-to-one return for the client and stressed that value conversations must be held with the "economic buyer," the person who can actually authorize payment [5]. He has also said he dislikes performance or contingency fees because he cannot control variables such as key people leaving or competitors' technology [6]. Weiss's views and results are his own, not TTI's; many consultants use hybrid approaches, and some clients' procurement rules require hourly rates.
:::

## Applying value reasoning

**Step 1: Agree on objectives** with the economic buyer.

**Step 2: Ask how the client measures success** and what achieving it is worth, in money and in other terms.

**Step 3: Estimate a conservative value range**, including how long benefits last.

**Step 4: Set a fee** that is well above your floor and represents a strong, conservative return for the client.

**Step 5: Offer options** at different scope and price levels.

:::example Illustrative: three options for an installation engagement
After the diagnostic, Elena's hypothetical client agrees that missed inquiries are costing roughly 25 booked jobs a year at an average gross profit of $800, or about $20,000 annually, and that the benefit of a fix should last at least three years. Conservatively, the value is in the range of $40,000 to $60,000 over three years.

| Option | Scope | Price |
|---|---|---|
| A: Blueprint | Written intake process, scripts, and tool configuration plan; client implements | $6,500 |
| B: Installation | Option A plus configuration, staff training, and 30 days of monitoring | $12,500 |
| C: Installation plus quarterly review | Option B plus quarterly reviews for one year | $18,500 |

Option B at $12,500 represents about one-quarter of the conservative low end of the value range, and it equals roughly 85 hours at her floor rate; Elena estimates it will take 55 hours. Figures are illustrative; actual value depends on the client's execution and market.
:::

:::checklist Pricing review
- Floor rate calculated from compensation, expenses, taxes, and utilization.
- Objectives and value agreed with the economic buyer.
- Value estimate is conservative and documented.
- Fee comfortably exceeds the floor.
- Two or three options presented.
- Payment terms specified (deposit, milestones, due dates).
:::

:::summary
Consultants can price by time, project, value, retainer, or performance. A floor rate grounded in compensation goals, expenses, self-employment tax, and realistic utilization prevents unprofitable work. Value-based reasoning, as advocated by Alan Weiss, sets fees according to the client's conservatively estimated return, discussed with the economic buyer. Presenting options helps clients choose scope rather than debate price.
:::

# Chapter 6: Proposals, Scope, and Contracts

:::objectives
- Write a proposal that confirms the client's objectives, value, and options.
- Draft a statement of work with scope, exclusions, assumptions, and responsibilities.
- Use change control to manage scope creep.
- Identify core contract terms to review with an attorney.
:::

## The proposal confirms; it does not persuade

A proposal should summarize what has already been discussed and agreed in conversation with the economic buyer. If the proposal contains surprises, the conversation was incomplete. Treating the proposal as a written summary of an agreement already reached, rather than as a sales brochure, also makes proposals shorter and faster to approve.

## Proposal structure

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Situation | The client's current circumstances in their words |
| Objectives | The outcomes the engagement will produce |
| Measures of success | How progress will be measured |
| Value | What achieving the objectives is worth to the client |
| Methodology and options | What you will do, in two or three options |
| Timing | Start date, milestones, end date |
| Joint accountabilities | What you and the client will each provide |
| Terms | Fees, payment schedule, expenses, cancellation |
| Acceptance | Signature or written approval |

## The statement of work

The statement of work (SOW) translates the chosen option into operational detail. It should state:

- **Scope**: deliverables, activities, and quantities.
- **Exclusions**: related work that is not included.
- **Assumptions**: conditions the plan depends on, such as timely data access.
- **Client responsibilities**: people, data, decisions, and deadlines.
- **Acceptance**: how each deliverable will be reviewed and accepted.
- **Change control**: how changes are requested, priced, and approved.

## Change control

Scope creep is rarely malicious; it usually results from enthusiasm and ambiguity. A written change process protects the relationship. When the client requests work outside scope, the consultant acknowledges the request, describes its effect on time and price, and proceeds only after written approval.

:::example Illustrative: a change order
Midway through Elena's hypothetical installation engagement, the owner asks her to also redesign the company's online booking page. The SOW excludes "website design or development." Elena writes a one-page change order: description (review the booking page and specify changes for the client's web developer), effect (adds 6 hours and 4 business days), price ($1,200 fixed), and approval line. The owner signs; the relationship remains positive because expectations were clear from the start. Details are hypothetical.
:::

## Contract terms to review with an attorney

| Term | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Independent contractor status | Clarifies the relationship; the actual working arrangement also matters |
| Payment terms and late fees | Protects cash flow |
| Intellectual property | Who owns deliverables and pre-existing tools |
| Confidentiality | Protects client information and yours |
| Limitation of liability | Caps exposure, often to fees paid |
| Indemnification | Allocates responsibility for third-party claims |
| Termination | How either party may end the engagement and what is owed |
| Non-solicitation | Whether either party may hire the other's staff |
| Dispute resolution and governing law | Where and how disputes are resolved |

Template agreements are a starting point only. Contract law varies by state, and an attorney can tailor terms to your field's risks.

:::checklist Proposal and SOW review
- Objectives and value were agreed in conversation before writing.
- Two or three options are presented.
- Deliverables are specific and verifiable.
- Exclusions and assumptions are explicit.
- Client responsibilities and deadlines are stated.
- Acceptance and change control are defined.
- Payment terms and deposit are clear.
- Contract reviewed by an attorney at least once.
:::

:::summary
A proposal confirms an agreement already reached with the economic buyer. The statement of work defines scope, exclusions, assumptions, responsibilities, acceptance, and change control. A written change process preserves both margins and relationships. Core contract terms such as intellectual property, liability, and termination should be reviewed with an attorney.
:::

# Chapter 7: Setting Up the Business

:::objectives
- Compare business structures and their liability and tax implications.
- Identify registrations, licenses, and permits to verify.
- Plan for estimated tax payments.
- Establish banking, bookkeeping, insurance, and record-keeping systems.
:::

## Choosing a structure

The SBA notes that business structure affects how much you pay in taxes, your ability to raise money, the paperwork you need to file, and your personal liability [7]. A sole proprietorship is easy to form and gives complete control, but the owner can be held personally liable for business debts and obligations [7]. A limited liability company generally protects owners from personal liability in most instances, while profits can pass through to personal income; LLC members are considered self-employed and pay self-employment tax [7].

| Structure | Liability | Taxes (general) | Consider when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Unlimited personal liability [7] | Personal and self-employment tax [7] | Testing an idea with low risk |
| Single-member LLC | Owner generally not personally liable [7] | Pass-through by default; self-employment tax [7] | Moderate risk; separation desired |
| S corporation election | Owners not personally liable [7] | Personal tax; payroll requirements | Profits high enough to justify added compliance (ask a CPA) |
| C corporation | Owners not personally liable [7] | Corporate tax [7] | Raising outside capital |

Liability protection is not absolute. Personal negligence, personal guarantees, and failure to keep business and personal affairs separate can expose owners. An attorney and CPA can help you choose.

## Registrations and licenses

- Register the entity with your state if you form an LLC or corporation.
- Obtain an Employer Identification Number from the IRS if required for your structure or if you will hire.
- Register a trade name if you operate under a name other than your legal name.
- Check city or county business license requirements.
- Determine whether your advisory field requires a professional license.

## Taxes

Self-employed people generally must file an annual return and pay estimated taxes quarterly; a return is required if net earnings from self-employment were $400 or more [15]. The IRS states that individuals, including sole proprietors, partners, and S corporation shareholders, generally must make estimated payments if they expect to owe $1,000 or more when they file [8]. The year is divided into four payment periods with due dates, and underpayment can result in a penalty even if a refund is due at filing [8].

:::example Illustrative: a tax set-aside rule
Elena opens a separate savings account for taxes and transfers 30 percent of every client payment into it on the day it arrives. In her first quarter she collects $28,000, so she transfers $8,400, from which she pays her first quarterly estimate after her CPA calculates it using Form 1040-ES. The percentage is a planning rule of thumb chosen with her CPA, not a universal rate. Figures are illustrative.
:::

## Banking, bookkeeping, and records

- Open a dedicated business bank account and card; never mix personal and business transactions.
- Use accounting software and reconcile monthly.
- Keep signed proposals, SOWs, change orders, invoices, receipts, and correspondence.
- Track time even under fixed or value pricing, to learn your actual effort and improve future estimates.

## Insurance

Common coverages for consultants include professional liability (errors and omissions), general liability, cyber liability if you handle client data, and business owner's policies. Some clients require specific coverage before signing. An insurance professional can advise on limits.

:::checklist Business setup
- Structure chosen with professional advice.
- State registration and EIN completed as applicable.
- Local business license verified.
- Professional licensing requirements checked.
- Business bank account opened.
- Accounting software set up; monthly reconciliation scheduled.
- Tax set-aside account and quarterly estimate calendar created.
- Insurance quotes obtained and coverage bound.
- Document templates (proposal, SOW, change order, invoice) ready.
:::

:::summary
Business structure affects liability, taxes, and paperwork; the SBA's comparison is a useful starting point, but professional advice is essential. Registrations, local licenses, and professional licensing rules must be verified. Self-employed consultants generally pay estimated taxes quarterly, so a disciplined set-aside rule protects cash. Separate banking, monthly bookkeeping, complete records, and appropriate insurance complete the foundation.
:::

# Chapter 8: Winning Clients Honestly

:::objectives
- Build a client acquisition system around referrals, outreach, and expertise content.
- Track a simple pipeline with conversion rates.
- Use testimonials and case examples in compliance with FTC rules.
- Avoid marketing claims that create legal and reputational risk.

## Where consulting clients come from

For most independent consultants, early clients come from people who already know and trust them: former colleagues, past employers, vendors, and professional associations. Over time, referrals from satisfied clients, visible expertise (articles, talks, webinars, podcasts), and targeted outreach supplement the network. Paid advertising can work for some productized offers, but trust-based channels usually dominate high-value advisory work.

| Channel | How it works | Time to results | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warm network | Personal outreach to people who know you | Weeks | Best first channel; be specific about who you help |
| Referrals | Introductions from clients and partners | Months | Ask systematically at the right moments |
| Expertise content | Articles, talks, newsletters | Months to years | Builds credibility; consistency matters |
| Targeted outreach | Personalized messages to defined prospects | Weeks to months | Lead with insight, not a pitch; follow anti-spam and telemarketing rules |
| Partnerships | Alliances with complementary providers | Months | Define referral terms in writing |
| Speaking and associations | Industry events and groups | Months | High trust; requires preparation |

## The pipeline

Track every opportunity through stages: contact, conversation, diagnostic proposed, diagnostic signed, implementation proposed, implementation signed. Conversion rates between stages reveal where to improve.

:::example Illustrative: a quarterly pipeline
In her first quarter, Elena contacts 60 people in her network and niche. Twenty-four agree to conversations; nine are proposed diagnostics; five sign at $2,500 each; three proceed to implementation at an average of $11,000.

| Stage | Count | Conversion from prior stage |
|---|---|---|
| Contacted | 60 | — |
| Conversations | 24 | 40% |
| Diagnostics proposed | 9 | 38% |
| Diagnostics signed | 5 | 56% |
| Implementations signed | 3 | 60% |

Quarterly revenue booked: (5 × $2,500) + (3 × $11,000) = $12,500 + $33,000 = $45,500. The weakest step is conversations to proposals, so she refines her discovery questions. Figures are illustrative; actual results vary widely.
:::

## Testimonials, reviews, and the law

Social proof is persuasive, which is why it is regulated. The FTC's Endorsement Guides reflect the principle that endorsements must be honest and not misleading, must reflect the honest opinion of the endorser, and cannot be used to make a claim the marketer could not legally make [10]. If there is a connection between an endorser and the marketer that consumers would not expect and that would affect how they evaluate the endorsement, it should be disclosed clearly and conspicuously [10]. The Guides also caution that ads featuring endorsers who achieved exceptional results may need to make clear what results people can generally expect [10].

In August 2024 the FTC announced a final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials, including reviews by people who do not exist (such as AI-generated fake reviews), reviews by people without actual experience with the business, and reviews that misrepresent the reviewer's experience; the rule also prohibits compensation conditioned on reviews expressing a particular sentiment [11]. The rule allows the FTC to seek civil penalties against knowing violators [11].

For consultants, the practical rules are straightforward:

- Use only real testimonials from real clients, with written permission.
- Do not edit testimonials in ways that change their meaning.
- Disclose material connections, such as discounts given in exchange for a review.
- Do not present exceptional results as typical.
- Never invent clients, numbers, or case studies. Label illustrative examples clearly as hypothetical.

## Outreach that respects the recipient

Effective outreach is short, specific, and useful. Reference something particular to the recipient, share a relevant insight, and propose a small next step. Respect opt-outs. Commercial email and telephone outreach are subject to federal and state rules; review them before running any campaign at volume.

Testimonials and reviews are governed by the FTC's Endorsement Guides and its 2024 rule against fake reviews, so consultants must use only genuine, permitted, representative endorsements and must never invent results.

# Chapter 9: Delivering the Engagement

:::objectives
- Run a structured kickoff that confirms objectives, roles, and logistics.
- Manage an engagement with a cadence of updates and decision points.
- Handle problems and disagreements professionally.
- Close an engagement with documentation, measurement, and referral requests.
:::

## Delivery is marketing

The best source of future work is a current client who is delighted. Delivery quality determines repeat business, referrals, and testimonials. It also determines how much of the consultant's time each engagement actually consumes and therefore the effective profitability of a fixed or value-based fee.

## The engagement life cycle

| Phase | Key activities | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Kickoff | Confirm objectives, measures, roles, communication, data access | Kickoff summary |
| Discovery | Interviews, data review, observation | Findings |
| Analysis | Root cause, options, quantification | Recommendations |
| Design | Solution specification, plans | Design documents |
| Implementation | Configure, train, pilot, adjust | Working solution |
| Close-out | Measure, hand off, document, review | Close-out report |

## Communication cadence

A brief weekly status update prevents surprises: what was completed, what is next, decisions needed, and risks. Agree in advance who receives it. Schedule decision meetings at milestones rather than waiting for the client to ask.

## The 1-3-1 approach to problems

Dan Martell, a software entrepreneur and author, has described a "1-3-1" rule for team communication: bring one problem, three options, and one recommendation [12]. The same structure works when a consultant must raise an issue with a client. Instead of reporting only a problem, describe it precisely, present three possible responses with their trade-offs, and recommend one. Clients appreciate being given decisions rather than worries.

:::example Illustrative: a 1-3-1 escalation
Week three of an installation engagement: the client's office manager has not completed the call-log export, delaying analysis.

Problem: data access is five business days late, putting the training date at risk.

Options: (1) extend the schedule by one week at no cost; (2) have Elena pull the data directly with temporary system access; (3) proceed with a 30-day sample instead of 60 days.

Recommendation: option 2, with access revoked after export, because it protects the training date and preserves the full data set.

The owner approves option 2 the same day. Details are hypothetical.
:::

## Handling disagreements

Disagreements about findings are normal; a consultant who never disagrees with a client adds little perspective. State evidence, acknowledge the client's knowledge of their business, separate facts from interpretations, and propose a test where possible. If a disagreement concerns scope or payment, return to the signed SOW and change-control process.

## Close-out

At the end of the engagement:

1. Measure results against the agreed success measures.
2. Hand off documentation, tools, and access.
3. Hold a review meeting with the economic buyer.
4. Ask what went well and what could improve.
5. Ask whether the client would provide a testimonial (with written permission) and whether they know others who face similar problems.
6. Discuss any appropriate follow-on work.

:::checklist Engagement close-out
- Success measures reported, with data sources.
- All deliverables accepted in writing.
- Documentation and access handed off; consultant access removed where appropriate.
- Final invoice sent and paid.
- Review meeting held with the economic buyer.
- Feedback recorded and lessons logged.
- Testimonial request made with written permission form.
- Referral request made.
:::

:::summary
Delivery quality drives repeat work, referrals, and profitability. A clear life cycle, a weekly status cadence, and structured escalation using the 1-3-1 approach keep engagements on track. Disagreements are handled with evidence and tests, and scope disputes return to the SOW. A disciplined close-out measures results, hands off work, captures lessons, and requests testimonials and referrals.
:::

# Chapter 10: Ethics, Confidentiality, and Independence

:::objectives
- Identify common ethical risks in consulting.
- Establish practices for confidentiality and data protection.
- Recognize and manage conflicts of interest.
- Know when to decline or exit an engagement.
:::

## Why ethics is a business issue

A consultant's product is trust. Clients share sensitive financial, operational, and personal information, and they rely on the consultant's independent judgment. A single breach of confidence or undisclosed conflict can end a practice. Ethical discipline is therefore both a moral and a commercial necessity.

## Common ethical risks

| Risk | Example | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality breach | Mentioning a client's problems to another prospect | Never disclose client information without permission |
| Conflict of interest | Recommending software that pays you a referral fee | Disclose financial relationships in writing |
| Competence | Accepting work outside your expertise | Decline or bring in qualified partners |
| Unauthorized practice | Giving legal or tax advice without a license | Refer to licensed professionals |
| Misrepresentation | Overstating past results or credentials | Claim only what you can document |
| Data mishandling | Storing client data on personal devices without protection | Use secure tools; minimize data collected |

## Confidentiality practices

- Sign confidentiality terms in every engagement.
- Collect only the data needed and delete it when no longer required, consistent with the contract.
- Use strong passwords, multi-factor authentication, and encrypted storage.
- Anonymize examples in marketing unless the client has given written permission to be named.

## Conflicts of interest

A conflict exists when your interests, or your obligations to another party, could reasonably be seen as affecting your advice. Disclose conflicts in writing, let the client decide, and decline when the conflict cannot be managed. Referral fees from vendors are not necessarily unethical, but undisclosed ones are.

## When to say no

Decline or exit an engagement when the client asks you to do something illegal or deceptive, when you lack the competence and cannot obtain it, when a conflict cannot be managed, or when the client repeatedly fails to meet responsibilities that make success possible. Follow the termination terms in your contract.

:::example Illustrative: a disclosed referral arrangement
Elena's recommended call-tracking software offers her a 15 percent referral commission. She adds a sentence to her recommendations: "Disclosure: I receive a referral commission if you purchase Vendor X through my link. Two alternatives that pay me nothing are listed in Appendix 2 with a comparison." The client chooses Vendor X anyway, and the relationship is strengthened by the transparency. Details are hypothetical.
:::

:::checklist Ethics review
- Confidentiality terms are in every agreement.
- Data handling practices are documented.
- Financial relationships with vendors are disclosed.
- Work stays within my competence and licensure.
- Marketing claims are documented and accurate.
- I know when I would decline or exit.
:::

:::summary
Trust is the consultant's product. Confidentiality, disclosure of conflicts, staying within competence and licensure, accurate representation, and sound data practices protect both clients and the practice. Declining or exiting engagements that cannot be performed ethically is part of professional discipline.
:::

# Chapter 11: Building Leverage with Systems and Delegation

:::objectives
- Explain why a solo practice hits a capacity ceiling.
- Calculate a buyback rate to guide delegation decisions.
- Document repeatable work so others can perform it.
- Choose leverage strategies: delegation, productization, and recurring offers.
:::

## The capacity ceiling

A solo consultant's revenue is limited by billable capacity and price. Raising prices helps, but eventually growth requires leverage: getting more value delivered per hour of the owner's time. Leverage comes from delegation, documented processes, reusable tools, and offers that do not require the owner's continuous presence.

## Dan Martell's buyback framework

:::case Buying back time
Dan Martell has described a "Buyback Loop" of auditing how time is spent, transferring low-value tasks to others, and filling the freed time with higher-value work [13]. His accompanying workbook explains a "Buyback Rate": divide annual income by 2,000 working hours and then by 4, which suggests the hourly cost at which hiring help is worthwhile; for example, ($100,000 ÷ 2,000) ÷ 4 = $12.50 per hour [14]. In a podcast interview, Martell described a "Camcorder Method" in which the owner records themselves doing a task as they normally would, so that documentation adds little extra time, and he described using a small set of such videos as onboarding material for new team members [12]. Martell's experiences and results are his own, not TTI's.
:::

## Applying the framework to a consulting practice

| Task category | Examples | Leverage option |
|---|---|---|
| Administrative | Scheduling, invoicing, bookkeeping | Virtual assistant, software, bookkeeper |
| Production | Data cleaning, slide formatting, transcription | Analyst or contractor; templates |
| Delivery support | Training sessions, configuration | Trained associate under your oversight |
| Expert judgment | Diagnosis, recommendations, executive conversations | Keep; this is your highest value |
| Business development | Relationship building, proposals | Keep core conversations; delegate research and drafting |

## Documenting repeatable work

**Step 1: Track time** for two weeks and list recurring tasks.

**Step 2: Rank tasks** by hours consumed and by how much expert judgment they require.

**Step 3: Record** yourself performing the top delegable tasks.

**Step 4: Convert** recordings into checklists with quality standards.

**Step 5: Delegate** one task at a time with a review period.

**Step 6: Measure** quality and the owner hours freed.

:::example Illustrative: Elena's first delegation
Elena earns about $150,000 in revenue. Her buyback rate is ($150,000 ÷ 2,000) ÷ 4 = $18.75 per hour. Her time audit shows 7 hours a week on scheduling, invoicing, data exports, and slide formatting. She records each task, writes checklists, and hires a part-time assistant at $22 per hour for 8 hours a week, slightly above her calculated buyback rate, because the assistant also handles client-facing scheduling. Cost: about $176 a week. She redirects 6 freed hours to business development and diagnostics. Figures are illustrative.
:::

## Productization and recurring offers

Other forms of leverage include: standardized diagnostics delivered with templates; group workshops; licensing tools or frameworks to clients; and retainers for ongoing advisory access. Each must still be delivered with quality and honesty about what is included.

:::worksheet Leverage plan

Recording and checklist completion date: ___
Person or tool to take over: ___
Hours freed and how I will use them: ___
Productized or recurring offer to develop: ___
:::

:::summary
A solo practice reaches a capacity ceiling defined by hours and price. Dan Martell's buyback framework (audit, transfer, fill) and buyback rate offer a practical way to decide what to delegate, and recording tasks as they are performed reduces documentation effort. Consultants should keep expert judgment and core relationships while delegating administrative and production work, and should consider productized and recurring offers.
:::

# Chapter 12: Managing the Numbers

:::objectives
- Read the three core financial reports of a consulting practice.
- Manage cash flow, receivables, and reserves.
- Track key performance indicators for a professional services firm.
- Use monthly reviews to make pricing and capacity decisions.
:::

## Why consultants need financial discipline

Many consultants are excellent advisers to others and inattentive managers of their own businesses. The economics of a small practice are simple but unforgiving: revenue arrives in lumps, expenses arrive monthly, and taxes arrive quarterly. A practice can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash. The habits in this chapter take about two hours a month and prevent most avoidable crises.

## The three core reports

| Report | What it shows | Key question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Profit and loss (income statement) | Revenue, expenses, and profit over a period | Is the practice profitable? |
| Balance sheet | Assets, liabilities, and owner's equity at a point in time | What does the practice own and owe? |
| Cash flow statement or forecast | Cash coming in and going out | Will there be enough cash to meet obligations? |

Accounting software produces the first two automatically if transactions are recorded and reconciled. The cash forecast is usually a simple spreadsheet that lists expected receipts and payments for the next 13 weeks.

## Receivables

A signed proposal is not cash. Protect collections with clear payment terms:

- **Deposits.** Many consultants require a deposit, often 30 to 50 percent, before work begins.
- **Milestone billing.** Tie remaining payments to deliverable acceptance.
- **Due dates.** State them explicitly, such as "due within 15 days of invoice."
- **Follow-up cadence.** A friendly reminder a few days before the due date, a second on the due date, and a call shortly after.
- **Pause clause.** The contract can allow work to pause if invoices are significantly overdue.

## Reserves

Hold three separate reserves: a tax reserve (Chapter 7), an operating reserve of several months of fixed expenses, and an owner's personal emergency fund. The operating reserve allows the consultant to decline poorly fitting work during slow periods rather than accept it out of fear.

## Key performance indicators

| Indicator | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue booked | Value of signed engagements in the period | Leading indicator of future cash |
| Revenue collected | Cash received | Pays the bills |
| Utilization | Delivery hours ÷ available hours | Shows capacity and burnout risk |
| Effective hourly rate | Fee ÷ actual hours on an engagement | Tests pricing and scoping accuracy |
| Pipeline coverage | Weighted pipeline ÷ revenue target | Shows whether enough opportunities exist |
| Days sales outstanding | Average days to collect invoices | Tests collections discipline |
| Client concentration | Largest client's share of revenue | Measures dependence risk |
| Repeat and referral share | Revenue from existing clients and referrals | Signals delivery quality |

:::example Illustrative: effective hourly rate analysis
Elena reviews four completed engagements.

| Engagement | Fee | Actual hours | Effective rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diagnostic A | $2,500 | 18 | $138.89 |
| Diagnostic B | $2,500 | 22 | $113.64 |
| Installation A | $12,500 | 58 | $215.52 |
| Installation B | $9,500 | 61 | $155.74 |

Both diagnostics fall below her floor rate of about $148 (Chapter 5), while installations exceed it. She concludes that diagnostics are underpriced or overscoped, raises the diagnostic fee to $3,200, and limits the data review to 60 days. Installation B, discounted to win the work, still exceeded the floor but by a smaller margin. Figures are illustrative.
:::

## Client concentration

If one client provides more than about a third of revenue, the practice is vulnerable to that client's budget decisions. Concentration is common in a first year and is not a problem in itself, but it should be tracked and reduced over time by keeping marketing active even when busy.

## The monthly review

**Step 1: Reconcile** bank and card accounts in the accounting software.

**Step 2: Review** the profit and loss and balance sheet.

**Step 3: Update** the 13-week cash forecast.

**Step 4: Review receivables** and follow up on overdue invoices.

**Step 5: Calculate KPIs** and compare with last month.

**Step 6: Decide** one adjustment: a price change, a scope template change, a marketing push, or a delegation step.

:::

:::summary
Consulting revenue is uneven while expenses and taxes are regular, so financial discipline is essential. The profit and loss, balance sheet, and 13-week cash forecast give a complete picture. Deposits, milestone billing, and a collection cadence protect cash; tax and operating reserves buy freedom of choice. Key indicators such as effective hourly rate, utilization, pipeline coverage, and client concentration guide pricing and capacity decisions in a short monthly review.
:::

# Glossary

**Buyback rate.** In Dan Martell's framework, annual income divided by 2,000 hours and then by 4, used to guide delegation decisions.

**Change order.** A written agreement modifying scope, schedule, or fee.

**Diagnostic.** A short, paid engagement to understand and quantify a client's problem.

**Economic buyer.** The person with authority to approve payment for an engagement.

**Floor rate.** The minimum price per billable hour at which the practice covers compensation, expenses, and taxes.

**Material connection.** A relationship between an endorser and a marketer that could affect how an endorsement is evaluated.

**Niche.** A specific client, problem, and outcome combination.

**Retainer.** A recurring fee for defined ongoing work or access.

**Scope creep.** Gradual expansion of work beyond the agreed scope without a corresponding change in fee.

**Statement of work.** A document specifying deliverables, activities, exclusions, assumptions, responsibilities, and acceptance.

**Utilization.** The share of working hours that are billable or devoted to client delivery.

**Value-based fee.** A fee based on the consultant's contribution to the value the client receives.
