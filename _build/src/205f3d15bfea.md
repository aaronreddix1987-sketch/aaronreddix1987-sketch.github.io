# How to Use This Workbook

This independent, unofficial companion offers plain-language explanations, reflection prompts, and space to write for people working the Twelve Steps with a sponsor or group. It is not a substitute for those supports or for clinical care. Use one section at a time, return to a prompt later, or skip an exercise that does not feel right.

## Who This Workbook Is For

This workbook is for people new to Step work, returning to it, or seeking a companion to conversations with a sponsor or group. You need not work in order or write a particular amount. The purpose is thoughtful reflection, not perfect answers.

People come to recovery through many pathways, including mutual-help groups, clinical care, medication, peer support, family, faith, culture, and self-care. Approaches can be combined or changed over time. [1] SMART Recovery, for example, is a secular mutual-support program grounded in cognitive behavioral therapy. [2] Medication can be part of treatment alongside counseling and behavioral therapies. [3] Medication-supported recovery and Step work can be pursued together; discuss treatment or medication questions with a doctor or clinician.

This workbook is not affiliated with or endorsed by Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, AA World Services, NA World Services, or any fellowship. The official Steps are copyrighted by AA World Services and are not reprinted here. [4] For official wording, consult aa.org; official Spanish wording is available at aa.org/es. [4]

## What Research Can and Cannot Tell Us

A review of 27 studies involving 10,565 people found that clinically delivered, manualized Twelve-Step Facilitation programs designed to increase Alcoholics Anonymous participation led to higher rates of continuous abstinence than other active treatments. About 42% of AA participants remained completely abstinent at one year, compared with about 35% of people receiving other treatments. [5]

These findings concern specific programs and alcohol outcomes. They do not show that this workbook causes recovery, that one approach suits everyone, or that abstinence is the only meaningful measure of progress. Recovery has many pathways, and progress can include setbacks. [1] This workbook supports reflection; it is not a treatment program or a promise of results.

## Your Pace, Your Choices

Recovery work can bring up strong feelings. A trauma-informed approach emphasizes safety, trust, collaboration, and choice. [6] Set your own pace: pause, leave a question blank, or return later. A sponsor or group can help you decide whether a prompt is useful now.

Work the Steps with support, not in isolation. Inventory and amends may stir painful memories or concerns; consider doing this work with your sponsor and, if needed, a therapist. If a step or conversation may be unsafe, pause and seek support. Never make an amend in a way that could harm you or someone else. You need not share private details in this workbook or with a group if you do not feel safe.

This book is educational, not a substitute for treatment. For clinical guidance, speak with a doctor or clinician. The SAMHSA National Helpline offers free, confidential, 24-hour treatment referrals and information for people and families, including connections to local treatment facilities, support groups, and community organizations. [7]

:::warning If you are in danger right now
If you are in immediate danger, call 911. For crisis support, call or text 988; the service is free, confidential, and available 24/7. [8] For treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. [7]
:::

## A Note on Language

People use different words for recovery and their experiences. This workbook uses person-first language, such as “person with a substance use disorder” and “person in recovery,” and says “return to use” or “lapse” rather than labeling someone a failure. Use words that feel accurate and respectful to you; no single term captures every experience.

These pages support reflection and conversation. Take what is useful, seek help when needed, and let your experience shape the work.

# Step One: Honesty About Where I Am

## The theme in plain words

Step One asks you to look honestly at the role alcohol or other drugs play in your life. Its theme is admitting that a problem exists and that efforts to control it, or its effects, have not made life manageable. You do not need to explain every event, choose a label, or tell your story before you are ready. Begin by naming what you observe today.

Powerlessness does not mean helplessness or an inability to change. It points to the limits of your control over a substance and its effects. You may have tried rules or promises that did not work as hoped. Recognizing that pattern can help you consider support beyond trying harder alone.

“Unmanageable” need not mean a dramatic crisis. It may show up as missed responsibilities, strained relationships, worry, or a growing gap between your values and actions. Your experience does not need to match anyone else’s to deserve care.

This workbook is an independent, unofficial companion for people exploring the Twelve Steps with a sponsor or group. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, or any fellowship. The theme is paraphrased. For official wording, read the relevant material at aa.org [4].

## Common misunderstandings

**“Powerless” means helpless.** It does not. You may not control every urge or outcome, but you can ask for support, change a plan, or make a safer choice. The step is not a verdict on your worth.

**Admitting a problem means taking all the blame.** Reflection can include your choices and their effects without denying harm done to you or the influence of other people and circumstances. Responsibility is not shame. If reflection becomes punishing, pause and return to it with support.

**I have to hit a particular kind of bottom.** There is no required crisis or comparison test. You do not need to wait for more loss or danger to ask for help; your reasons for concern are enough to explore.

**I must disclose everything at once.** Honesty can begin in writing or in conversation with someone you trust. You choose what to share, with whom, and when. A sponsor or group can help you explore the step; you make choices about your process.

## Trauma-informed note

Choose a manageable pace. Skip a prompt, write a few words, or stop and return later. If a memory or question feels overwhelming, orient to the room and consider contacting someone you trust. You do not have to describe traumatic events to work on this step.

I want help making a different plan.” She chooses to bring that sentence to her sponsor. Lena’s next step is hers to decide; this example does not predict an outcome.

# Step Two: Room for Hope

## The theme in plain words

Step Two turns toward the possibility that support beyond individual effort can help you move toward a more grounded life. Its theme is coming to believe that a power greater than yourself could restore sanity. In this workbook, that phrase can mean what it means to you: God or a spiritual tradition, a group, nature, shared human care, or values that guide action. You need not settle on one explanation before exploring the step.

“Restore sanity” does not mean that you are broken, foolish, or mentally ill. Here it can mean finding room to pause, see choices, and act in line with your values. Hope need not be certainty. You might begin with the possibility that change can happen with help, even if you cannot yet picture how.

The step invites curiosity about whether something beyond isolated effort could help. A group, trusted person, spiritual practice, time in nature, or commitment to honesty may offer perspective or direction. Explore what feels supportive, respectful, and safe without borrowing someone else’s beliefs.

This workbook paraphrases the theme and does not reproduce the official wording. For that wording, read the relevant material at aa.org [4]. It is an independent, unofficial companion, not affiliated with or endorsed by Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, or any fellowship.

## Common misunderstandings

**I must believe in a religious God.** You need not adopt a religion to reflect on this step. People understand support and spiritual language differently. You can bring questions to a sponsor or group without claiming beliefs you do not hold.

**I must feel certain before I begin.** Belief can start as willingness to consider a possibility. Doubt and hope can coexist; uncertainty about whether a group, value, or practice can help does not prevent exploration.

**A power greater than me takes away my responsibility.** Seeking help can widen your options without making decisions for you. You can ask questions, set boundaries, and choose what fits.

**“Restore sanity” means something is wrong with me.** This phrase need not be a judgment or diagnosis. In this reflection, it points toward steadiness and a greater ability to respond rather than feel driven by old patterns. For mental or physical health concerns, speak with a doctor or clinician; this workbook cannot assess or treat health conditions.

## Trauma-informed note

Beliefs can be connected to painful experiences, family pressure, or past harm. You do not have to use spiritual language that feels unsafe, explain your history, or join a practice you do not choose. Ask how your sponsor or group makes room for different beliefs, and pause or skip any reflection that does not feel right.

:::example Illustrative example: Marcus
Marcus is fictional and feels uneasy when people describe recovery in religious terms. He is not ready to call himself a believer. He notices that a weekly group helps him see options he misses alone, and that walking outdoors helps him remember his commitment to honesty. He writes, “I do not know what I believe about a higher power. I am willing to consider that people and practices outside my own thoughts can help me.” He brings this uncertainty to his sponsor and asks how others work with questions. His understanding may change or stay the same; either way, he is allowed to proceed honestly.
:::

# Step Three: Choosing to Let Go

:::objectives
- Describe Step Three as a decision to stop trying to manage everything alone.
- Explore what a higher power or guiding principle could mean to you.
- Notice misunderstandings that can make this step feel unsafe or forced.
- Choose one small action that puts your decision into practice.
:::

## The theme in plain words

Step Three asks you to decide to seek guidance or support rather than run your life alone. It does not require a particular religion or certainty. It invites you to act with help and according to values that matter to you.

A higher power might mean God, a spiritual tradition, a recovery group, the natural world, love, conscience, or principles such as honesty and compassion. If you are unsure, you can begin there and keep exploring.

Letting go does not mean giving up responsibility. You still make choices and take practical steps; you loosen the belief that you must control every person, outcome, or feeling. Recovery can take many pathways and is person-driven; choice, culture, relationships, and peer support can all be part of recovery [1]. This chapter is educational, not a substitute for treatment. Discuss questions with a clinician or sponsor, and skip a prompt if needed.

## Common misunderstandings

**“I have to believe in God.”** The step’s spiritual language has many interpretations. Start by asking what guidance, connection, or values you are willing to trust.

**“Letting go means I stop making decisions.”** You remain responsible for your choices. Letting go means considering support rather than relying only on control.

**“If I trust, nothing painful will happen.”** Trust cannot guarantee an outcome or make you responsible for events outside your control.

**“I must feel completely ready.”** Doubt can accompany a decision. You can try one small step without claiming certainty about the future.

## A practical way to work Step Three

1. **Name the decision.** Describe the difference between controlling everything and allowing guidance or support into your life.
2. **Explore possible guidance.** List values, people, practices, or beliefs that help you move toward the life you want. If unsure, write “I am still exploring.”
3. **Notice the cost of control.** Choose a situation and separate what you can influence from what you cannot.
4. **Choose a small act of trust.** Ask someone trustworthy for perspective, follow through on a recovery commitment, or pause before reacting.
5. **Review with curiosity.** Consider what you learned. A difficult result is information for your next choice, not proof that you failed.

| When I notice | I can try | A reminder |
|---|---|---|
| I am trying to control an outcome | Name what is mine to do | I cannot control every result |
| I feel alone with a decision | Ask a trusted person to listen | Asking is a choice |
| I am unsure what I believe | Name what I value today | Uncertainty can be included |
| I have made a choice I regret | Review it and seek support | A next step is still possible |

:::example Illustrative example: Maya explores trust
Maya connects spiritual language with a religious community where she did not feel safe. She is not ready to use the word “God.” With her sponsor, she considers guidance she can trust: honesty, care, and group members who have earned her trust. When overwhelmed, she decides to call one member before making a major choice. She does not claim to have resolved every question about belief. For Maya, Step Three begins by letting trustworthy support and chosen values influence her next action.
:::

# Step Four: A Searching Inventory

:::objectives
- Describe Step Four as an honest written inventory, not a judgment of your worth.
- Reflect on resentments, fears, harms, and strengths at a manageable pace.
- Distinguish your responsibility from harm done to you.
- Identify support and boundaries that can make this work safer.
:::

## The theme in plain words

Step Four is a careful look at your life and choices. In this workbook, it means making a written inventory of resentments, fears, harms, and strengths. The aim is understanding, not building a case against yourself. “Searching” means looking beyond the first explanation; “fearless” does not mean feeling no fear.

An inventory can reveal patterns in situations, feelings, choices, and needs. It is not a complete account of your identity. A painful action does not define your character, and recording harm done to you does not make you responsible for it.

You choose what and when to write, and what to share. Skip an unsafe prompt or return to it with support. SAMHSA’s trauma-informed principles include safety, trust, collaboration, empowerment, voice and choice, and attention to cultural, historical, and gender issues [6]. Apply these principles to your own process. Include strengths and care you have shown. Abuse you suffered is not your fault; record it only if useful, and consider a therapist’s support if trauma is involved. A sponsor or group is not a substitute for clinical care when you need it.

## Common misunderstandings

**“Fearless means I should push through.”** Honesty includes recognizing limits. Pause, take a break, or ask for support.

**“My inventory should list only my faults.”** Include strengths, efforts, values, and helpful relationships for a more balanced account.

**“If I list harm done to me, I must find my part in it.”** No. You are not responsible for another person’s abuse. Leave “my part” blank or skip the entry.

**“I have to write every detail and share it with everyone.”** Decide what to record and whom to tell. Consider privacy and safety before sharing sensitive material.

**“A pattern proves I am a bad person.”** A pattern is information, not a verdict on your worth. Use it to understand choices and needs.

## A steady way to work Step Four

1. **Set a safe container.** Choose a private time and place, a way to pause, and someone you could contact if unsettled.
2. **Pick a manageable starting area.** Begin with a resentment, fear, harm, or strength; you need not start with the most painful memory.
3. **Describe what happened.** Use plain language, separate known facts from assumptions, and do not force details you do not remember or want to record.
4. **Notice the effect.** Name feelings, beliefs, relationships, or choices affected. It is fine to be unsure.
5. **Consider your part only where it applies.** Identify choices you want to understand or repair, without searching for blame. This does not apply to abuse you suffered.
6. **Include strengths and support.** Record values, efforts, and help that sustained you.
7. **Review with care.** Choose what to share with a sponsor, group, or clinician. Ask for help sorting questions of responsibility.

| Area | What I might explore | A useful boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Resentments | What happened and how it affected me | I can separate facts from assumptions |
| Fears | What I fear and what I do in response | I can work at my own pace |
| Harms | Choices I made and their effects | Accountability is not self-attack |
| Strengths | Values, efforts, and support | Strengths belong in the inventory |

:::example Illustrative example: Luis makes room for strengths
Luis writes about a conflict with a family member: what happened, how it affected his trust, and how he responded. He notes avoiding a conversation and later speaking harshly, without taking responsibility for the other person’s actions. He also records asking a friend for help and protecting his recovery. When another memory feels overwhelming, Luis stops and talks with his therapist about whether and how to return to it. His inventory includes accountability and care for himself.
:::

# Step Five: Telling the Truth to Another

:::objectives
- Describe Step Five as honest acknowledgment, not public confession.
- Choose a listener and share at a safe pace.
- Prepare for a useful conversation with a sponsor or trusted person.
:::

## Theme

Step Five invites you to name how your actions or patterns have caused harm or moved you away from the person you want to be. In Twelve-Step language, this means acknowledging the nature of your wrongs to a higher power, yourself, and another person. This chapter paraphrases the theme rather than reproducing the official wording. Read the official wording at aa.org [4].

Here, telling the truth means being as accurate and complete as you can while respecting your safety and limits. It does not mean calling yourself bad, exposing every private detail, or accepting someone else’s version of your story without question. You can acknowledge responsibility without denying context. A listener may be a sponsor, someone in your recovery community, or another trusted person. Your understanding of a higher power may be religious, spiritual, or personal.

## Misunderstandings to Set Aside

Step Five is not a demand to tell everyone everything, a test of worthiness, or a reason to put yourself in danger. One conversation will not necessarily erase harm or settle every feeling; repair may require time, further conversations, or practical changes.

Self-blame does not prove honesty. A useful account names what you did, the effects you can see, and what you do not yet understand. It avoids both minimizing harm and turning a behavior into a permanent label for your whole self.

## A Trauma-Informed Approach

Choose someone who listens respectfully and can explain what they can and cannot keep private. Trustworthiness and transparency are key principles of a trauma-informed approach [6]. You control the pace: agree on a time, ask what to expect, take breaks, or return to a detail later. If someone pressures, threatens, or humiliates you, pause and seek support elsewhere.

A sponsor or other non-professional may not have a clinician’s confidentiality duties. Ask how they handle what you share, including whether they might tell anyone and what they would do if they feared someone was in immediate danger. If sharing could affect your safety, legal situation, or care, consider consulting a qualified professional. This workbook is educational and is not a substitute for treatment.

## A Step-by-Step Preparation Framework

1. **Set your purpose.** Note what you hope to understand or do; do not make a particular reaction your goal.
2. **Choose a safe listener.** Consider whether the person listens without shaming, respects boundaries, and will discuss privacy. You can first ask for a brief planning conversation.
3. **Organize what you want to say.** Note actions, patterns, and effects on others and yourself. Separate what you know from what you guess; do not add details to make the account sound more serious.
4. **Agree on conditions.** Choose a time, place, and duration. Plan pauses, bring notes, or ask someone to help you prepare.
5. **Share and reflect.** Speak in your own words. Ask for clarification, and take time before answering questions if needed.
6. **Close with care.** Notice how you feel, identify support for afterward, and choose a next step, such as resting or arranging a check-in.

:::example Illustrative example: Maya prepares to share
Maya wants to talk with her sponsor about hiding spending from her partner. She notes what she remembers, what is uncertain, and the impact she can identify. Beforehand, she asks how the sponsor handles privacy and agrees she can pause if overwhelmed. She takes a break during the conversation, then returns to the topic. She leaves with a clearer account, not a demand to punish herself or fix everything that day.
:::

# Step Six: Becoming Willing to Change

:::objectives
- Explain willingness as openness to change, not instant perfection.

## Theme

Step Six centers on willingness to let go of patterns that hurt you or others. In Twelve-Step language, it means becoming ready to change what the program calls character defects. This chapter uses plain language rather than quoting the official Steps. Read the official wording at aa.org [4].

A pattern is a repeated way of thinking or acting that continues despite causing problems. Examples include avoiding difficult conversations, trying to control outcomes, or reacting quickly to criticism. Naming a pattern is not condemning yourself; it helps you notice what happens and consider another response.

Willingness is not certainty. You may want a pattern’s consequences to change while still feeling attached to the pattern. You need not feel completely ready to begin; curiosity and one manageable step can be enough.

## Misunderstandings to Set Aside

Willingness does not mean promising never to struggle, erasing your personality, accepting every criticism, or changing on someone else’s schedule. A pattern may help in some situations and harm in others, so consider its effects rather than rushing to label it.

You do not have to hate a behavior to change it. Ask whether it still serves your values and relationships, and what another response might make possible.

## A Trauma-Informed Approach

Some patterns began as survival strategies that helped you manage fear, instability, loss, or other painful experiences. Recognizing that history does not require you to keep a pattern that now causes harm. It lets you approach change with respect for what you lived through rather than shame.

Choose a safe pace and level of reflection. Skip a prompt, work with a sponsor, or seek clinical support if the material feels difficult to manage. Trauma-informed support values safety, choice, collaboration, and empowerment [6].

## A Step-by-Step Framework for Exploring a Pattern

1. **Name one pattern.** Choose something observable, such as withdrawing during conflict, rather than judging your whole self.
2. **Notice its context.** Ask when it appears, what you feel or expect, and what happens beforehand. Do not force a memory or explanation.
3. **Ask what it protected.** Consider whether it once helped you avoid danger, rejection, conflict, or uncertainty. Treat this as an idea to explore, not a verdict.
4. **Count its current costs.** Notice effects on well-being, commitments, relationships, or recovery, including short-term relief and longer-term consequences.
5. **Choose an opposite action.** Pick a small step toward your values. If you avoid a needed conversation, you might ask for a time to talk.
6. **Practice and review.** Try it in a manageable situation. Notice what helped and what was difficult; adjust as needed. Returning to an old pattern is information, not proof that change is impossible.

Luis often agrees to plans he does not want, then feels resentful and stops responding to messages. He considers that agreeing quickly may once have helped him avoid arguments. Now the pattern strains friendships and leaves him isolated. His small opposite action is to say he needs time to think before committing and will answer later. He plans to review the experience with his sponsor rather than expecting one practice to change every relationship.

# Step Seven: Humility in Practice

:::objectives
- Define humility as an honest, non-degrading view of yourself.
- Explore the difference between asking for help and demanding perfection.
- Identify one pattern you may be ready to change.
- Prepare a question to discuss with a sponsor or group.

## Theme: Humility as an Honest View of Self

Humility is seeing yourself accurately: recognizing strengths, acknowledging where your actions cause difficulty, and admitting you do not have to manage every change alone. It leaves room for responsibility and self-respect.

A shortcoming is a repeated behavior or response that conflicts with your values or harms relationships. Examples include avoiding an honest conversation, reacting with anger, or trying to control another person’s choices. Naming a pattern is not declaring yourself bad; it makes change easier to consider.

Asking for help might mean prayer, talking with a sponsor or group, seeking counseling, or trying a different response. Choose support that fits your beliefs, culture, needs, and sense of safety.

## Misunderstandings: Humility Is Not Humiliation

Humility does not require accepting insults, surrendering boundaries, or agreeing with every judgment. A degrading view says, “I am the problem and cannot change.” A more useful view says, “This behavior is causing a problem, and I can ask for support in changing it.”

Asking for help does not mean expecting instant change. Some patterns connect to painful experiences or difficult circumstances and may need careful attention. Move at a manageable pace; pause and return with support if reflection feels overwhelming. Consult a doctor or clinician about health concerns or treatment. This workbook is educational, not a substitute for treatment.

## A Step-by-Step Framework

1. **Choose one pattern.** Identify a specific behavior, not a broad label. You might ask someone you trust what they notice, then decide what to explore.
2. **Describe what happens.** Note what you do, when it happens, and its effects. Use observable details, not insults or guesses about your character.
3. **Notice its purpose.** The pattern may help you avoid conflict, feel in control, or protect yourself from discomfort. Understanding it does not excuse harm; it can help you find a safer response.
4. **Name a manageable change.** For example, pause before responding or ask a clarifying question instead of making an assumption.
5. **Ask for fitting support.** Decide whether you want someone to listen, help you reflect, or check in later.
6. **Review without grading yourself.** Notice what you tried and learned. If the pattern returns, treat it as information, not proof that you are hopeless.

Mara notices she agrees to plans she do not want, becomes resentful, then avoids the people involved. Instead of calling herself “selfish,” she names the behavior: “I say yes when I mean no, then withdraw.” With her sponsor, she chooses one practice: asking for time to think before answering. She does not expect to handle every conversation perfectly; she aims to notice the pattern earlier and keep asking for support.

## Step Eight: Listing Harms, Becoming Willing

:::objectives
- Describe the purpose of making a careful list of harms.
- Separate harms you caused from harms done to you.
- Consider willingness without treating it as an obligation to act now.
- Identify safety concerns to discuss with a sponsor or other trusted support.
:::

### Theme: Honest Inventory and Willingness

This step focuses on listing people you have harmed and becoming willing to make amends. The list supports reflection; it is not a verdict on your character. Name your actions clearly and consider what repair might involve.

Willingness means readiness to consider responsibility and possible repair. It does not require contact, an apology, or immediate action. Discuss next steps with a sponsor or trusted support, considering safety, boundaries, and possible effects on everyone involved.

### Misunderstandings: Willingness Is Not Action Yet

Not every item calls for the same response. Some require more reflection or guidance. You need not force a conversation, promise an outcome, or seek forgiveness. Respect a person’s choice not to have contact.

Listing harms you caused does not require denying harms done to you. You can own your actions without taking responsibility for someone else’s behavior.

### A Trauma-Informed Note

Include yourself if your actions harmed your well-being, such as neglecting your needs or taking risks. Describe what happened without punishing yourself, and omit private details that do not help you reflect.

Keep harms you caused separate from harms done to you. Contacting someone who abused you is never a required amends. Do not contact that person because of pressure from a worksheet, group, or anyone else. If an action raises safety concerns, pause and discuss it with a trusted sponsor or qualified clinician. You may skip an item and return only if and when it feels safe.

### A Step-by-Step Framework

1. **Set a safe pace.** Choose a private setting where you can stop, and decide whether you want support afterward.
2. **Recall relationships and situations.** Consider family, friends, work, community, and yourself. Start with one area if that is more manageable.
3. **Describe your actions.** Write what you did or failed to do. Avoid guessing what others felt or adding uncertain details.
4. **Separate responsibility.** Note what was yours to own and what belonged to someone else. Record harms done to you separately.
5. **Rate willingness and note safety.** Rate willingness to consider repair from 0 to 10. Note concerns about contact, boundaries, privacy, or emotional safety. A low number is information, not failure.
6. **Review before acting.** Discuss the list and possible next steps with a sponsor or trusted person. Reflection does not require immediate contact or action.

Luis does not treat contacting the former partner as required; he takes time to consider what, if anything, is safe and appropriate.

# Step Nine: Making Amends Safely

:::objectives
- Describe making amends as responsible repair, not a demand for forgiveness.
- Distinguish direct, indirect, living, and financial amends.
- Use a safety check before deciding whether, when, and how to make contact.
- Plan an amends approach with support from a sponsor, counselor, or other trusted person.
:::

## Theme and purpose

This step is about taking responsibility for harm and making repair where possible, without causing further harm. This reflective workbook is an independent, unofficial companion for people working the Twelve Steps with a sponsor or group. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, or any fellowship, and does not reproduce official wording. Read the official wording at aa.org and discuss its meaning with your sponsor or group.

An **amends** is an action that acknowledges harm and takes responsibility. It may involve a conversation, practical repair, or sustained change in behavior. It is not a request for forgiveness, renewed contact, or reassurance. You can offer accountability, but cannot control the response.

You do not need to make every possible amends immediately. Preparation is part of responsibility, and support can help you work through shame, anger, grief, or fear. Recovery is person-driven and can include setbacks as well as progress [1].

## Misunderstandings to set aside

Making amends does not mean contacting everyone on a list, forcing a conversation, disclosing private information, or asking the person you harmed to carry the emotional burden of your recovery. An apology does not erase consequences, and taking responsibility does not mean accepting blame for harm someone else caused you.

Direct contact is not always safe or respectful. Someone may have set a boundary, or contact may create danger or legal risk. Indirect repair, a living amends, or waiting may be more appropriate. Discuss the choice with a sponsor or counselor; seek legal advice if legal exposure may be involved.

## A trauma-informed approach

Trauma-informed support prioritizes safety, trust, collaboration, and personal choice while working to avoid re-traumatization [6]. You can pause or skip an exercise. Do not let pressure from a group or sponsor override your boundaries or the other person’s. Seek support that respects both.

:::warning Amends safety check
Do not contact someone if there is a protective or no-contact order, domestic violence, risk of harm to either person, or possible legal exposure. Get legal advice first when legal exposure may be involved. Never pressure someone to respond, meet, forgive, or restart a relationship. Indirect or living amends may fit when direct contact is unsafe or unwanted. Review your plan with a sponsor or counselor. If there is immediate danger, call 911.
:::

## A step-by-step planning framework

1. **List possible harms without contacting anyone.** Describe actions and effects plainly. Separate what you know from what you assume. Pause or seek support if writing feels too difficult.
2. **Check safety, boundaries, and legal concerns.** Consider whether the person requested no contact, whether your presence could reopen harm, and whether communication could affect a legal matter. Silence is not permission to keep trying.
3. **Identify reasonable repair.** Consider whether an apology, practical correction, repayment, or change in conduct is relevant. Focus on the other person’s needs, not your wish for relief.
4. **Choose a form and time.** Consider direct, indirect, living, or financial amends, and review the plan with a sponsor or counselor. If contacting the person, prepare a brief message and be ready to accept refusal or no response.
5. **Follow through without demands.** Do what you said you would, within your ability and any agreed limits. Do not ask the person to manage your feelings.
6. **Review and adjust.** Consider what is complete and whether further contact would respect safety and boundaries. Waiting or not contacting someone can be responsible.

| Type | When it may fit |
|---|---|
| Direct | Contact is safe and welcome, and a conversation or apology may help repair harm. |
| Indirect | Direct contact is unsafe, unwanted, or inappropriate; repair may be possible through an intermediary or another action. |
| Living amends | Sustained change in behavior is the most respectful repair, especially when contact would be harmful or unwelcome. |
| Financial restitution | Money or property was taken or damaged, and a safe, realistic repayment or replacement plan is possible. |

# Step Ten: Daily Inventory

:::objectives
- Explain a daily inventory as a review of actions, feelings, and repair needs.
- Use a short evening review without turning it into a scorecard.
- Apply a spot-check when a strong reaction or concern arises during the day.
- Decide when to pause, seek support, or make a prompt and proportionate correction.
:::

## Theme and purpose

This step is about noticing your conduct and acknowledging when you have caused harm or acted against your values. This workbook is an independent, unofficial companion for people working the Twelve Steps with a sponsor or group. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, or any fellowship, and does not reproduce official wording. Read the official wording at aa.org and discuss its meaning with your sponsor or group.

A **daily inventory** is a brief review of what happened, how you responded, and whether repair or change is needed. It is not a trial or a reason for self-punishment. An **admission** means naming your part clearly to yourself and, when appropriate, to the person affected or a trusted support person. Prompt acknowledgment does not require an impulsive conversation or unsafe contact.

Recovery involves working toward health, a self-directed life, and personal potential [1]. If review becomes repetitive self-attack, frightening, or hard to stop, pause and discuss another approach with a sponsor or clinician. This workbook does not replace treatment or clinical care.

## Misunderstandings to set aside

An inventory is not a list of everything wrong with you or a demand to confess every thought. Feelings are information, not proof of wrongdoing. Notice anger, fear, or resentment without condemning yourself for having it.

“Promptly” does not mean acting before considering safety, privacy, and boundaries. Name a mistake, seek guidance, and choose a reasonable next step. A lapse or return to use does not mean treatment has failed; it can signal a need to reinstate or adjust support [9]. For substance-use concerns or a crisis, contact a clinician or qualified support. In a crisis, call or text 988; for treatment referrals, call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357 [8] [7].

## A trauma-informed approach

Trauma-informed care emphasizes safety, trust, peer support, collaboration, empowerment and choice, and attention to cultural, historical, and gender issues [6]. Adapt the review to your circumstances: write instead of speaking, use only helpful prompts, or return to a difficult question with support. You need not disclose private details to someone you do not trust.

## A step-by-step daily framework

1. **Set a manageable time.** Choose a private, steady moment. Keep the review short when tired or distressed; consistency matters more than completeness.
2. **Recall events neutrally.** What happened? What choices did you make? What went well? Begin with observable events, not character judgments.
3. **Notice feelings and needs.** What emotions or pressures were present? What support, rest, or boundary might help? Pause before interpreting an intense feeling.
4. **Consider your part.** Were you honest, respectful, and willing to listen? What might you do differently? Do not take responsibility for another person’s actions.
5. **Identify repair.** Describe any needed correction, apology, or follow-through. Check safety and boundaries before contact; use the Step Nine safety check when relevant.
6. **Choose one next action.** Listen, correct an error, ask for help, or plan a safer response. If no repair is needed, note what you want to continue.
7. **Close the review.** Record one thing you appreciate or want to carry into tomorrow. Stop there; take serious issues to a support person rather than trying to solve them alone at night.

## Evening review prompts

Use the questions that help; you do not need to answer all of them each night.

- What am I glad I did today, even if it was small?
- Was I honest with myself and others? If not, what is a truthful next step?
- Did I treat anyone unfairly, break an agreement, or avoid a responsibility?
- Did I respect my own and others’ boundaries?
- What was difficult, and what support might help next time?
- Is repair needed, and is it safe and appropriate to do now?
- What is one specific action I will take tomorrow?

## Spot-check method

A spot-check is a brief pause when you notice tension, resentment, an urge to react, or uncertainty about your conduct. Use it when pausing could help; it is not a reason to monitor every feeling.

1. **Pause.** Take a moment before replying or acting, if possible.
2. **Describe what happened.** State the event without guessing another person’s motives.
3. **Name your response.** What are you feeling, thinking, or wanting to do?
4. **Check your choices.** What would be honest, safe, and respectful? Would waiting or asking for help be wiser?
5. **Act or make a note.** Take a proportionate step or revisit the issue in your evening review. If there is danger, step away if possible and seek appropriate help.

# Step Eleven: Quiet Connection

:::objectives
- Explore Step Eleven as an invitation to a more conscious relationship with a higher power, as each person understands it.
- Identify spiritual and secular ways to make space for reflection.
- Choose a brief practice that respects your safety and needs.
- Prepare questions for a sponsor or group.
:::

## Theme: making room for connection

Step Eleven invites a person to seek, through prayer and meditation, a deeper connection with a higher power as they understand it. People may relate this to a personal God, a spiritual tradition, nature, shared human wisdom, or a recovery community. Others use reflection or mindfulness without belief in a higher power.

This chapter is part of Honest Steps, an independent, unofficial companion for people working the Twelve Steps with a sponsor or group. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, or any fellowship, and does not reproduce the official wording of any Step. Consult the appropriate fellowship’s literature for official wording; Alcoholics Anonymous literature is available at aa.org.

Prayer may mean speaking, listening, expressing gratitude, asking for guidance, or sitting with uncertainty. Meditation may involve attending to breath, sounds, or sensations. Reflection, journaling, and time in nature are also options. Adapt or change a practice to fit your beliefs, culture, access needs, and experience.

## Misunderstandings to set aside

Quiet practice need not produce a special feeling, a clear answer, or sudden spiritual experience. A busy mind does not mean you are doing it wrong; the aim is not to force silence or eliminate difficult thoughts. You can pause, notice what is happening, and gently return to a focus.

Prayer and meditation need not follow a prescribed form. You might pray aloud, walk outdoors, write, or sit with a group. You can also decide a practice does not fit and try another. Choice matters.

## A flexible practice in five steps

1. **Choose a purpose.** Name what you hope to bring to the pause: honesty, patience, guidance, gratitude, or attention.
2. **Choose a safe form.** Pray, reflect, notice your surroundings, write, or try mindfulness. Keep your eyes open or choose a familiar place if that helps.
3. **Set a small boundary.** Choose a brief time. You may stop, change position, or switch activities at any point.
4. **Notice and return.** Focus on a word, breath, sound, or sight. When attention wanders, acknowledge it and return without scolding yourself.
5. **Close and reflect.** Consider what you noticed and whether the practice felt helpful, neutral, or uncomfortable. Adjust next time.

Mindfulness-based relapse prevention is a related practice, not evidence for or a requirement of Step Eleven. In a trial of 286 people, both mindfulness-based relapse prevention and standard relapse prevention reduced relapse compared with treatment as usual at six months. At 12 months, participants in mindfulness-based relapse prevention had 31% fewer drug-use days and a lower probability of heavy drinking [10]. This research concerns a specific relapse-prevention approach; it does not show that one form of prayer or meditation is right for everyone.

Elena is unsure what she believes about a higher power. With her sponsor, she decides to try a two-minute pause after breakfast. She keeps her eyes open and names three things she can see, then writes one sentence about what she hopes to practice that day. Some mornings she feels calmer; other mornings she feels no different. She treats both results as information, not as a grade. After a week, she and her sponsor discuss whether the routine feels useful and safe for her.

## Trauma-informed note

Quiet or inward-focused practice can bring up difficult feelings or memories. You do not have to close your eyes, stay still, or continue if you feel overwhelmed. Try a shorter session, notice ordinary objects, or feel your feet on the floor. You can stop, skip the practice, or choose another. If distress continues or you need help deciding what is safe, talk with a clinician. This workbook is educational and is not a substitute for treatment.

# Step Twelve: Carrying the Message, Living the Principles

:::objectives
- Describe Step Twelve as change, service, and putting recovery principles into daily life.
- Consider service that fits your capacity and values.
- Identify boundaries that protect both the person offering help and the person receiving it.
- Prepare questions for a sponsor or group.
:::

## Theme: service as part of daily life

Step Twelve connects personal change with helping others and practicing recovery principles in everyday life. A spiritual awakening may be a clear shift or gradual change seen in choices, relationships, or willingness to ask for help. No one has to claim a particular experience to take a next step toward honesty, care, and responsibility.

Carrying a message does not mean persuading or rescuing someone, or presenting yourself as an expert. It may mean sharing what helped when welcome, listening without judgment, or making a practical contribution. Service can happen in a fellowship, family, workplace, or neighborhood.

SAMHSA identifies peer support as a principle of recovery and notes that helping others can strengthen one’s own recovery [1]. This does not mean service must be constant or replace treatment, mutual help, or personal care. Your health, safety, and limits matter.

## Misunderstandings to set aside

Service does not require telling your story publicly. You decide what to share, with whom, and when. You can help without sharing personal details, or decline a request that feels unsafe or beyond your role.

Carrying a message does not guarantee another person’s progress. You can offer an invitation, share a resource, or listen, but cannot control someone else’s choices. Practicing principles does not mean being perfect; a mistake may be a chance to take responsibility and consider repair, not a reason for shame or abandoning recovery.

## A service framework in five steps

1. **Name what you can offer.** Consider your time, skills, interests, and energy. A small contribution can matter.
2. **Ask what is wanted.** Check whether help is welcome and what would be useful. Listening may be better than advice.
3. **Choose a clear role.** Offer a specific task or kind of support, and be honest about your limits.
4. **Respect boundaries and safety.** Protect privacy, do not take responsibility for another person’s decisions, and seek support when a need is beyond your role.
5. **Reflect and adjust.** Consider whether the service felt sustainable and respectful; change your commitment as needed.

| Service option | Possible contribution | Boundary to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Fellowship or group | Welcome a newcomer or help with a task | Follow group guidance and protect privacy |
| Recovery Café | Join a Recovery Circle or contribute to the Café | Follow its membership agreements [11] |
| Community setting | Offer a skill or practical help | Agree on a clear time and role |
| Everyday relationships | Listen or share what helped you | Ask before offering advice |

Recovery Cafés are recovery community centers with membership agreements that include being drug- and alcohol-free in the space, participating in a weekly Recovery Circle, and contributing to the Café. Their practices include loving accountability and radical hospitality [11]. A Café is one option for connection and contribution.

:::example Illustrative example: Marcus offers a bounded kind of help
Marcus has been asked by a newer group member to explain how he handled a difficult week. He checks with his sponsor about what feels appropriate, then asks the person whether they would like him to listen or share his own experience. The person chooses to hear a brief story. Marcus avoids telling the person what to do and suggests they discuss next steps with their own sponsor. Later, he reflects that listening felt useful, while taking responsibility for the other person’s choices would not have been his role.
:::

## Trauma-informed note

Helping can bring up strong feelings, especially when someone’s situation resembles your own. Pause, notice your limits, and ask for support before continuing. You do not need to share personal experiences, accept every request, or take on an overwhelming role. If you feel pressured or unsafe, step back and talk with a trusted sponsor, group member, or clinician. Service works best with choice, respect, and clear boundaries.

:::

# Glossary

**Amends:** A considered effort to take responsibility and, when safe and appropriate, repair harm. It does not require forgiveness, a response, or renewed contact.

**Character defects:** A Twelve-Step phrase for patterns a person wants to change. It does not mean a person lacks worth. Describe patterns plainly, such as avoiding difficult conversations or reacting in anger.

**Fellowship:** People meeting around shared experience, mutual support, or a common recovery approach.

**Grounding:** Bringing attention to the present and surroundings, such as noticing your feet on the floor, naming objects, or holding a familiar object if comfortable.

**Higher power:** A personal source of guidance, strength, meaning, or connection, understood in religious, spiritual, secular, or community-based ways.

**Home group:** A meeting attended regularly as a consistent place of connection. Practices vary.

**Inventory:** Structured reflection on experiences, feelings, actions, effects on others, and strengths—not a measure of worth.

**Living amends:** Ongoing behavior change intended to show responsibility and prevent repeated harm. It does not replace a safe, appropriate direct repair.

**Mutual help:** People sharing experience and encouragement in a peer group, fellowship, or community.

**Recovery:** An ongoing process of change toward health, well-being, a self-directed life, and personal potential. Pathways differ, and setbacks may occur.

**Recovery community center:** A community setting for connection and recovery activities; services and expectations vary.

**Sponsor:** A Twelve-Step member offering peer guidance, often by sharing experience with the Steps. A sponsor is not necessarily a clinician.

**Sponsee:** A person receiving peer guidance from a sponsor in a Twelve-Step fellowship.

**Step work:** Reflection and actions connected to the Twelve Steps, often with a sponsor or group. Read the official Steps through Alcoholics Anonymous at aa.org. This workbook is an independent, unofficial companion, not affiliated with or endorsed by Alcoholics Anonymous, Narcotics Anonymous, or any fellowship.

**Twelve-Step Facilitation:** A structured clinical approach encouraging participation in Twelve-Step mutual-help groups; distinct from attending a peer meeting alone.

**SMART Recovery:** A secular mutual-support program grounded in cognitive behavioral therapy. Its four-point program focuses on motivation, urges, thoughts and behaviors, and a balanced life [2].

**Service:** A contribution to a group or community, such as setting up a meeting or welcoming participants. Service should respect capacity and boundaries.

**Trauma-informed:** An approach recognizing trauma’s possible effects and emphasizing safety, trust, peer support, collaboration, choice, empowerment, and attention to culture and identity. It aims to avoid further harm.

**Urge:** A strong desire or impulse to use a substance or take an action. An urge is an experience, not an instruction; support can help you choose how to respond.

**Values:** Principles that guide choices, such as honesty, care, responsibility, or creativity.
