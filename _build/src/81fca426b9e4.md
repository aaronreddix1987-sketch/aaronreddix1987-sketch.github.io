# How to Use This Book

This book teaches a complete, ethical process for generating business-to-business (B2B) sales conversations on LinkedIn and converting them into clients. It is written for owners of small service firms, independent consultants, agency founders, and salespeople who sell to other businesses and who are tired of two extremes: posting into the void with no plan, or sending scripted pitches that recipients ignore or report.

The method rests on a simple premise supported by buyer research: business buyers spend most of their purchase journey researching on their own, they avoid sellers who send irrelevant outreach, and most of them are not ready to buy at any given moment. A system that works with those facts, rather than against them, builds a reputation before it asks for a meeting, makes every message relevant to a specific person, and measures honestly.

## What you will be able to do

After working through the book you should be able to define an ideal customer profile and the business events that make a prospect ready to talk; design an offer that a buyer can evaluate quickly; turn your LinkedIn profile into a credible landing page; publish useful content on a sustainable schedule; research accounts manually and within LinkedIn's rules; open conversations with connection requests and messages that earn replies; run discovery calls that qualify rather than pitch; write short proposals; and manage a pipeline with metrics that tell you what to fix.

## How each chapter works

Each chapter begins with learning objectives and ends with a summary and review questions. In between you will find definitions, a step-by-step framework, a worked example, a table, and a checklist or worksheet. Worked examples are labeled illustrative: the people, firms, and numbers in them are invented to demonstrate method and arithmetic. Case-study boxes describe real, publicly documented research or practitioners, with citations; their findings and results are their own, not Total Transformation Inc.'s, and they are not predictions of what you will achieve.

The appendices contain scripts, worksheets, and a 30-day launch plan you can copy into your own workflow.

# Chapter 1: How Business Buyers Actually Buy

:::objectives
- Describe how B2B buyers divide their time during a purchase.
- Explain the "95-5" idea and why most prospects are not ready to buy now.
- Distinguish relevant outreach from interruption.
- Translate buyer research into three design rules for a LinkedIn sales system.
:::

Before designing a sales process, it helps to understand the process on the other side of the table. A B2B purchase is usually a group decision, made over weeks or months, by people who are busy with their regular jobs. They do not experience your outreach as the start of their journey; they experience it as one input among many.

## Where buyer time goes

Gartner surveyed 750 B2B buyers involved in complex purchases and found that they spent only about 17 percent of their buying time meeting with potential suppliers; much of the rest went to independent online and offline research and to meetings within their own buying group [1][2]. When buyers compared several suppliers, Gartner estimated that any single sales representative might get only 5 to 6 percent of the buyer's total time [2]. The implication is uncomfortable but useful: most of the persuasion happens when you are not in the room. Your profile, your content, and what others say about you do most of the selling.

More recent Gartner research reinforces the point. In a survey of 632 B2B buyers conducted in August and September 2024, Gartner reported that 61 percent preferred an overall rep-free buying experience and that 73 percent actively avoid suppliers who send irrelevant outreach [3]. The same release noted that buyers still prefer seller input for tasks requiring contextual judgment, such as deciding whether a solution fits their company [3]. Buyers are not anti-seller. They are anti-waste.

## Most buyers are not in market

Professor John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, in research published by LinkedIn's B2B Institute, explained that many business purchases happen infrequently. If a company changes a service provider about once every five years, then only about 20 percent of such buyers are in the market in a given year and roughly 5 percent in a given quarter, which he summarized as the "95-5" heuristic [4][5]. Dawes stressed that 95 percent is not a precise rule but an illustration that most potential buyers are out of market at any moment [5].

For a LinkedIn seller, this means that a prospect who ignores you today may be an excellent client next year. The goal of most activity is therefore to be remembered and trusted when the need arises, while having a respectful process for the minority who need help now.

## Three design rules

1. Be findable and credible when buyers research independently. Your profile and content must answer the questions buyers ask before they talk to anyone.
2. Make every direct message relevant to a specific person and a specific situation. Generic outreach is the behavior buyers say they avoid.
3. Play a long game with the out-of-market majority, and a fast, helpful game with the in-market minority.

| Buyer reality | Common seller mistake | System response |
|---|---|---|
| Buyers research mostly on their own | Treating the first call as the first impression | Build a profile and content that pre-answer questions |
| Most buyers are out of market | Pushing for meetings with everyone | Stay visible; track triggers that signal readiness |
| Buyers avoid irrelevant outreach | Mass, templated pitches | Research each prospect; reference a real reason |
| Buyers want contextual advice | Product monologues | Ask diagnostic questions; offer judgment |

:::case LinkedIn's Social Selling Index research
LinkedIn created a Social Selling Index (SSI) that scores four activities: establishing a professional brand, finding the right people, engaging with insights, and building relationships [6]. LinkedIn reported that, in its own research, sellers with high SSI scores had 45 percent more opportunities per quarter and were 51 percent more likely to hit quota than those with low scores, and that its own representatives with an SSI above 90 were three times more likely to reach its top sales club [6][7]. This is vendor research with a correlational design: high performers may simply be more active, rather than activity causing performance. Treat it as evidence that the four activities are associated with results, not as a promise.
:::

:::example Illustrative: the time math of a buying decision
A hypothetical regional property-management firm spends about 40 hours across a buying group evaluating a new bookkeeping provider. If roughly 17 percent of that time is spent with suppliers, about 7 hours go to vendor meetings. Shared among three providers, each gets a little over 2 hours. The other 33 hours go to reading websites, checking LinkedIn profiles, asking peers, and internal discussion. A provider who has published three clear posts on the exact problem the firm faces, and whose profile explains the process and pricing approach, influences those 33 hours. A provider who has only a job title does not.
:::

:::checklist Buyer-centered starting point
- I can explain where buyers spend their time before contacting a seller.
- I accept that most prospects are not in market today.
- I have committed to relevant, researched outreach only.
- I know the four SSI activities and can name one action for each.
:::

:::summary
Business buyers spend most of their journey without sellers, avoid irrelevant outreach, and are mostly out of market at any given time. A LinkedIn sales system should therefore make you credible during independent research, keep you remembered by future buyers, and reserve direct outreach for relevant, researched conversations.
:::

# Chapter 2: Defining Your Ideal Customer and Buying Triggers

:::objectives
- Write an ideal customer profile (ICP) at the company level.
- Identify buyer personas within the buying group.
- Define observable buying triggers that indicate readiness.
- Size your addressable list to choose a realistic weekly pace.
:::

An ideal customer profile describes the kind of organization most likely to benefit from your offer and to become a good client. A buyer persona describes a person inside that organization: their role, responsibilities, pressures, and the outcomes they are measured on. A buying trigger is an observable event that makes a need more urgent, such as hiring a new leader, opening a location, raising capital, adopting new software, or receiving negative reviews about a specific problem.

The point of this chapter is narrowing. A seller who tries to talk to "small businesses" has no basis for relevance. A seller who talks to "owner-operated HVAC companies with 10 to 50 employees in two metropolitan areas that are hiring dispatchers" can write a message that sounds like it was written for one person, because it nearly was.

## Building the ICP

Look first at your best existing clients if you have any, or at the clients you are best equipped to serve if you do not. Describe them using firmographics (industry, size, location, ownership, growth stage), technographics (tools they use), and situational factors (problems they commonly face). Then ask what they have in common that predicts a good result.

## Mapping the buying group

Even in small firms, more than one person often influences a purchase. Gartner has described B2B buying groups for complex solutions as involving multiple stakeholders across functions [3]. In a small firm the group might be the owner, an operations manager, and a bookkeeper. Identify who feels the problem, who controls the budget, who will use the solution, and who might block it.

| Persona | What they care about | What they fear | Useful first topic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner or general manager | Profit, time, growth, reputation | Wasted money, disruption | Cost of the current problem in dollars or hours |
| Operations manager | Smooth daily processes, staff workload | Change that adds work | Steps that remove repetitive tasks |
| Finance or bookkeeper | Accurate numbers, cash flow | Unpredictable costs | Pricing structure and measurable return |
| Frontline user | Ease of use | Being blamed for failure | Training and support plan |

## Choosing triggers

A good trigger is observable from public information, correlated with need, and timely. Examples include a job posting for a role your service supports, a new location announcement, a leadership change, a public post from the owner describing a pain point, a funding announcement, or a regulatory change affecting the industry. Triggers give you a legitimate reason to start a conversation and improve relevance.

## Sizing the market

Estimate how many organizations fit your ICP in your chosen geography or niche. If the answer is 300, you cannot contact hundreds of new people per week without exhausting the list and your reputation. If it is 30,000, you need sharper prioritization. Knowing the size helps you set a pace you can sustain with genuine research.

## Six steps to an ICP

1. List your best current or intended clients and what made them good fits.
2. Write a one-paragraph company-level ICP with firmographic limits.
3. Name two or three personas and what each values.
4. List five observable buying triggers.
5. Estimate the number of organizations that match.
6. Write a disqualification list: organizations you will not pursue and why.

:::example Illustrative: an ICP for a fractional operations consultant
A hypothetical consultant helps service businesses fix scheduling and handoffs. ICP: residential and light-commercial service companies (plumbing, HVAC, electrical, cleaning) with 15 to 80 employees, owner-operated, in two named metropolitan areas, using a field-service management system or spreadsheets. Personas: owner, operations manager. Triggers: job postings for dispatchers or customer service staff; owner posts about missed calls or scheduling chaos; new location opening; recent negative reviews mentioning late technicians. Market size: about 900 companies in the two metros, estimated from industry directories. Disqualifiers: franchise locations whose corporate office controls systems, companies under 10 employees, companies outside the two metros.
:::

# Chapter 3: Designing an Offer Buyers Can Evaluate

:::objectives
- Explain the four variables of Alex Hormozi's value equation.
- Write a clear offer statement with a specific outcome, mechanism, and scope.
- Create a low-risk first step that reduces buyer effort.
- Avoid claims you cannot substantiate.
:::

An offer is the specific thing you propose to do for a client, for a specific price or price range, with a specific scope and expected outcome. On LinkedIn, where buyers evaluate you quickly and mostly on their own, a vague offer ("I help businesses grow") gives them nothing to evaluate. A clear offer ("I redesign dispatch and scheduling for service companies with 15 to 80 employees in a six-week engagement that typically starts with a two-hour process audit") lets a buyer decide in seconds whether to keep reading.

## The value equation

Entrepreneur Alex Hormozi, in his book $100M Offers, describes value as a function of four variables: the dream outcome and the perceived likelihood of achieving it, divided by the perceived time delay and the effort and sacrifice required [8]. The practical lesson is that you can raise the value of an offer without changing its price by making the outcome clearer, increasing the buyer's confidence, shortening the time to a first result, or reducing what the buyer must do.

| Variable | Question the buyer asks | How to improve it honestly |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | What will I get? | State the business outcome in the buyer's terms |
| Perceived likelihood | Will it work for us? | Explain your process; show real, documented work; offer a pilot |
| Time delay | How long until I see something? | Deliver an early, tangible output in week one |
| Effort and sacrifice | What will this cost me in time and disruption? | Do the heavy lifting; limit meetings; provide templates |

## Substantiation

Increasing perceived likelihood must never mean exaggeration. The Federal Trade Commission requires that objective claims in advertising be truthful and supported by evidence [9], and its Endorsement Guides explain that testimonials must not convey results that are not typical without clear disclosure [10]. If you have no client results yet, say so and offer a smaller, lower-risk first engagement. Describe your process and qualifications. Credibility built on honesty compounds; credibility built on inflated claims eventually collapses.

## The first step

Buyers often hesitate to commit to a large engagement with someone they found online. A paid diagnostic, audit, or pilot with a fixed fee and a clear deliverable reduces their risk and gives you a chance to demonstrate competence. It should be valuable on its own, not merely a sales pitch in disguise.

## Writing the offer in six steps

1. Name the client type in one phrase.
2. State the outcome in their language.
3. Describe the mechanism: what you do and how.
4. Define scope and duration.
5. Define a first step with a fixed price and deliverable.
6. List the evidence you can honestly provide.

:::example Illustrative: rewriting a weak offer
Before: "Helping businesses streamline operations with innovative solutions." After: "For residential service companies with 15 to 80 employees: I map your call-to-dispatch process, find where jobs and hours are lost, and implement a fixed scheduling playbook in six weeks. Most engagements start with a two-hour audit and written findings for a flat fee of $750. I'm early in my practice, so I'll show you my method and sample deliverables rather than client testimonials." The fee and scope are invented for illustration.
:::

:::worksheet Offer builder

Mechanism (what I do): ___

:::

:::summary
A clear offer names a client type, outcome, mechanism, scope, and first step. Hormozi's value equation shows how to raise value by improving outcome clarity and confidence while reducing time and effort. Every claim must be truthful and substantiated; a low-risk first step builds trust when you lack a track record.
:::

# Chapter 4: Your Profile as a Landing Page

:::objectives
- Treat each profile section as part of a buyer's research.
- Write a headline and About section that state who you help and how.
- Use the Featured and Experience sections to provide proof.
- Audit a profile against a buyer-centered checklist.
:::

When your message or post interests a prospect, the next thing they typically do is look at your profile. The profile is your landing page: it must confirm in seconds that you are relevant, and in a minute that you are credible. Most profiles are written as résumés for recruiters. A seller's profile should be written for buyers.

## Section by section

The photo should be a clear, recent, professional headshot; the banner can state your offer or show your work. The headline, which appears next to your name across LinkedIn, should say who you help and with what, not just your title. The About section should open with the buyer's problem, explain your approach, describe who it is for and not for, and end with a simple next step. The Featured section can hold a sample deliverable, a short article explaining your method, or a booking link. Experience entries should describe outcomes and responsibilities in plain language. Recommendations, if you have them, should come from real clients or colleagues and describe specific work.

| Section | Résumé style | Buyer style |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | "Owner at Smith Consulting" | "Helping service companies fix scheduling and dispatch, 6-week engagements" |
| About opening | "Experienced professional with a passion for excellence" | "If your office misses calls and techs drive across town twice, here's how I fix that" |
| Featured | Empty | Sample audit report (anonymized), method explainer, booking link |
| Experience | List of duties | Problems solved and the approach used |

## Writing the About section

Use a four-part structure. First, the problem in the buyer's words. Second, your approach and what makes it practical. Third, who it is for and who it is not for. Fourth, a call to action, such as a message prompt or a link to a short explainer. Keep paragraphs short. Avoid adjectives that cannot be verified ("world-class," "leading"). Concrete nouns and verbs build credibility.

## Seven-step profile build

1. Choose a clear headshot and a banner that states your offer.
2. Rewrite the headline in "who I help + with what" form.
3. Draft the About section using the four-part structure.
4. Add two or three Featured items that show how you work.
5. Rewrite recent Experience entries around problems solved.
6. Ask two real clients or colleagues for specific recommendations.
7. Have someone in your ICP read the profile and tell you what you do.

:::example Illustrative: an About section in four parts
"Most 20-to-60-person service companies lose jobs between the phone and the truck: calls go to voicemail, dispatch is done from memory, and technicians crisscross town. I help owners fix that. In six weeks I map your process, build a scheduling playbook your office can follow, and train your team to use it. It's a fit if you have at least two dispatch or office staff and use a scheduling tool or spreadsheet. It's not a fit if you need new software built. If you'd like to see a sample audit, message me the word 'audit' and I'll send one."
:::

:::checklist Profile audit
- Headline states who I help and with what.
- About section opens with the buyer's problem, not my biography.
- About section states who it is not for.
- Featured section shows a sample of my work or method.
- No unverifiable superlatives or invented results.
- A person in my ICP can explain what I do after 30 seconds.
:::

:::summary
Buyers check your profile before replying. Write it for them: a headline that states who you help, an About section that starts with their problem and ends with a next step, and Featured items that show how you work. Remove unverifiable claims.
:::

:::review
1. Why is a résumé-style profile weak for selling?
2. What are the four parts of a buyer-centered About section?
3. What belongs in the Featured section?
4. Rewrite your headline in "who I help + with what" form.
5. How can you test whether your profile communicates clearly?

# Chapter 5: A Sustainable Content System

:::objectives
- Explain the role of content in a system where most buyers are out of market.
- Choose three to five content pillars tied to your offer.
- Use repeatable post formats that teach rather than boast.
- Set a publishing cadence you can maintain for a year.
:::

Content is how you participate in the buyer's independent research without being present. A useful post explains a problem, a method, or a decision the buyer faces. Over months, a body of useful posts makes you familiar to the out-of-market majority described in Chapter 1, so that when one of them enters the market, you are already on the shortlist. Dawes's point about advertising applies to organic content as well: much of its value comes from building memory links that activate later [5].

## Content pillars

A pillar is a recurring theme connected to your offer. Three to five pillars are enough. For a consultant who fixes scheduling in service companies, pillars might be: the true cost of missed calls; dispatch and routing practices; hiring and training office staff; owner time management; and lessons from process audits (anonymized and with permission). Every post should fit a pillar. Posts that do not fit may get attention, but from the wrong people.

## Formats that teach

| Format | Structure | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnostic list | "Five signs your [process] is costing you [outcome]" | Helping buyers recognize a problem |
| Method walkthrough | Problem, steps, expected result, caveat | Demonstrating how you work |
| Worked example | Illustrative numbers that show a calculation | Making cost or benefit concrete |
| Myth and correction | Common belief, evidence, better practice | Positioning your expertise |
| Behind the work | What you learned from a project, anonymized and with permission | Showing real experience honestly |
| Question post | A specific question to your ICP | Learning language and pain points |

## Writing a useful post

Open with a line that names a specific situation your buyer recognizes. Deliver one idea with enough detail to act on. Use short paragraphs and plain language. End with a question or a light invitation, not a hard pitch. Never invent client stories or results. When you cite a statistic, name the source.

## Cadence

Consistency matters more than volume. Two or three posts per week, maintained for a year, typically produce more familiarity than a burst of daily posts followed by silence. Batch-write on one day; schedule or publish on others. Reserve time each day to reply to comments on your posts and to comment thoughtfully on posts by people in your ICP, which LinkedIn's SSI treats as part of engaging with insights [6].

:::case Justin Welsh: consistency is necessary but not sufficient
Justin Welsh, a former software sales executive who left corporate work in 2019 to build a one-person business, is one of the most visible examples of LinkedIn content turning into revenue. His account is instructive precisely because it begins with failure. In a 2025 LinkedIn post he wrote that he "published content every single day for 8.5 months without getting a single customer"; people liked his writing and commented, but no one reached out [18]. His diagnosis was that his writing was vague, his stories did not build credibility, and his calls to action were unclear. He reported making three changes: writing for one specific person rather than "an audience," sharing his own experience rather than generic advice, and ending posts with a simple, useful next step [18]. In another post he described the earlier mistake as "creating content to impress, not to convert," with "no clear positioning" and "no buyer-specific narrative" [19]. Welsh publicly reports more than $10 million in revenue from his business [19]. Those figures are self-reported, his business sells courses to a very large audience rather than services to a narrow ICP, and his results are his own, not TTI's. The transferable lesson is the one this chapter teaches: pillars tied to a specific buyer and a clear next step matter as much as volume.
:::

## Six steps to a content system

1. Choose three to five pillars tied to your offer.
2. List ten post ideas per pillar.
3. Pick two or three formats you can produce reliably.
4. Set a weekly cadence and a batch-writing block.
5. Spend 15 to 20 minutes daily commenting on ICP posts and replying to comments.
6. Review monthly which pillars and formats draw comments from your ICP, not just views.

:::example Illustrative: a two-week content calendar
Week 1. Monday: diagnostic list, "Five signs your office is losing booked jobs between the phone and the truck." Wednesday: worked example, the monthly cost of 12 missed calls at a $350 average ticket and a 40 percent booking rate (12 × 0.40 × $350 = $1,680 in potential revenue). Friday: question post, "Owners: who answers your phone after 5 p.m.?" Week 2. Monday: method walkthrough, "How to run a 30-minute dispatch huddle." Wednesday: myth and correction, "More technicians won't fix a scheduling problem." Friday: behind the work, a lesson from building a scheduling template, with no client named. The ticket size and booking rate are hypothetical.
:::

Pillar 2 and three post ideas: ___
Pillar 3 and three post ideas: ___
Formats I will use: ___
Weekly cadence and batch-writing time: ___
Daily engagement time block: ___

:::summary
Content lets you influence buyers during the independent research that dominates their journey and keeps you familiar to future buyers. Choose a few pillars tied to your offer, use teaching formats, never invent stories or results, and keep a cadence you can sustain.

# Chapter 6: Researching Prospects Within the Rules

:::objectives
- Build a prospect list manually using LinkedIn search and public sources.
- Understand what LinkedIn prohibits regarding automation and data copying.
- Record research notes that make outreach relevant.
- Prioritize prospects by fit and trigger.
:::

Relevant outreach requires research, and research requires a method. This chapter shows how to build and prioritize a list of prospects while respecting LinkedIn's rules and the privacy of the people you research.

## What LinkedIn prohibits

LinkedIn's help center states that it does not permit third-party software, including crawlers, bots, browser plug-ins, or extensions, that scrape, modify the appearance of, or automate activity on LinkedIn, and that using such tools violates its User Agreement and risks account restriction or shutdown [11]. The prohibitions it lists include using bots or unauthorized automated methods to add contacts, send messages, or engage with posts, and copying information obtained from the services without consent [11]. LinkedIn also says that automated activity may violate privacy legislation in some jurisdictions [12]. The practical rule is simple: do your research and outreach by hand, inside LinkedIn's own products, and do not export or copy profile data into other systems in ways the User Agreement forbids. Read the current User Agreement yourself [13].

## Manual list building

Use LinkedIn's search filters (location, industry, company size, title keywords) to find people who match your ICP, or a paid LinkedIn product if it suits your volume. Supplement with public sources outside LinkedIn: company websites, industry association directories, local business journals, job boards, and review sites. Keep your own notes about why each prospect fits and what trigger, if any, you observed, written in your words rather than copied profile data.

## Research notes that matter

For each prospect, aim to answer four questions in a few minutes: Does the company fit the ICP? Is this person a relevant persona? Is there a current trigger? What specific, respectful reason could I give for contacting them? If you cannot answer the last question, the prospect is not ready for outreach; follow them, engage with their content, and revisit later.

## Prioritizing

| Priority | Fit | Trigger | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Strong ICP match, right persona | Current trigger observed | Personal connection request this week |
| B | Strong ICP match, right persona | No current trigger | Follow; engage with content; revisit monthly |
| C | Partial match | Any | Light engagement only; do not pitch |
| Exclude | On disqualification list | Any | Do not contact |

## Seven steps to a researched list

1. Translate your ICP into LinkedIn search filters.
2. Review results by hand and open profiles that appear to fit.
3. Check the company website and one other public source.
4. Note fit, persona, trigger, and a reason to connect in your own words.
5. Assign A, B, C, or Exclude.
6. Limit A prospects to the number you can contact thoughtfully each week.
7. Re-review B prospects monthly for new triggers.

:::example Illustrative: a 30-minute research session
A hypothetical consultant searches for owners of HVAC companies in one metro area with 11 to 50 employees. The search returns about 140 people. In 30 minutes the consultant reviews 15 profiles and company websites. Results: 4 A prospects (two posted dispatcher job openings this month, one opened a second location, one owner wrote a post about after-hours calls); 7 B prospects (good fit, no trigger); 3 C prospects; 1 Exclude (a franchise location). The consultant writes one sentence for each A prospect stating the reason to connect.
:::

:::checklist Research within the rules
- I use no bots, scrapers, or automation extensions on LinkedIn.
- I do not copy profile data into other systems in ways the User Agreement forbids.
- My notes are my own observations, written in my own words.
- Each A prospect has a specific, respectful reason for contact.
- I have read the current LinkedIn User Agreement.
:::

:::summary
Research makes relevance possible. LinkedIn prohibits scraping and automation tools, so build lists manually with its search and with public sources, record your own notes, and prioritize by fit and trigger. Contact only those for whom you have a genuine reason.
:::

2. Name three public sources outside LinkedIn useful for research.

# Chapter 7: Connection Requests That Get Accepted

:::objectives
- Understand LinkedIn's invitation limits and why they exist.
- Write connection requests that state a relevant, specific reason.
- Avoid pitching in the connection request.
- Track acceptance rate as a measure of relevance.
:::

A connection request is a small ask: "May I be part of your professional network?" Treat it as a greeting, not a sales pitch. Its job is to be accepted, not to book a meeting.

## Limits and what they signal

LinkedIn states that all members, including Premium subscribers, are subject to invitation limits, and that accounts may be temporarily restricted if they send many invitations in a short time or if many invitations are ignored, left pending, or marked as spam [14][15]. LinkedIn also notes that basic members can add a personalized message to only a limited number of connection requests per month, while Premium members can personalize more [14]. These limits exist to keep invitations relevant. A high acceptance rate is a sign you are inviting the right people with a good reason; a low one is a warning that you are not.

## Anatomy of a good request

A good request, when you can add a note, has three parts in two or three short sentences: who you are in a few words, the specific reason you are reaching out (often a trigger or a shared context), and a low-pressure close. It does not contain a link, a calendar invitation, or a description of your services.

| Weak request | Why it fails | Stronger request |
|---|---|---|
| "I'd love to add you to my network." | No reason | "Saw your post about after-hours calls; I write about dispatch for service companies and would value connecting." |
| "We help companies 10x revenue. Book a call: [link]" | Pitch, link, unverifiable claim | "Congrats on the second location. I work with HVAC operators on scheduling and follow a lot of growth stories like yours." |
| "Hi [FirstName], I noticed we're both in business." | Template, irrelevant | "We're both members of the regional contractors' association; I'd be glad to connect." |

## When you cannot add a note

If you cannot personalize, rely on the relevance of the person and the strength of your profile. Engaging with a prospect's posts for a week or two before sending a request makes your name familiar when the invitation arrives.

## Six steps for connection requests

1. Engage with the prospect's content once or twice where you have something useful to add.
2. Confirm your profile is buyer-ready (Chapter 4).
3. Write a request with a specific reason and no pitch.
4. Send a modest number per day, spread over the week.
5. Withdraw requests that remain pending after several weeks.
6. Track acceptance rate weekly; investigate if it falls.

:::example Illustrative: acceptance rate as a diagnostic
A hypothetical seller sends 40 requests in a week: 20 to A prospects with a trigger-based note and 20 to people chosen only by title, without notes. Results: 14 of 20 A prospects accept (70 percent); 5 of 20 title-only prospects accept (25 percent). The seller concludes that research and relevance, not volume, drive acceptance and shifts all invitations to researched prospects. The rates are invented to illustrate the calculation.
:::

:::checklist Before you click Connect
- This person matches my ICP and persona.
- I have a specific, honest reason to connect.
- My note contains no pitch, link, or claim.
- My profile is ready for their visit.
- I am within a pace that keeps my acceptance rate healthy.
:::

:::summary
Connection requests should be relevant greetings, not pitches. LinkedIn's invitation limits and restrictions reward relevance and penalize spray-and-pray behavior. Use a specific reason, engage first where possible, and treat acceptance rate as a measure of targeting quality.

# Chapter 8: Conversations, Not Sequences

:::objectives
- Open a direct-message conversation without pitching.
- Use a permission-based approach to move toward a call.
- Follow up a limited number of times and then stop respectfully.
- Handle common replies, including "not now" and "no."
:::

Once a connection is accepted, many sellers immediately send a long pitch. Buyers recognize this pattern and, as Gartner's survey suggests, avoid suppliers whose outreach is irrelevant [3]. A better approach treats direct messages as the beginning of a professional conversation.

## The first message

The first message after acceptance should thank the person, reference the reason you connected, and offer something useful or ask a genuine question about their situation. It should be short enough to read on a phone without scrolling.

## Asking permission

Before offering a call, ask whether the topic is relevant to them now. A permission question ("Is scheduling something you're working on this year, or is it running smoothly?") respects their time and gives them an easy way to say no. If they say it is relevant, offer a specific, short next step. If not, thank them and stay connected; they may enter the market later.

## Follow-up rules

Follow up once or twice at most if there is no reply, spacing messages by at least a week, and make each follow-up add something new, such as a relevant article or an answer to a common question. Then stop. Persistent messaging after silence damages your reputation with someone who might become a buyer later.

| Reply | Meaning | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Interested, asks a question | In market or curious | Answer fully; offer a short call |
| "Not right now" | Out of market | Thank them; ask if you may check back in a set number of months; note it |
| "No thanks" | Not interested | Thank them; do not message again about the offer |
| Referral to colleague | Wrong persona | Thank them; contact the colleague, mentioning the referral |
| No reply after two follow-ups | Unknown | Stop messaging; continue light engagement with content |

## Six steps for a conversation

1. Send a short thank-you message referencing why you connected.
2. Ask a genuine question or share something useful, with no pitch.
3. If they engage, ask a permission question about relevance.
4. If relevant, propose a specific 20-minute call with a clear agenda.
5. If silent, follow up at most twice with new value, then stop.
6. Record outcomes and check-back dates.

:::example Illustrative: a four-message conversation
Message 1 (after acceptance): "Thanks for connecting, Dana. Congratulations on the new Eastside location. How are you handling dispatch across two sites?" Dana replies that it has been chaotic. Message 2: "That's common in the first few months. One thing that often helps is a single shared board with a 10-minute morning huddle. Is tightening up scheduling a priority this quarter, or are other things ahead of it?" Dana says it is a priority. Message 3: "Would a 20-minute call next week be useful? I'd ask about your current flow and share two or three ideas you could use whether or not we work together." Dana accepts. Message 4: a calendar confirmation with a three-point agenda. The names and situation are invented.
:::

:::worksheet Message drafting

First message (thank, reference, question): ___
Useful resource or insight to share: ___
Permission question: ___
Proposed next step and agenda: ___
Follow-up 1 (new value, date): ___
Follow-up 2 (new value, date): ___
Check-back date if "not now": ___
:::

:::summary
Treat direct messages as conversations. Thank, reference, and ask before you offer. Use a permission question to test relevance, follow up no more than twice with new value, and respect "not now" and "no." The out-of-market prospect today may be a buyer next year.
:::

# Chapter 9: Discovery Calls That Qualify

:::objectives
- Structure a 20-to-30-minute discovery call.
- Ask questions that uncover problem, impact, priority, and decision process.
- Use a qualification framework to decide whether to propose.
- End every call with a clear next step.
:::

A discovery call is a structured conversation to determine whether a prospect has a problem you can solve, whether solving it is a priority, and whether there is a path to a decision. It is not a presentation. The prospect should talk most of the time.

## A call structure

| Segment | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | 2 min | Thank them, confirm time, state the agenda, ask what they want from the call |
| Current state | 6–8 min | How things work now; who is involved; what tools are used |
| Problem and impact | 6–8 min | What isn't working; what it costs in time, money, or stress |
| Priority and timing | 3–4 min | Why now; what happens if nothing changes; other priorities |
| Decision process | 3–4 min | Who else is involved; how decisions are made; budget approach |
| Next step | 2–3 min | Summarize; agree on a specific next action and date |

## Questions that work

Open questions invite description: "Walk me through what happens when a customer calls after hours." Impact questions quantify: "Roughly how many calls a week go to voicemail?" Priority questions test urgency: "Where does this rank against the other things on your list this quarter?" Decision questions map the buying group: "Besides you, who would weigh in on a change like this?"

## Qualifying

Many qualification frameworks exist. A simple one asks four things: Is there a real problem I can solve? Is the impact large enough to justify the cost? Is it a current priority? Is there an identifiable decision process and budget? If any answer is clearly no, the honest move is to say so, offer a useful suggestion, and stay in touch. Proposing to an unqualified prospect wastes both parties' time.

:::case Gong's analysis of recorded discovery calls
Gong, a company that sells software for recording and analyzing sales conversations, has published some of the most widely cited data on how discovery calls are conducted. Its discovery-call checklist, based on its analysis of recorded B2B calls, reports that the best results came when sellers asked between 11 and 14 targeted questions, and that winning discovery calls had a talk-to-listen ratio of about 46 percent seller to 54 percent buyer [21]. The same guide advises against front-loading questions at the start of the call and recommends spreading them through the conversation so that it feels like a dialogue rather than an interrogation [21]. Two cautions apply. First, this is vendor research based on its customers' recorded calls: it is correlational, not a controlled experiment, and it describes larger software sales teams more than small service firms. Second, the lesson is not a target number but a pattern: ask focused questions about the buyer's business, follow up on the answers, and let the buyer do much of the talking. Gong's findings describe its customers' calls, not TTI's.
:::

## Six steps for a discovery call

1. Send a brief agenda in advance.
2. Open by confirming time and asking their goals for the call.
3. Spend most of the call on current state, problem, and impact.
4. Test priority and map the decision process.
5. Summarize what you heard and check accuracy.
6. Agree on a specific next step with a date, or agree to part ways.

:::example Illustrative: quantifying impact on a call
The hypothetical owner estimates that about 15 after-hours calls a week go to voicemail and that about one in three such callers would book if answered. The average ticket is $300. Weekly potential revenue not captured: 15 × (1/3) × $300 = $1,500, or about $78,000 a year if the pattern held. The seller does not claim to recover all of it. Instead, the seller asks: "If a change captured even a portion of that, would it be worth a closer look this quarter?" The owner agrees, and they schedule a 45-minute process review. All figures come from the owner's own estimates in this invented scenario.
:::

:::checklist Discovery call quality
- The buyer talked at least as much as I did.
- I understand the current process and the people involved.
- The prospect quantified the problem in their own numbers.
- I know whether this is a current priority.
- I know who else is involved in the decision.
- We agreed on a dated next step, or agreed it is not a fit.
:::

:::summary
Discovery calls qualify; they do not pitch. Structure the call around current state, problem and impact, priority, and decision process, and let the prospect quantify the problem in their own numbers. End with a specific next step or an honest conclusion that it is not a fit.
:::

# Chapter 10: Proposals, Objections, and Closing

:::objectives
- Write a one-to-two-page proposal that mirrors the prospect's words.
- Present options that make a decision easy.
- Respond to common objections with questions and evidence.
- Close with a clear, low-friction next step.
:::

A proposal should confirm a decision the prospect is already close to making, not introduce new information. If the discovery process was thorough, the proposal summarizes what you heard, describes what you will do, and explains the investment and terms.

## Proposal structure

1. Situation: the prospect's current state and goals, in their words.
2. Objectives: two to four measurable outcomes you agreed on.
3. Approach: phases, deliverables, and timeline.
4. Options: usually two or three, differing in scope or support.
5. Investment and terms: price, payment schedule, what is included and excluded.
6. Next step: how to accept and what happens immediately after.

Keep it short. Use their language from the discovery call. Avoid guarantees of outcomes you do not control.

## Options

Offering two or three options lets the buyer choose how to work with you rather than whether to. Options typically vary in scope (a narrower or broader engagement), speed, or level of support. Each should be a complete, honest offer, not a decoy.

## Objections

Treat an objection as a request for information. Respond by asking a clarifying question, then answering with facts.

| Objection | Clarifying question | Helpful response |
|---|---|---|
| "It's too expensive." | "Compared to what?" | Revisit the impact numbers; offer a smaller first phase |
| "We need to think about it." | "What would you want to be sure of before deciding?" | Address the specific concern; agree on a decision date |
| "We could do it ourselves." | "What would that look like in your team's time?" | Compare internal time cost; offer templates or a hybrid |
| "We're too busy right now." | "When does it make sense to revisit?" | Schedule a check-in; propose a lighter first step |
| "Can you guarantee results?" | "What result matters most to you?" | Explain your process, what you control, and a pilot or milestone review |

## Six steps to a clean close

1. Review the proposal together live rather than only sending it.
2. Walk through the situation and objectives first; confirm agreement.
3. Present options and invite questions.
4. Address objections with clarifying questions and facts.
5. Ask directly: "Which option would you like to move forward with?"
6. Send a simple agreement and an onboarding schedule promptly.

:::example Illustrative: three options
For the hypothetical HVAC company: Option 1, process audit only: two-hour session plus a written findings report, $750. Option 2, audit plus scheduling playbook: six weeks, playbook, two staff training sessions, $4,800. Option 3, audit, playbook, and 90 days of weekly coaching calls: $7,900. The owner chooses Option 2 and asks to add one coaching call per month, which the seller prices separately. Prices are invented.
:::

:::worksheet Proposal outline

Agreed objectives (2–4): ___
Approach, phases, and timeline: ___
Option 1 (scope, price): ___
Option 2 (scope, price): ___
Option 3 (scope, price): ___
Inclusions and exclusions: ___
How to accept and first step after acceptance: ___
:::

:::summary
A good proposal mirrors the discovery conversation, offers honest options, and states terms clearly. Objections are requests for information; answer them with clarifying questions and facts, not pressure. Review proposals live and ask directly for a decision.
:::

# Chapter 11: Pipeline Metrics and Weekly Rhythm

:::objectives
- Define the stages of a LinkedIn-sourced pipeline.
- Track conversion rates between stages with honest denominators.
- Run a weekly review that identifies the constraint.
- Avoid vanity metrics.
:::

A pipeline is the set of opportunities at each stage of your sales process. Measuring it tells you where the process is working and where it is stuck. Without measurement, sellers tend to respond to slow months by doing more of everything, which often means more of whatever is not working.

## Stages and conversion rates

| Stage | Definition | Conversion metric |
|---|---|---|
| Researched | A prospects with notes | — |
| Invited | Connection request sent | Acceptance rate = accepted ÷ invited |
| Conversing | Replied to a message | Reply rate = replied ÷ accepted |
| Call booked | Discovery call scheduled | Booking rate = booked ÷ conversing |
| Qualified | Met qualification criteria | Qualification rate = qualified ÷ calls held |
| Proposal | Proposal reviewed | Proposal rate = proposals ÷ qualified |
| Won | Agreement signed | Win rate = won ÷ proposals |

Always state the denominator. "Six calls this month" means little without knowing whether it came from 20 conversations or 200.

## Finding the constraint

The constraint is the stage with the weakest conversion relative to what is achievable. If acceptance is low, revisit targeting and connection notes. If replies are low, revisit first messages. If calls are booked but rarely qualify, revisit your ICP or triggers. If proposals rarely close, revisit discovery depth, options, or pricing. Fix one constraint at a time.

## Vanity metrics

Post impressions, follower counts, and profile views are interesting but are not sales outcomes. Track them lightly, and track ICP comments, conversations started, and calls booked closely.

## Weekly rhythm

| Day | Activity |
|---|---|
| Monday | Research and prioritize A prospects; plan the week's content |
| Tuesday–Thursday | Publish posts; send invitations; reply to messages; hold calls |
| Daily | 15–20 minutes engaging with ICP content |
| Friday | Update the pipeline; calculate conversion rates; choose next week's one improvement |

## Five steps to a weekly review

1. Update counts at each stage.
2. Calculate each conversion rate with its denominator.
3. Compare with the previous four weeks.
4. Identify the single weakest stage.
5. Choose one change to test next week and record it.

:::example Illustrative: diagnosing a pipeline
Four hypothetical weeks: 120 researched, 100 invited, 62 accepted (62 percent), 21 replied (34 percent of accepted), 7 calls booked (33 percent of conversing), 6 held, 3 qualified (50 percent), 2 proposals, 1 won. The seller notices that reply rate is the weakest stage relative to acceptance and that first messages have been asking for calls too early. Next week's test: first messages ask a situational question only. Two weeks later, replies rise to 15 of 30 accepted (50 percent). All figures are invented to illustrate the method.
:::

:::worksheet Weekly pipeline review
Week ending: ___
Researched / invited / accepted: ___
Replied / calls booked / calls held: ___
Qualified / proposals / won: ___
Weakest conversion this week: ___
Likely cause: ___
One change to test next week: ___
:::

:::summary
Measure each pipeline stage with honest denominators, find the weakest conversion, and fix one constraint at a time. Ignore vanity metrics in favor of conversations, calls, and qualified opportunities. A simple weekly rhythm turns activity into learning.
:::

# Chapter 12: Ethics, Compliance, and Scaling

:::objectives
- Summarize the ethical and platform rules that govern LinkedIn selling.
- Recognize practices that put accounts and reputations at risk.
- Decide when and how to add team members or paid tools.
- Build a system that remains relevant as volume grows.
:::

A LinkedIn sales system is built on reputation. Everything in this book depends on buyers believing that you are who you say you are and that your messages are worth reading. This chapter collects the rules and principles that protect that trust.

## Platform rules

LinkedIn's Professional Community Policies ask members to be safe, trustworthy, and professional, and LinkedIn may restrict accounts that violate its policies [16]. Its help center prohibits automation tools, scraping, and inauthentic engagement [11]. Its invitation limits and restrictions discourage high-volume, low-relevance invitations [15]. Engagement pods, purchased followers, fake profiles, and automated commenting all violate the spirit, and often the letter, of these rules.

## Truth in claims

The FTC's guidance requires that advertising claims be truthful and backed by evidence [9], and its Endorsement Guides address testimonials and material connections [10]. If someone recommends you because you paid them or gave them something of value, that connection should be disclosed. Never invent clients, case studies, or numbers.

## Privacy and respect

Use what people share publicly for the purpose they shared it: professional networking. Do not export contact data in violation of the User Agreement, add people to email lists without a lawful basis, or contact people who have asked you to stop.

| Practice | Risk | Better alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Automation extensions | Account restriction; User Agreement violation | Manual, researched outreach |
| Engagement pods | Inauthentic engagement; reputational harm | Genuine comments on ICP posts |
| Invented testimonials | Deception; FTC exposure | Describe your process; offer a pilot |
| Mass templated invitations | Restrictions; low acceptance | Fewer, researched invitations |
| Copying profile data into email lists | Policy and privacy violations | Ask for email in conversation |

## Scaling

Scale only what works. Once your pipeline metrics are stable, consider whether a paid LinkedIn product helps you research or message more efficiently within the rules, whether an assistant can handle scheduling and pipeline updates, or whether a second seller should be trained on your system. Keep the research and personalization standard intact. Before you delegate any of it, write the process down, or better, record it.

:::case Dan Martell's Camcorder Method
Dan Martell is a Canadian software entrepreneur, investor, and founder of SaaS Academy, a coaching company for software founders. In Buy Back Your Time (Portfolio, 2023) he argues that owners should hire to free their own time rather than simply to grow, using a three-step Buyback Loop: audit how you spend your time and energy, transfer low-value or draining tasks to others, and fill the recovered time with higher-value work [17]. For the transfer step he teaches the Camcorder Method: record yourself, screen and voice, doing the task while narrating your choices, ideally several times to capture variations, then have the new team member watch the recordings and write the playbook themselves. In interviews Martell explains that the playbook the hire produces is itself a test of whether they understood the work, and that the recordings remain as training material even if the hire does not work out [20]. In a LinkedIn sales system, the research routine, the A/B/C prioritization, and the Friday pipeline review are natural candidates for this method; message writing, where relevance depends on judgment, is usually the last thing to hand off. Martell's results are his own, not TTI's.
:::

## Five steps to scale responsibly

1. Confirm at least two months of stable conversion rates.
2. Document each step of your process with examples and recordings.
3. Add one resource at a time: tool, assistant, or seller.
4. Monitor acceptance and reply rates for signs of declining relevance.
5. Review platform policies quarterly.

:::example Illustrative: adding an assistant
A hypothetical consultant spends about six hours a week on research and pipeline updates. After documenting both tasks with screen recordings and a checklist, the consultant hires a part-time assistant for five hours a week at $25 per hour ($125 per week). The consultant keeps writing all messages personally and reviews the assistant's research notes for the first month. Acceptance rate holds steady; the consultant gains about five hours a week for discovery calls and delivery. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Integrity audit
- No automation tools, pods, or purchased engagement.
- No invented testimonials, clients, or results.
- Material connections disclosed when others recommend me.
- No profile data exported in violation of the User Agreement.
- "No" and "stop" are honored immediately and recorded.
- Platform policies reviewed this quarter.
:::

:::summary
Trust is the foundation of LinkedIn selling. Follow LinkedIn's policies, make only truthful claims, respect privacy, and honor refusals. Scale only after metrics stabilize, document your process, and add resources one at a time while protecting relevance.
:::

1. Name four practices that violate LinkedIn's rules or spirit.
2. What do the FTC's Endorsement Guides require about material connections?
3. Why should you scale only after metrics stabilize?

# Appendix E: Discovery Call Guide

Before the call: review your research card, the prospect's recent posts, and their company website. Send a three-point agenda the day before.

Opening (2 minutes): "Thanks for making time. I have us down for 25 minutes; does that still work? I'd like to understand how [process] works today, what's working and what isn't, and then we can decide together whether a next step makes sense. What would make this call useful for you?"

Current state (6–8 minutes): "Walk me through what happens when [trigger event, such as a customer calling]." "Who's involved?" "What tools do you use?" "How long has it worked this way?"

Problem and impact (6–8 minutes): "What part of that is most frustrating?" "How often does that happen in a typical week?" "What does it cost you, in time, money, or customer experience?" "Have you tried to fix it before? What happened?"

Priority and timing (3–4 minutes): "Why is this coming up now?" "What happens if nothing changes for six months?" "Where does it rank against other priorities?"

Decision process (3–4 minutes): "Besides you, who would weigh in on a change like this?" "How do you usually evaluate outside help?" "Do you have a range in mind for addressing this, or should I suggest options?"

Close (2–3 minutes): "Here's what I heard: [summary]. Did I get that right?" Then either: "Would it make sense to [specific next step] on [date]?" or "Honestly, I don't think I'm the right fit for this, but here's what I'd suggest."

# Appendix G: An Illustrative Profile Rewrite

The following before-and-after example applies Chapter 4 to a hypothetical independent consultant. The person, numbers, and wording are invented for teaching purposes.

## Before

Headline: "Consultant | Entrepreneur | Passionate About Helping Businesses Grow | Husband and Dad"

About: "I am a results-driven professional with over 15 years of experience in operations. I love helping businesses reach their full potential. Let's connect!"

Featured: empty. Experience: a list of former job titles with no descriptions.

## Diagnosis

The headline does not name a buyer or a problem. "Results-driven" and "full potential" are claims without evidence. The About section describes the seller rather than the buyer's situation. Nothing on the profile helps a visiting owner decide whether a conversation is worth their time, and nothing offers a next step.

## After

Headline: "I help HVAC and plumbing companies (10–50 employees) stop losing booked jobs between the phone and the truck | Scheduling and dispatch systems"

About, paragraph 1 (the buyer's situation): "If you own a home-services company, you know the pattern: the phones ring, jobs get booked, and somewhere between the office and the technician something slips. A missed callback, a double-booked afternoon, a customer who was never told the tech was running late."

About, paragraph 2 (what you do and how): "I spent 15 years running operations for regional service companies, including dispatch for a 40-technician team. Now I help owners redesign how calls become scheduled jobs: who answers, how jobs are booked, how the board is managed, and how customers are kept informed."

About, paragraph 3 (honest proof): "I'm early in my independent practice, so instead of testimonials I offer a two-hour process audit at a fixed price. You'll get a written findings report whether or not we work together."

About, paragraph 4 (next step): "If scheduling is on your list this year, send me a message with a sentence about your setup. I post twice a week about dispatch, staffing, and customer communication."

Featured: a downloadable one-page dispatch huddle checklist; a post walking through the cost of after-hours voicemails with illustrative numbers; a short description of the process audit.

Experience: the former operations role rewritten to describe responsibilities and scope (team size, number of daily jobs) without claiming credit for outcomes that cannot be verified.

## Why it works

The new headline lets the right buyer self-identify in seconds. The About section mirrors the buyer's experience, then explains the seller's relevant background in plain terms. Instead of inventing proof, it offers a low-risk first step that lets the buyer evaluate the seller directly, consistent with the FTC's expectation that claims be supported [9]. Every element points toward a conversation.

# Appendix H: Practice Drills

Skills improve with deliberate practice. Use these drills alone, with a colleague, or by recording yourself.

## Drill 1: The ten-minute research sprint

Set a timer for ten minutes. Choose one prospect from your list. Find their company website, one recent public signal (a post, a job listing, a news item), and one likely problem related to your offer. Write a two-sentence reason to connect. Repeat with a second prospect. Over a few weeks, the time needed per prospect should fall while the quality of your reasons rises.

## Drill 2: Rewriting weak messages

Take each weak message below and rewrite it using the principles in Chapters 7 and 8. Compare your version against the checklist: specific reason, no pitch, short, honest, easy to answer.

1. "Hi! I help businesses like yours scale. Do you have 15 minutes this week?"
2. "Just following up on my last three messages. Did you see them?"
3. "We've helped hundreds of companies triple their leads. Want to be next?"
4. "I noticed you're the owner. Are you open to new opportunities?"

## Drill 3: Objection role-play

Ask a colleague to play a prospect and raise each objection from the Chapter 10 table in turn. Respond first with a clarifying question, then with a factual answer. Record the session and review: Did you ask before answering? Did you avoid pressure and unsupported claims? Did you propose a clear next step?

## Drill 4: Impact questions

Write three impact questions for your offer that help a buyer estimate the cost of the problem in their own numbers: one about frequency, one about value per occurrence, and one about consequences. Practice asking them aloud until they sound conversational rather than scripted.

## Drill 5: The weekly teardown

Every Friday, choose one conversation that went well and one that stalled. For each, identify the message or moment that made the difference. Write one sentence on what you will repeat and one on what you will change. Keep these notes; after a quarter they become your own playbook.

# Glossary

Acceptance rate: The share of connection requests accepted, calculated as accepted divided by sent.

Buying group: The set of people who influence or approve a B2B purchase.

Buying trigger: An observable event that makes a purchase more likely soon, such as a new location, new leader, or hiring spree.

Camcorder Method: Dan Martell's practice of recording yourself performing a task, with narration, so someone else can learn it and write the playbook.

Constraint: The pipeline stage with the weakest conversion, which limits overall results.

Content pillar: A recurring content theme tied to your offer and your buyer's problems.

Conversion rate: The share of items at one pipeline stage that move to the next.

Discovery call: A structured conversation to determine whether a prospect has a problem you can solve, whether it is a priority, and how a decision would be made.

Disqualifier: A characteristic that makes a prospect a poor fit regardless of interest.

Endorsement Guides: FTC guidance on the use of testimonials and endorsements in advertising, including disclosure of material connections.

First-step offer: A small, low-risk paid or free engagement that lets a buyer evaluate you before a larger commitment.

ICP (ideal customer profile): A description of the companies and people most likely to benefit from and buy your offer.

In-market buyer: A buyer currently evaluating a purchase in your category.

Material connection: A relationship between an endorser and a seller, such as payment or a gift, that might affect how people weigh the endorsement.

Out-of-market buyer: A potential buyer not currently evaluating a purchase; most buyers at any time.

Permission question: A question that asks whether a topic is relevant before offering a meeting or pitch.

Persona: A representative description of a role within the buying group.

Pipeline: The set of opportunities at each stage of the sales process.

Proposal: A short document that summarizes the buyer's situation, agreed objectives, approach, options, and terms.

Qualification: The process of deciding whether an opportunity is worth pursuing, based on problem, impact, priority, and decision process.

Social Selling Index (SSI): LinkedIn's score measuring professional brand, finding the right people, engaging with insights, and building relationships.

Substantiation: Evidence that supports an advertising claim before it is made.

Value equation: Alex Hormozi's model in which perceived value rises with dream outcome and perceived likelihood of achievement and falls with time delay and effort and sacrifice.

Vanity metric: A measure such as impressions or follower count that is easy to grow but weakly tied to sales outcomes.

95-5 rule: The LinkedIn B2B Institute heuristic that roughly 95 percent of B2B buyers are out of market at any given time.
