# How to Use This Book

Owners of small businesses often experience their companies as a series of fires: a late delivery, an angry customer, a missed invoice, an employee who quits. Each fire gets its own fix, and the same fires return. Systems thinking offers a different lens. Instead of asking "Who made this mistake?" it asks "What about the way our work is designed made this mistake likely?" W. Edwards Deming, the statistician whose methods shaped postwar Japanese manufacturing, estimated that in his experience about 94 percent of troubles and possibilities for improvement belong to the system, which he called the responsibility of management, and about 6 percent to special causes ([The W. Edwards Deming Institute](https://deming.org/appreciation-for-a-system/)).

This book teaches a practical sequence for applying that idea in an owner-led business: draw the boundary of a system, map how work actually flows, distinguish events from patterns and structures, find feedback loops and delays, locate the bottleneck, test small changes using the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle, and lock in improvements with standard work and review routines. It draws on the Institute for Healthcare Improvement's Model for Improvement, the Lean Enterprise Institute's value stream mapping, Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, Donella Meadows's leverage points, and documented case histories from Toyota and Virginia Mason.

## Who This Book Is For

- Owners and managers of service, trade, retail, and professional businesses with roughly 2 to 50 people.
- Operations leads who want a disciplined way to improve recurring processes.
- Anyone who has fixed the same problem more than twice.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Define a system's purpose, boundary, inputs, outputs, and customers.
- Draw a current-state map of how work actually flows.
- Separate one-off events from recurring patterns and the structures that cause them.
- Recognize reinforcing and balancing feedback loops and the delays that distort them.
- Find and manage the constraint that limits throughput.
- Design small, safe tests of change and measure their effects.
- Standardize what works and build a review rhythm that sustains it.

## How Each Chapter Is Built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives and key definitions, presents a step-by-step method, works through an illustrative example (a hypothetical business used to demonstrate the method), and, where relevant, summarizes a documented case with citations. Chapters close with a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions.

:::warning Disclaimer
This book is educational. It does not provide legal, financial, safety, or engineering advice. Case studies describe the documented experiences of named organizations; their results are their own, not TTI's, and are not typical or guaranteed. Test changes on a small scale and consult qualified professionals where safety, regulation, or significant money is involved.
:::

# Chapter 1: What a System Is and Why It Matters

:::objectives
- Define a system and its essential parts.
- Explain why system design drives most performance.
- Distinguish common-cause from special-cause variation.
- Describe the owner's role as designer of the system.
:::

## Defining a System

A **system** is a set of elements connected to achieve a purpose. Donella Meadows, a pioneer of system dynamics, emphasized that the most important parts of a system are often not the visible elements but the interconnections and the purpose ([Donella Meadows Project](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/)). In a business, the elements include people, equipment, software, and materials; the interconnections include handoffs, information flows, rules, and incentives; the purpose is the result the system produces for customers.

| Part | Definition | Example in a plumbing company |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | What the system is for | Fix customers' plumbing problems promptly and correctly |
| Elements | People, tools, materials | Dispatcher, technicians, trucks, parts, scheduling software |
| Interconnections | Handoffs, information, rules | Call to dispatch, dispatch to tech, tech to billing |
| Inputs | What enters | Customer calls, parts deliveries |
| Outputs | What leaves | Completed repairs, invoices, reviews |
| Boundary | Where the system ends | From first call to payment received |

## Why the System Matters More Than Effort

Deming's Institute summarizes his view that "a bad system will beat a good person every time" ([The W. Edwards Deming Institute](https://deming.org/a-bad-system-will-beat-a-good-person-every-time/)). If the scheduling software allows double bookings, a careful dispatcher will still double-book occasionally. If parts are stored in three places, technicians will waste time searching regardless of how hard they work. Exhorting people to try harder does not change the system that produces the result.

## Common Causes and Special Causes

Deming distinguished **common-cause variation**, the routine ups and downs built into a stable process, from **special-cause variation**, which comes from unusual, identifiable events. His 94/6 estimate refers to this distinction: most variation and most improvement opportunity lie in the design of the system rather than in isolated events ([Deming Institute](https://deming.org/appreciation-for-a-system/)). The practical consequence is significant. Reacting to every common-cause fluctuation as if it were special (for example, scolding a technician for one slow week) adds noise and blame without changing results. Improving the process changes the whole distribution.

| Variation type | Description | Appropriate response |
|---|---|---|
| Common cause | Routine, built into the process | Redesign the process |
| Special cause | Unusual, assignable event | Investigate and address the specific event |

## The Owner as System Designer

In an owner-led business, the owner usually designed the system, often without realizing it. Every rule, tool choice, hiring decision, and habit accumulated into "how we do things." Systems thinking asks the owner to step back from working in the system and work on it: observe, map, test, and redesign.

:::example Illustrative: The Late Invoice Problem
A landscaping company owner notices invoices often go out a week or more after jobs finish. Her first instinct is to remind the office manager to be faster. Viewed as a system, the delay has several structural causes: crews submit paper job sheets only on Fridays; job sheets often lack materials used; the office manager must call crew leads to fill gaps; and invoices are batched monthly. None of these is the office manager's personal failing. Changing the handoff (crews photograph job sheets in an app at each job's end, with required fields for materials) addresses the cause.
:::

:::checklist System Awareness
- I can state the purpose of my business's main system in one sentence.
- I can name its elements, interconnections, inputs, outputs, and boundary.
- I understand the difference between common-cause and special-cause variation.
- I am willing to look at design before blaming individuals.
- I have identified one recurring problem to study with this book.
:::

:::summary
A system is a set of connected elements with a purpose. Deming estimated that most troubles and improvement opportunities belong to the system rather than to individuals, and he distinguished routine common-cause variation from unusual special causes. Owners design the systems their businesses run on, and improving results requires working on that design rather than exhorting greater effort.
:::

# Chapter 2: Drawing the Boundary and Defining Flow

:::objectives
- Choose a system to study and define its boundary.
- Identify customers, purpose, inputs, and outputs with a SIPOC.
- Distinguish material, information, and work flows.
- Write a problem statement grounded in data.
:::

## Choosing What to Study

Systems thinking can become abstract quickly. Start with one value stream: a sequence of steps that delivers a specific product or service to a customer. The Lean Enterprise Institute defines a value stream as all of the actions, both value-creating and non-value-creating, required to bring a product from order to delivery ([Lean Enterprise Institute](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/value-stream/)). Good candidates for a first study are processes that are frequent, painful, and owned by your business: lead to booked appointment, order to delivery, job completion to payment, or new hire to productive employee.

## Setting the Boundary

A **boundary** defines where the system begins and ends for the purpose of the study. A boundary too narrow ("the invoicing step") misses causes upstream; a boundary too wide ("the whole company") becomes unmanageable. A useful rule is to start at the customer's trigger (a call, an order, a request) and end at the customer's outcome (service delivered, payment received).

## The SIPOC

A **SIPOC** is a one-page summary of Suppliers, Inputs, Process (five to seven high-level steps), Outputs, and Customers. It ensures everyone studying the system agrees on scope.

| Suppliers | Inputs | Process | Outputs | Customers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customers; parts distributor; answering service | Service request; parts; schedule | 1. Request received 2. Scheduled 3. Dispatched 4. Repair done 5. Invoiced 6. Paid | Completed repair; invoice; review request | Homeowners; property managers |

## Three Kinds of Flow

Work moves through three interwoven flows:

1. **Material flow:** physical items (parts, products, paperwork).
2. **Information flow:** orders, schedules, messages, approvals.
3. **Work flow:** the sequence of tasks people perform.

Value stream mapping, as taught by the Lean Enterprise Institute, deliberately captures both material and information flows, because problems frequently originate in how information moves ([Lean Enterprise Institute](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/value-stream-mapping/)). A job that is physically ready may sit idle for days because nobody told billing.

## A Data-Grounded Problem Statement

Before mapping, write a problem statement that names the gap between current and desired performance without assuming a cause or solution: "Over the past 90 days, the average time from job completion to invoice sent was 9.4 business days; our target is 1 business day. Late invoices delay cash collection and generate customer confusion." Avoid statements like "The office is too slow," which assign blame, or "We need new software," which jumps to a solution.

:::example Illustrative: Scoping a Bakery's Catering Orders
A bakery receives catering orders by phone, email, and website form. Complaints include wrong items and late deliveries. The owner scopes the system from "customer places catering order" to "order delivered and paid." The SIPOC lists customers, the website form, and the ingredient supplier as suppliers; order details, ingredients, and delivery addresses as inputs; six steps from order intake to payment; and two customer groups (offices and families). The problem statement: "In the last 60 days, 11 of 74 catering orders (15 percent) had an error or late delivery; target is under 3 percent."
:::

# Chapter 3: Mapping the Current State

:::objectives
- Observe work directly rather than relying on descriptions.
- Draw a current-state map with steps, handoffs, and wait times.
- Calculate lead time, process time, and the ratio between them.
- Identify waste in the map.
:::

## Go and See

Lean practitioners stress direct observation. The Lean Enterprise Institute describes value stream mapping as beginning with a current-state map that captures the actual condition of the value stream's material and information flow, followed by a future-state map of how it should flow ([Lean Enterprise Institute](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/value-stream-mapping/)). The word "actual" matters. Procedures in a manual, or in the owner's memory, often differ from what really happens. Walk the process, sit with the people who do the work, and follow a real order from start to finish.

## Building the Map

1. **List the steps** in sequence from trigger to outcome, using sticky notes or a whiteboard.
2. **Name who performs each step** and with what tool.
3. **Mark handoffs**, where work passes between people, departments, or systems.
4. **Record process time**, the time someone actively works on the step.
5. **Record wait time**, the time work sits between steps.
6. **Note information flows**: how does each step learn what to do?
7. **Mark rework loops**, where work goes back to an earlier step.
8. **Add data**: volumes, error rates, and how often each loop occurs.

## Lead Time and Process Time

**Lead time** is the total elapsed time from trigger to outcome. **Process time** is the sum of time actively spent working. In many service processes, process time is a small fraction of lead time; the rest is waiting. This ratio, sometimes called **flow efficiency**, often reveals that improving wait times matters more than making individual steps faster.

| Step | Owner | Process time | Wait before next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Call answered, details captured | Dispatcher | 6 min | 4 hours |
| Job scheduled | Dispatcher | 5 min | 2 days |
| Repair performed | Technician | 90 min | 3 days (paper ticket to office) |
| Invoice created | Office manager | 10 min | 4 days (monthly batch) |
| Payment received | Customer | 2 min | 14 days |
| Totals | | about 1.9 hours | about 23 days |

In this illustrative map, about 2 hours of work take about 23 days of elapsed time.

## Seeing Waste

Lean thinking classifies non-value-adding activity as waste. Common forms in service businesses include waiting, rework from errors, unnecessary motion (searching for tools or files), overprocessing (duplicate data entry), excess inventory (stock or work in progress piling up), transportation of materials or files, and underused skills. Mark each on the map.

:::case Virginia Mason's Nursing Cells
Virginia Mason Medical Center in Seattle adopted Toyota Production System methods in 2002 under the name Virginia Mason Production System. CEO Gary Kaplan wrote for the National Academy of Medicine that nursing teams used improvement workshops in 2005 to redesign work into "cells" of nearby rooms; steps walked per day fell from about 10,000 to roughly 1,200, and nurse time with patients rose from about 35 percent to more than 90 percent ([National Academy of Medicine](https://nam.edu/perspectives/the-lean-approach-to-health-care-safety-quality-and-cost/)). The redesign came from mapping where time actually went. Virginia Mason's results are its own, not TTI's.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Mapping the Bakery's Catering Orders
Mapping reveals that orders arrive through three channels, each recorded differently. The owner retypes email orders into a spreadsheet (overprocessing). Phone orders are written on paper and sometimes lost (rework). Delivery addresses are confirmed only on the delivery day (waiting and rework). Process time per order is about 45 minutes; lead time from order to confirmed production plan averages 1.5 days.
:::

:::checklist Current-State Map
- I observed the process directly and followed at least one real order.
- Every step, owner, and tool is on the map.
- Handoffs and information flows are marked.
- Process time and wait time are recorded for each step.
- Rework loops and their frequency are shown.
- Lead time, process time, and flow efficiency are calculated.
- Waste is labeled by type.
:::

:::summary
Map the current state by observing actual work, listing steps, owners, handoffs, information flows, process times, wait times, and rework loops. Lead time often vastly exceeds process time, revealing waiting as the main opportunity. Label waste by type. Virginia Mason's nursing redesign illustrates how mapping where time goes can uncover large improvements.
:::

# Chapter 4: Events, Patterns, and Structures

:::objectives
- Use the iceberg model to look beneath events.
- Track patterns over time with run charts.
- Identify the structures that produce recurring patterns.
- Recognize mental models that sustain structures.
:::

## The Iceberg Model

Systems practitioners often describe four levels of understanding, visualized as an iceberg:

| Level | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Events | What just happened? | A customer complained about a missed appointment |
| Patterns | What has been happening over time? | Missed appointments spike on Mondays |
| Structures | What causes the pattern? | Weekend calls are logged on paper and entered Monday morning; the schedule is built before the entries |
| Mental models | What beliefs keep the structure in place? | "Weekend calls are rare, so we don't need a process for them" |

Most management attention goes to events, the visible tip. Lasting change happens at the levels of structure and mental model, which Meadows ranked among the higher-leverage places to intervene: the rules of the system, its goals, and the mindset out of which it arises ([Donella Meadows Project](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/)).

## Seeing Patterns With Run Charts

A **run chart** plots a measure over time, in order. It is the simplest tool for moving from events to patterns. Plot weekly counts of missed appointments, late deliveries, or errors for at least 12 to 20 points. Look for trends (sustained rises or falls), shifts (a run of points above or below the median), and cycles (regular repetition, such as Monday spikes). IHI's improvement guidance relies on data over time to judge whether a change is an improvement ([IHI](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement)).

## Finding the Structure

Structures include:

- **Physical layout** and tools: where things are, what software does or does not allow.
- **Process design**: sequence of steps, handoffs, batching.
- **Policies and rules**: approval requirements, pricing rules, schedules.
- **Incentives**: what people are rewarded or penalized for.
- **Information flows**: who knows what, and when.

The "Five Whys," a technique associated with Toyota, asks "why?" repeatedly until a structural cause appears. Use it with data, not guesses, and expect several branches rather than a single root cause.

## Mental Models

Mental models are the assumptions people hold about how things work. They are hard to see because they feel like facts. Surface them by asking: "What would have to be true for this structure to make sense?" Then test the assumption with data. In the Monday example, the belief that weekend calls are rare may have been true years ago and is no longer.

:::example Illustrative: Iceberg Analysis for a Dental Office
Event: A patient waited 40 minutes past her appointment.
Pattern: A run chart of average wait times shows waits exceed 20 minutes most afternoons after 2 p.m.
Structure: The schedule books new-patient exams (which often run long) in the early afternoon; hygienists' lunch breaks are staggered so one chair sits empty from 1 to 2 p.m.; there is no buffer slot.
Mental model: "Booking back-to-back maximizes revenue."
Intervention: Test moving new-patient exams to the start of morning and afternoon blocks and adding one 20-minute buffer slot at 3 p.m. for two weeks; track waits on the run chart.
:::

:::worksheet My Iceberg
Recent event: ___
Measure to plot over time: ___
Pattern observed (trend, shift, cycle): ___
Structures that could produce the pattern: ___
Five Whys chain: ___
Mental models behind the structure: ___

:::

:::summary
The iceberg model moves attention from events to patterns, structures, and mental models. Run charts reveal patterns over time. Structures include layout, process design, policies, incentives, and information flows. Mental models sustain structures and should be surfaced and tested. Meadows ranked rules, goals, and mindsets among the highest-leverage intervention points.
:::

# Chapter 5: Feedback Loops and Delays

:::objectives
- Distinguish reinforcing from balancing feedback loops.
- Draw a simple causal loop diagram.
- Explain how delays cause overshoot and oscillation.
- Apply lessons from the Beer Distribution Game.
:::

## Feedback Loops

A **feedback loop** exists when a change in one element eventually affects itself through a chain of connections. Meadows distinguished negative (balancing, self-correcting) loops from positive (reinforcing, self-amplifying) loops ([Donella Meadows Project](https://www.donellameadows.org/wp-content/userfiles/Leverage_Points.pdf)).

| Loop type | Behavior | Business example |
|---|---|---|
| Reinforcing | Amplifies change: growth or decline accelerates | Good service leads to referrals, which lead to more customers, more revenue, more investment in service |
| Reinforcing (vicious) | Decline accelerates | Overloaded staff make errors, causing rework, which increases overload |
| Balancing | Pushes toward a target | When backlog rises, the owner adds overtime until backlog falls |

## Causal Loop Diagrams

A **causal loop diagram** uses arrows to show how variables influence one another. Label each arrow "same" (both move in the same direction) or "opposite" (they move in opposite directions). A loop with an even number of "opposite" links is reinforcing; an odd number makes it balancing. Keep diagrams to five to eight variables; their purpose is shared understanding, not precision.

Example reinforcing loop in words: more workload leads to more overtime (same); more overtime leads to more fatigue (same); more fatigue leads to more errors (same); more errors lead to more rework (same); more rework leads to more workload (same). Every link moves in the same direction, so the loop reinforces.

## Delays

A **delay** is a gap between an action and its effect. Delays are everywhere: the weeks between running an ad and receiving calls, the months between hiring and full productivity, the days between ordering stock and receiving it. Meadows listed the lengths of delays relative to the rate of system change as a leverage point ([Donella Meadows Project](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/)). When people act on a balancing loop without accounting for delay, they overcorrect: ordering too much because the earlier order has not arrived yet, or hiring too many people because the last hires are not yet productive.

:::case The Beer Distribution Game
The Beer Distribution Game, developed at MIT's Sloan School in the 1960s, asks teams to play retailer, wholesaler, distributor, and factory in a supply chain, with each player seeing only orders from the next player downstream ([MIT Sloan Management Review](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-bullwhip-effect-in-supply-chains/)). Professor John Sterman's research found that players systematically underweight the "supply line" of orders already placed but not yet received, producing amplifying swings in orders known as the bullwhip effect. In experiments where customer demand was held constant and known to all participants, the bullwhip effect and oscillations still persisted ([Croson, Donohue, Katok, and Sterman](https://www.mit.edu/~jsterman/Order_Stability_070505.pdf)). Sterman's findings come from controlled experiments; they illustrate a pattern, not a prediction about any specific business.
:::

## Practical Rules for Delays

- **Account for what is already in the pipeline** before acting again (orders placed, hires in training, campaigns running).
- **Make smaller, more frequent adjustments** rather than large, infrequent ones.
- **Shorten delays** where possible: faster information, smaller batches, quicker feedback.
- **Share information** across the chain so each actor sees real demand, not only the next person's orders.

:::example Illustrative: Overhiring After a Busy Season
A cleaning company's backlog grows in March. The owner hires three cleaners. Because training takes six weeks, the backlog keeps growing, so she hires three more in April. By June, all six are productive just as demand softens, leaving too many cleaners and too little work. A causal loop diagram showing the hiring delay, and a rule to wait for trainees' impact before hiring again, would have dampened the swing.
:::

:::checklist Loops and Delays
- I have drawn one causal loop diagram for a recurring problem.
- I can identify whether each loop is reinforcing or balancing.
- I have listed the key delays in my system and their typical lengths.
- I account for work already in the pipeline before acting again.
- I prefer small, frequent adjustments over large, rare ones.
:::

:::summary
Reinforcing loops amplify change and balancing loops seek targets. Causal loop diagrams make loops visible. Delays between action and effect cause overcorrection and oscillation, as Sterman's Beer Distribution Game research shows even when demand is constant. Account for the pipeline, shorten delays, adjust in small steps, and share information.
:::

# Chapter 6: Finding and Managing the Bottleneck

:::objectives
- Define a constraint and explain why it limits the whole system.
- Locate the constraint using queues and data.
- Apply Goldratt's Five Focusing Steps.
- Avoid common mistakes when relieving constraints.
:::

## The Constraint

A **constraint** (or bottleneck) is the part of a system that limits its overall output. Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints holds that improving anything other than the constraint does not increase the system's throughput, because the whole system can move only as fast as its slowest necessary step ([Goldratt Research Labs](https://www.goldrattresearchlabs.com/introduction-to-toc)).

## Finding the Constraint

- **Look for queues.** Work piles up in front of the constraint and the steps after it wait.
- **Check utilization.** The constraint is busy all the time; others have slack.
- **Ask the team.** People usually know where work gets stuck.
- **Look beyond equipment.** Constraints can be a person (often the owner), a skill, a policy, supplier capacity, or market demand.

In many small businesses, the owner is the constraint: every quote, approval, or difficult decision waits for one person.

## The Five Focusing Steps

| Step | Action | Small-business example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify | Find what limits the system | Owner prepares all quotes; 15 requests waiting |
| 2. Exploit | Get the most from the constraint without new investment | Owner blocks two uninterrupted quoting hours daily; office gathers measurements and photos first |
| 3. Subordinate | Align everything else to support the constraint | Sales calls scheduled so complete information reaches the owner; no quotes without photos |
| 4. Elevate | Add capacity if still needed | Train a senior tech to quote standard jobs using a price guide |
| 5. Repeat | Find the new constraint; avoid inertia | Now scheduling is the constraint |

These steps are summarized by [Goldratt Research Labs](https://www.goldrattresearchlabs.com/introduction-to-toc). The order matters: exploiting and subordinating are usually cheaper than elevating, and owners often skip directly to hiring or buying equipment.

## Protecting the Constraint

Because time lost at the constraint is lost for the whole system, protect it:

- Keep a small buffer of ready work in front of it so it never waits.
- Inspect quality before work reaches it so it never processes defective inputs.
- Remove non-essential tasks from it.
- Release new work into the system only as fast as the constraint can handle; extra work elsewhere just becomes inventory.

## Common Mistakes

- Improving a non-constraint and expecting overall results to rise.
- Running every step at maximum, which builds work in progress.
- Elevating before exploiting, spending money while free capacity is wasted.
- Failing to notice when the constraint moves.

:::example Illustrative: A Print Shop's Bottleneck
A print shop's lead time has grown to eight days. A walk-through shows proofs waiting for customer approval: 22 jobs sit in "awaiting approval," while the presses are idle half the day. The constraint is not the press but the approval step. Exploit: send proofs the same day with a one-click approve link. Subordinate: designers prepare proofs before 2 p.m. so they go out the same day. After four weeks, average lead time falls to four days in this illustration, and the new constraint becomes the finishing station.
:::

:::worksheet My Constraint
Where does work pile up? ___
Which step or person is always busy? ___
Constraint identified: ___
How I will exploit it (no new spending): ___
What other steps must change to support it: ___
If still needed, how I will elevate it: ___
Measure to confirm throughput change: ___
:::

:::summary
A constraint limits the output of the whole system, so improvements elsewhere do not increase throughput. Find it by looking for queues and full utilization, including constraints that are people or policies. Apply Goldratt's Five Focusing Steps in order: identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, and repeat. Protect the constraint and watch for it to move.
:::

# Chapter 7: Leverage Points and Where to Intervene

:::objectives
- Explain Meadows's concept of leverage points.
- Rank possible interventions from shallow to deep.
- Choose interventions that balance leverage and feasibility.
- Anticipate unintended consequences.
:::

## What Leverage Means

Meadows described leverage points as places within a complex system where "a small shift in one thing can produce big changes in everything" ([Donella Meadows Project](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/)). She also warned that people often know intuitively where leverage points are but push them in the wrong direction, and she presented her list as "a work in progress," not a recipe.

## Meadows's Hierarchy, Applied to Business

Meadows listed twelve places to intervene in increasing order of effectiveness, from constants and parameters at the bottom to goals, paradigms, and the power to transcend paradigms at the top ([Donella Meadows Project](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/)). The table translates a selection into small-business terms.

| Leverage point (Meadows) | Business translation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Constants, parameters, numbers | Prices, quotas, targets, wages | Raise the minimum job fee |
| Buffers and stocks relative to flows | Cash reserves, inventory, schedule slack | Hold two weeks of key parts |
| Lengths of delays | Turnaround times, approval cycles | Same-day proofs |
| Strength of balancing loops | Monitoring and correction | Daily callback review |
| Gain around reinforcing loops | Growth engines | Referral program |
| Structure of information flows | Who knows what, when | Shared job board visible to all |
| Rules of the system | Policies, incentives | Pay techs for first-time-fix rate, not jobs closed |
| Power to self-organize | Team authority to change processes | Staff can run tests without owner approval |
| Goals of the system | What the business optimizes for | Lifetime customer value rather than monthly volume |
| Mindset or paradigm | Core beliefs | "Problems are system signals, not personal failures" |

## Balancing Leverage and Feasibility

Deep leverage points (rules, goals, mindsets) produce lasting change but take longer and require trust. Shallow points (numbers, buffers) are fast but often temporary. A practical approach combines both: make a quick, low-risk change to relieve pain while working on a deeper structural change. Information flow is often a sweet spot: Meadows observed that adding or restoring information can be a powerful intervention, often easier and cheaper than rebuilding physical infrastructure ([Donella Meadows Project](https://www.donellameadows.org/wp-content/userfiles/Leverage_Points.pdf)).

## Unintended Consequences

Every intervention ripples through the system. Rules and incentives, in particular, can produce gaming: paying technicians per job may increase jobs closed while lowering quality. Before acting, ask:

- Who else is affected by this change?
- What behavior does this reward, and how could it be gamed?
- What might get worse while this gets better?
- What will we measure to catch side effects early?

The last question links to IHI's concept of **balancing measures**, discussed in Chapter 8.

:::example Illustrative: Choosing Interventions for Late Arrivals
Problem: Technicians arrive late to 18 percent of appointments.
Shallow option: Add 15 minutes of buffer between jobs (parameter). Fast, but reduces capacity.
Information flow option: Show live traffic and job status on the dispatcher's screen and send customers automatic "on the way" texts. Moderate cost; improves both coordination and customer experience.
Rule option: Stop scheduling jobs across town back to back; assign technicians by zone. Deeper change; requires rethinking dispatch.
Plan: Test the information flow change first for two weeks, while designing a zone-based schedule to test next.
:::

:::checklist Choosing an Intervention
- I listed at least three intervention options at different leverage levels.
- I considered information flow changes.
- I asked how each option could be gamed or cause side effects.
- I chose one quick change and one deeper change.
- I defined measures to detect unintended consequences.
:::

:::summary
Leverage points are places where small shifts can produce large changes. Meadows ranked them from parameters (shallow) to goals and paradigms (deep). Combine quick, low-risk changes with deeper structural ones, favor information-flow improvements, and anticipate unintended consequences, especially from rules and incentives.
:::

# Chapter 8: Aims and Measures

:::objectives
- Apply the three questions of the Model for Improvement.
- Write a specific, time-bound aim statement.
- Build a family of outcome, process, and balancing measures.
- Collect simple data over time.
:::

## The Model for Improvement

The Institute for Healthcare Improvement uses the Model for Improvement, developed by Associates in Process Improvement, as its framework. It has two parts: three fundamental questions and the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle for testing changes ([IHI](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement)). The questions are:

1. What are we trying to accomplish?
2. How will we know that a change is an improvement?
3. What change can we make that will result in improvement?

IHI notes the questions can be addressed in any order, though teams usually begin with the aim, and that answering them is iterative ([IHI](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement)). Although developed and popularized in healthcare, the model applies to any process.

## Writing an Aim Statement

IHI describes a good aim as time-bound and measurable (how much, by when), specific about the population affected, and clear about where the improvement takes place ([IHI](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement)).

| Weak aim | Strong aim |
|---|---|
| Improve invoicing | By June 30, send 90 percent of residential invoices within one business day of job completion |
| Better customer service | By the end of Q3, reduce the share of catering orders with errors or late delivery from 15 percent to under 3 percent |
| Reduce wait times | Within 8 weeks, reduce average afternoon patient wait from 22 minutes to under 10 minutes |

## A Family of Measures

IHI's improvement training teaches three kinds of measures: outcome, process, and balancing ([IHI Open School](https://www.ihi.org/learn/courses/open-school/catalog/qi-102)). AHRQ guidance describes outcome measures as the "voice of the customer," process measures as capturing changes to the steps that influence outcomes, and balancing measures as determining whether changes in one part of the system cause new problems elsewhere ([AHRQ](https://www.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/wysiwyg/evidencenow/tools-and-materials/q80-ramping%20up%20PDSAs%20revised.pdf)).

| Measure type | Purpose | Invoicing example |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | Did the result improve? | Percent of invoices sent within one business day; days sales outstanding |
| Process | Are the changed steps happening? | Percent of jobs with digital job sheets submitted same day |
| Balancing | Did something else get worse? | Invoice error rate; technician time spent on paperwork |

## Collecting Data Simply

- Prefer small, frequent samples over large, rare ones.
- Use data you already have (software reports, call logs) before creating new tracking.
- Define each measure precisely: what counts, how it is calculated, who collects it, and how often.
- Plot measures on run charts weekly.
- Keep it light; a tally sheet on a clipboard can be enough.

:::example Illustrative: Aims and Measures for the Dental Office
Aim: Within eight weeks, reduce average afternoon wait time from 22 minutes to under 10 minutes for scheduled patients at the Main Street office.
Outcome measure: Average wait from check-in to seating, 1 to 5 p.m., recorded by front desk.
Process measures: Percent of new-patient exams booked at block starts; buffer slot available daily.
Balancing measures: Daily production in dollars; hygienist overtime minutes; patient satisfaction comments.
Data: Front desk records check-in and seating times on the schedule; office manager plots weekly averages each Friday.
:::

:::summary
IHI's Model for Improvement asks what we are trying to accomplish, how we will know a change is an improvement, and what change will result in improvement, followed by PDSA testing. Strong aims are specific, measurable, and time-bound. A family of outcome, process, and balancing measures shows whether results improve, changes are happening, and side effects appear. # Chapter 9: Small, Safe Tests With PDSA

:::objectives
- Explain the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle.
- Design a small-scale test with a prediction.
- Scale tests gradually across conditions.
- Decide whether to adopt, adapt, or abandon a change.
:::

## Why Test Small

Large changes rolled out all at once are risky: if they fail, they fail everywhere, and it is hard to know why. IHI describes the PDSA cycle as testing a change on a small scale (for example, one patient or one day) in the real-world setting by planning it, trying it, observing the results, and acting on what is learned, calling it the scientific method adapted for action-oriented learning ([IHI](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement)). Small tests are cheap, fast, and reversible.

## The Four Phases

AHRQ describes the phases this way ([AHRQ](https://www.ahrq.gov/cahps/improvement-guide/improvement-models/plan-do-study-act.html)):

| Phase | What happens | Key questions |
|---|---|---|
| Plan | Define the change, prediction, and data to collect | What will we try? What do we predict? Who, where, when? |
| Do | Carry out the test on a small scale; record observations | What happened? What surprised us? |
| Study | Compare results to the prediction | Did it work as predicted? Why or why not? |
| Act | Decide next step | Adopt, adapt, or abandon? What is the next test? |

## The Importance of a Prediction

Writing down a prediction before testing turns a trial into learning. If the prediction is wrong, the gap reveals something about how the system really works. "We predict that sending proofs with a one-click approval link will cut average approval time from 2 days to under 1 day for the 10 jobs tested this week."

## Scaling Up

IHI recommends that after testing on a small scale and refining through multiple cycles, teams test under varying conditions, such as morning versus night or holiday versus non-holiday, before implementing ([IHI](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement)). A practical progression for a small business:

1. One person, one day.
2. One person, one week.
3. Two or three people, one week, including someone skeptical.
4. Busy and slow days.
5. Whole team, with standard work written (Chapter 10).

## Adopt, Adapt, or Abandon

- **Adopt** when the change worked as predicted under varied conditions without harming balancing measures.
- **Adapt** when it partly worked; modify and test again.
- **Abandon** when it did not work or caused harm; record what you learned.

Abandoning is not failure. A test that disproves a hypothesis cheaply saves the cost of a full rollout.

:::example Illustrative: PDSA Ramp for Digital Job Sheets
Cycle 1 (Plan): One crew lead photographs job sheets in the app at each job's end for one day. Prediction: all sheets reach the office same day; materials field complete. (Do): 5 of 5 sheets arrived; 1 missing materials. (Study): The materials field was optional in the app. (Act): Make materials field required.
Cycle 2: Same crew lead, full week. 24 of 24 sheets same day, all complete. Adopt for this crew.
Cycle 3: Add two more crews, including the most skeptical lead. 61 of 66 same day; the skeptical crew forgot on two days. Adapt: add a checklist item to the end-of-job routine and a 4 p.m. reminder.
Cycle 4: All crews, two weeks, including a rainy week with rescheduling. 96 percent same day; invoice lead time falls from 9.4 days to 1.6 days. Balancing measure (crew time on paperwork) unchanged. Adopt and write standard work.
:::

:::worksheet PDSA Record
Cycle number: ___ Dates: ___
Change being tested: ___
Prediction: ___
Who, where, when, how many: ___
Data to collect: ___
What happened (Do): ___
Comparison to prediction (Study): ___

:::

:::summary
PDSA tests changes on a small scale in real conditions through Plan, Do, Study, and Act. Writing a prediction turns trials into learning. Scale tests gradually across people and conditions, and decide whether to adopt, adapt, or abandon based on outcome and balancing measures. Cheap tests that disprove ideas save the cost of failed rollouts.
:::

# Chapter 10: Standard Work and Building Quality In

:::objectives
- Explain the purpose of standard work.
- Write a simple standard work document.
- Apply jidoka and andon principles to a small business.
- Design mistake-proofing for common errors.
:::

## Why Standardize

Once a change has been tested and adopted, it must become the normal way of working, or it will erode. **Standard work** is the current best known method for doing a task, written down and followed by everyone who does that task. It is not a rigid rule; it is the baseline from which the next improvement begins. Without a standard, there is nothing to improve against.

## Writing Standard Work

A standard work document should be short, visual, and located where the work happens. It includes:

1. **Task name and purpose.**
2. **Sequence of steps,** each with key points (what makes the step succeed) and reasons.
3. **Time** each step typically takes.
4. **Quality checks:** how to know the step was done correctly.
5. **What to do when something is abnormal.**
6. **Owner and revision date.**

| Step | Key point | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Photograph job sheet in app | All fields visible; materials listed | Office can invoice without calling |
| Confirm customer signature | Signature box filled | Prevents disputes |
| Tap "Submit" before leaving site | Green checkmark appears | Confirms upload |

## Jidoka and Andon

Toyota describes **jidoka**, one of the two pillars of the Toyota Production System, as "automation with a human touch," based on stopping immediately when abnormalities are detected to prevent defective products ([Toyota](https://global.toyota/en/company/vision-and-philosophy/production-system/)). Operators can stop the line by pulling a stop cord, and an **andon** board lights up to notify others of the abnormality ([Toyota](https://global.toyota/en/company/vision-and-philosophy/production-system/)). Toyota UK explains that if the problem can be resolved within the station's allotted time, production continues; if not, the line pauses while the issue is investigated, preventing a defect from moving down the line ([Toyota UK](https://mag.toyota.co.uk/toyota-manufacturing-25-objects-andon-cord/)).

The principle for a small business is: make it normal and easy to raise a problem the moment it appears, and respond quickly. A technician who discovers a wrong part should be able to signal the office immediately, not discover at day's end that the job must be rescheduled.

:::case Virginia Mason's Patient Safety Alert System
After executives visited Toyota in 2002, Virginia Mason modeled a patient safety alert system on Toyota's andon, enabling any employee to alert managers or colleagues to quality or process defects. According to the Virginia Mason Institute, the organization logged its 50,000th Patient Safety Alert in September 2014; staff participation in its patient safety culture survey grew from 16 percent in 2004 to 88 percent in 2013; and professional liability claims fell 74 percent from May 2005 to May 2015 ([Virginia Mason Institute](https://www.virginiamasoninstitute.org/case-studies/embedding-a-system-to-protect-patient-safety)). These results reflect a large health system's sustained, multi-year effort; they are Virginia Mason's own, not TTI's.
:::

## Mistake-Proofing

**Mistake-proofing** (poka-yoke in Toyota's vocabulary) designs a process so that errors are impossible or immediately obvious. Examples in small businesses:

- Required fields in forms so incomplete orders cannot be submitted.
- Pre-filled templates and dropdown menus instead of free text.
- Color-coded bins for parts by job.
- Checklists at handoffs, signed or checked digitally.
- Confirmation texts that repeat appointment details back to customers.
- Software rules that block double-booking.

Prefer prevention (the error cannot occur) over detection (the error is caught quickly) over correction (the error is fixed after it reaches the customer).

:::example Illustrative: Andon for a Restaurant Kitchen
A restaurant frequently sends dishes missing components during dinner rush. The owner introduces a simple signal: any cook who runs out of a component flips a red card at their station, and the expediter responds within one minute. Plates are checked against a photo card for each dish before leaving the pass. The owner tracks red-card flips per night; the most frequent shortages lead to changes in prep quantities. Over several weeks of testing, returned dishes decline in this illustration, and prep lists are updated as standard work.
:::

:::checklist Standard Work and Quality
- Each adopted change has written standard work at the point of use.
- Standards include key points, reasons, checks, and abnormality responses.
- Anyone can raise a problem immediately, and someone responds quickly.
- Problems raised are logged and reviewed for patterns.
- Common errors have mistake-proofing, preferably prevention.
- Standards have owners and revision dates.
:::

:::summary
Standard work captures the current best method so improvements stick and future improvements have a baseline. Toyota's jidoka and andon make abnormalities visible and stop defects from moving forward; Virginia Mason adapted the idea into a patient safety alert system. Mistake-proofing makes errors impossible or obvious. Prevention beats detection, and detection beats correction.
:::

# Chapter 11: Visual Management, Review, and Rollback

:::objectives
- Make system performance visible to the whole team.
- Run daily, weekly, and monthly review routines.
- Plan and execute rollbacks when changes fail.
- Keep a learning log.
:::

## Visual Management

When performance is visible, problems are noticed sooner. A **visual board** (physical or digital) shows current work, key measures, and problems. Effective boards answer at a glance: Are we on track today? What is stuck? Who needs help? Examples include a job board with columns for "requested, scheduled, in progress, waiting on parts, complete, invoiced"; a run chart of the main outcome measure posted weekly; and a problem log showing issues raised and their status.

## Review Rhythms

| Routine | Frequency | Length | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily huddle | Daily | 10 minutes | Today's work, yesterday's problems, help needed |
| Weekly system review | Weekly | 30 to 45 minutes | Measures on run charts, PDSA results, constraint status |
| Monthly improvement review | Monthly | 60 minutes | Progress toward aims, standard work updates, next priorities |
| Quarterly system reset | Quarterly | Half day | Remap a value stream, reset aims, review leverage points |

Keep meetings short and structured. The daily huddle is about action; the weekly review is about learning; the monthly review is about direction.

## Rollback Planning

Some changes will not work, or will work in tests but fail at scale. A **rollback plan** describes how to return to the previous state quickly and safely. Before scaling any change, write:

1. **Trigger:** what measure or event will cause a rollback (for example, invoice errors above 5 percent for two consecutive days).
2. **Steps:** exactly how to revert (restore old form, re-enable old process).
3. **Owner:** who decides and executes.
4. **Communication:** who must be told.
5. **Learning:** how the failure will be studied.

Rollback planning reduces fear of change. People are more willing to try something new when they know it can be undone.

## The Learning Log

Keep a simple log of every test: date, change, prediction, result, decision, and lesson. Over time it becomes institutional memory, preventing the business from repeating failed experiments and helping new team members understand why things are done as they are.

:::example Illustrative: Weekly System Review for a Property Management Office
Agenda (35 minutes):

1. Outcome measure run chart: maintenance requests closed within 72 hours (5 minutes).
2. Process measures: requests triaged within 4 hours; vendor confirmations same day (5 minutes).
3. Balancing measure: vendor cost per request (3 minutes).
4. Current PDSA: testing photo upload requirement on tenant portal; results from 31 requests (10 minutes).
5. Constraint check: vendor scheduling remains the constraint; discuss adding a second plumbing vendor (7 minutes).
6. Decisions and next tests (5 minutes).
:::

# Chapter 12: Leading a Systems-Minded Business

:::objectives
- Describe leadership behaviors that support systems thinking.
- Respond to problems in ways that encourage reporting.
- Build improvement into everyone's role.
- Create a 90-day systems improvement roadmap.
:::

## Leadership Behaviors

Systems thinking survives only if leaders model it. Key behaviors include:

- **Asking "what" and "how" before "who."** "What happened, and how did our process allow it?" invites learning; "Who did this?" invites hiding.
- **Going to see.** Observing work directly rather than relying on reports.
- **Thanking people for raising problems.** Virginia Mason's alert system succeeded partly because reporting became expected and supported ([Virginia Mason Institute](https://www.virginiamasoninstitute.org/case-studies/embedding-a-system-to-protect-patient-safety)).
- **Being patient with tests and impatient with inaction.** Small, frequent experiments beat large, rare projects.
- **Holding to standards.** Systems thinking does not excuse carelessness; it creates clear standards and then improves them.

## Accountability in a Systems View

Deming's emphasis on the system does not remove individual accountability; rather, it locates accountability correctly. Individuals are accountable for following standard work, raising problems, and participating in improvement. Leaders are accountable for the design of the system, which Deming identified as the responsibility of management ([Deming Institute](https://deming.org/appreciation-for-a-system/)). When a person deviates from a clear, workable standard, address it directly. When many people struggle with the same step, fix the step.

## Improvement as Part of Every Role

Build improvement into job descriptions and routines:

- Each role owns at least one measure and the standard work for its tasks.
- Team members can propose and run small tests within defined limits.
- Weekly reviews include time for frontline ideas.
- New hires learn the improvement method during onboarding.

## A 90-Day Roadmap

| Weeks | Focus | Deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | Choose and define | SIPOC, problem statement, aim |
| 3 to 4 | Map and measure | Current-state map, baseline run charts, family of measures |
| 5 to 6 | Diagnose | Iceberg analysis, causal loop diagram, constraint identified |
| 7 to 10 | Test | Three to six PDSA cycles, scaling across conditions |
| 11 to 12 | Standardize | Standard work, mistake-proofing, rollback plan, visual board |
| 13 | Review and choose next | Results summary, learning log, next value stream selected |

:::example Illustrative: A Contractor's First 90 Days
Weeks 1 to 2: A remodeling contractor chooses "estimate request to signed contract." Baseline: 14 days average; 30 percent of estimates lost to slow response.
Weeks 3 to 6: Mapping shows estimates wait for the owner, who is the constraint; site photos and measurements often missing.
Weeks 7 to 10: PDSA cycles test a pre-visit questionnaire, a measurement checklist for the project manager, and two protected estimating blocks per week.
Weeks 11 to 12: Standard work written; estimating template with required fields; rollback trigger set.
Week 13: In this illustration, average time to estimate falls to six days, and the next value stream (change orders) is chosen.
:::

# Appendix E: Common System Patterns in Small Businesses

System dynamics practitioners, including Peter Senge in The Fifth Discipline (cited in [MIT Sloan Management Review](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/the-bullwhip-effect-in-supply-chains/)), describe recurring structures often called system archetypes. Recognizing them speeds diagnosis. The descriptions below are adapted for small businesses.

| Pattern | How it looks | Underlying structure | Typical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixes that fail | A quick fix relieves a symptom, but the problem returns worse | The fix has a delayed side effect that feeds the original problem | Look for the delayed side effect; address the cause |
| Shifting the burden | The business relies on the owner, overtime, or discounts to cope | A symptomatic solution weakens the capacity for a fundamental solution | Invest in the fundamental solution (training, process redesign) while tapering the crutch |
| Limits to growth | Growth stalls despite more effort | A reinforcing growth loop hits a balancing limit (capacity, cash, owner time) | Find and relieve the limit before pushing harder on growth |
| Eroding goals | Standards slip gradually ("two days late is normal now") | The gap between goal and performance is closed by lowering the goal | Hold the standard; fix the process |
| Success to the successful | One product, location, or person gets all resources | Early success attracts resources, starving alternatives | Make allocation rules explicit; review periodically |
| Escalation | Two parties keep raising stakes (price war, internal conflict) | Each party's actions threaten the other, provoking response | Find a shared goal; change the measure of success |

## Applying the Patterns

When a problem recurs, compare your causal loop diagram to these patterns. If the owner keeps stepping in to rescue late jobs, the business may be shifting the burden: each rescue relieves the symptom while reducing pressure to train staff or fix scheduling. If a marketing push brings more leads but revenue does not rise, look for a limit to growth, such as estimating capacity or crew availability. If a monthly target has quietly fallen from 95 percent to 80 percent, goals may be eroding.

:::example Illustrative: Fixes That Fail in a Retail Shop
A boutique responds to slow weeks with storewide discounts. Sales rise briefly, but customers learn to wait for discounts, full-price sales fall, and margins shrink, making the next slow week worse. The quick fix had a delayed side effect. The owner tests alternatives over several months: a loyalty program that rewards repeat full-price purchases, targeted promotions on specific overstocked items only, and an email calendar that features new arrivals. Each is run as a PDSA cycle with gross margin as a balancing measure.
:::

# Glossary

**Andon:** A signal that makes an abnormality visible and calls for help, from the Toyota Production System.

**Balancing loop:** A feedback loop that pushes a system toward a target.

**Balancing measure:** A measure that detects whether improving one part of a system harms another.

**Bottleneck (constraint):** The part of a system that limits its overall output.

**Causal loop diagram:** A diagram of variables and their influences, showing feedback loops.

**Common-cause variation:** Routine variation built into a stable process.

**Current-state map:** A diagram of how work actually flows today.

**Delay:** A gap in time between an action and its effect.

**Five Focusing Steps:** Goldratt's cycle of identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, and repeat.

**Flow efficiency:** Process time divided by lead time.

**Jidoka:** Toyota's principle of stopping when an abnormality is detected so defects do not pass forward.

**Lead time:** Total elapsed time from trigger to outcome.

**Leverage point:** A place in a system where a small change can produce large effects.

**Mistake-proofing:** Designing a process so errors are impossible or immediately obvious.

**PDSA:** Plan-Do-Study-Act, a cycle for testing changes on a small scale.

**Reinforcing loop:** A feedback loop that amplifies change.

**SIPOC:** Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs, Customers.

**Special-cause variation:** Variation from unusual, assignable events.

**Standard work:** The current best known method for a task, documented and followed.

**Value stream:** All actions required to bring a product or service from order to delivery.
