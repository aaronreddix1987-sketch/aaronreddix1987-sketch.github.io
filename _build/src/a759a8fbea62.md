# How to Use This Book

This book is a working text for people who want to start or repair a small digital marketing service business: a solo consultant, a two-person studio, or a freelancer who wants to stop being a freelancer and start running a firm. It is written the way a good university course in small-business management is taught. Each chapter states what you should be able to do when you finish it, explains the underlying ideas in plain language, works through an example with numbers, examines a real company or practitioner whose story is publicly documented, and closes with a summary and review questions. The appendices hold the templates you will actually fill in and send.

The central argument is that most small agencies fail for business reasons, not marketing reasons. Their owners can write good ads, design good pages, and run good social accounts. What they lack is a clear position in the market, a price that covers the true cost of delivery, a repeatable way to win and keep clients, and a delivery system that does not depend on the owner working every evening. Each of those is a learnable discipline, and each has a substantial body of practitioner writing behind it. This book organizes that writing into a sequence you can follow.

## Who this book is for

- Freelancers who already do marketing work for a few clients and want to turn it into a business with prices, processes, and boundaries.
- Career changers with real marketing skills, from an in-house role or a related trade, who want to serve small businesses directly.
- Owners of existing small agencies whose revenue has stalled, whose margins are thin, or whose own time has become the bottleneck.

You do not need a business degree. You do need to be honest with yourself about your skills, your numbers, and your capacity, because every framework in this book depends on accurate inputs.

# Chapter 1: What a Small Agency Actually Sells

:::objectives
- Define a marketing service business in economic terms: what is bought, what is delivered, and what limits growth.
- Compare the four common agency business models: hourly, project, retainer, and productized service.
- Explain why owner time is the binding constraint in most small agencies.
- Describe how two well-known software companies grew out of agency work, and what that teaches about systems.
:::

## The client is not buying marketing

A small business owner who hires an agency is rarely buying "social media posts" or "Google Ads management" for their own sake. They are buying an outcome they cannot or will not produce themselves: more enquiries, a fuller calendar, a more credible public face, or simply relief from a task that keeps falling to the bottom of their list. The deliverable, a post or an ad or a report, is the evidence that work happened. The outcome is the reason they pay.

This distinction matters because it changes how you describe your service, how you price it, and how you report on it. An agency that sells deliverables competes on volume and price: twelve posts for less than the next freelancer. An agency that sells outcomes competes on judgment and reliability: the client trusts it to decide which twelve posts, or whether posts are the right tool at all. The second position is harder to earn, but it is the only one that supports a sustainable price.

At the same time, an honest agency never promises outcomes it does not control. Ad platforms change their algorithms, competitors change their prices, and a client's own sales process determines whether an enquiry becomes revenue. The professional stance, developed throughout this book, is to sell a clear process aimed at an outcome, to measure the leading indicators you do influence, and to report candidly on the results you cannot guarantee.

## The economics of a service firm

Every service business has three numbers that determine whether it survives.

1. **Revenue per client per month.** What a typical client pays, averaged across the year.
2. **Delivery cost per client per month.** The labor (yours or anyone else's), software, and direct expenses needed to serve that client at the promised standard.
3. **Capacity.** How many clients you can serve at that standard with the people and hours you have.

Gross profit per client is revenue minus delivery cost. Total gross profit is that figure multiplied by the number of clients, and it must cover overhead (insurance, accounting, your own sales time, tools that are not tied to any one client) before there is any profit at all. Capacity caps the whole calculation. A solo owner with 30 productive hours a week and clients who each need six hours a week can serve five clients, no matter how strong demand is.

This is why the typical arc of a small agency looks the same. The owner wins a few clients, works more hours, wins a few more, and reaches a ceiling where every additional client lowers quality or raises stress. Dan Martell, whose work appears throughout this book, calls this ceiling the **pain line**: the point at which the founder's personal capacity can no longer fuel the business. Getting past it requires one of three moves: raise revenue per client, lower delivery hours per client through better systems, or add capacity by hiring. All three are covered in later chapters.

## Four business models

| Model | How the client pays | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | For time logged | Easy to start; low risk of under-pricing a vague job | Caps income at hours worked; punishes efficiency; clients scrutinize time |
| Project | Fixed fee for a defined deliverable | Clear scope; rewards efficiency | Revenue resets to zero after each project; scoping errors are costly |
| Retainer | Fixed monthly fee for ongoing work | Predictable revenue; deeper client relationships | Scope can drift; value must be re-proven monthly |
| Productized service | Fixed price for a standardized package, often monthly | Easy to sell and deliver; systems improve with volume | Less flexible; requires a clear niche and discipline to say no |

Most successful small agencies use a combination: a fixed-price project to start (an audit or setup) that leads into a monthly retainer or productized package. The pure hourly model is common among beginners and is the hardest to grow, for reasons Chapter 4 examines in detail.

:::callout Working definition
A **productized service** is a service sold like a product: a named package with a fixed scope, a fixed price, a defined turnaround, and a standard delivery process. The client knows exactly what they get before they buy. The provider knows exactly what to deliver and can document, delegate, and improve each step.
:::

## Why owner time is the real constraint

In the early months the owner is the salesperson, the strategist, the producer, the account manager, and the bookkeeper. Each role competes for the same limited hours. The work that brings in revenue next month, which is selling, is the work most easily postponed by the work due this week, which is delivery. The result is a feast-and-famine cycle: busy delivery months with no selling, followed by empty months when projects end.

Breaking the cycle requires treating the owner's hours as the scarcest resource in the business and deliberately moving routine work off the owner's desk. Chapter 9 covers how to document delivery so that someone else, or a well-supervised tool, can do it. Chapter 11 covers when to hire and in what order.

:::case From agency to product: 37signals and Mailchimp
Two widely known software companies began as small web design agencies, and both grew out of a system the agency built to solve its own delivery problem.

**37signals (Basecamp).** Jason Fried co-founded 37signals in Chicago in 1999 as a web design firm. In a 2009 post marking Basecamp's fifth birthday, Fried wrote that "Basecamp was a side project. We were a web design firm at the time. We built Basecamp because our projects and client communications were a mess." The team started using the tool on client projects, clients asked for it for their own work, and it launched publicly on February 4, 2004. On Basecamp's own "about" page, the company states that about a year after release it was generating more revenue than the web design business, and the firm stopped doing client work.

**Mailchimp.** Ben Chestnut and Dan Kurzius started a web design agency called the Rocket Science Group in Atlanta. Mailchimp's official history says their focus was on larger corporate clients, "but on the side, they created a delightful email marketing service for small businesses," founded in 2001. The email product grew while the agency did not, and the founders eventually closed the agency to focus on it. Intuit announced its acquisition of Mailchimp in September 2021 for approximately $12 billion in cash and stock.

**Lesson for a small agency.** Neither story is a template for building a software company; that outcome is rare. The transferable lesson is narrower and more useful. In both firms, the turning point came when the founders stopped tolerating a messy, repeated delivery problem and built a system for it. The system made the agency better before it became a product. Every small agency has its own version: the way it collects client approvals, builds monthly reports, or onboards a new account. Systematizing that work is the first step toward a business that does not depend on the owner's evenings.

These are the companies' own reported histories; see References.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choosing a Niche and a Core Service

:::objectives
- Explain positioning and why specialization makes selling and delivery easier.
- Score candidate niches using a weighted matrix based on skill, access, ability to pay, and recurring need.
- Write a one-sentence positioning statement that is specific and honest.
- Choose one core service to lead with and identify what you will decline.
:::

## Why specialize

A new agency owner's instinct is to accept any paying work. The instinct is understandable and usually wrong. A business that offers "all marketing for everyone" has no clear message, cannot build reusable systems, and competes on price against every freelancer on every marketplace. A business that offers one service to one type of client has a clear message, gets better and faster with every client, and can point to proof that a prospective client recognizes as relevant.

Practitioners who write about professional services make the same point in different words. Jonathan Stark, a consultant who writes about pricing, argues that specialization is how a service provider escapes being treated as a commodity, because a specialist can speak to a client's specific problems and is compared with fewer alternatives. The point is not that broad agencies cannot succeed. It is that a small agency without a reputation, a team, or a budget needs the efficiency that focus provides.

Specialization works along two axes, and you can use either or both.

- **Vertical (who you serve):** dental practices, independent restaurants, home-service contractors, real estate agents, local fitness studios.
- **Horizontal (what you do):** local search and Google Business Profile management, paid social ads, email newsletters, website conversion improvement.

A vertical focus makes marketing easier because you know where your clients gather and what they worry about. A horizontal focus makes delivery easier because you repeat the same work. The strongest early position for a small agency is usually narrow on both: one service for one type of client in a defined area.

## The niche scoring matrix

Use a weighted score to compare three to five candidate niches. Score each factor from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong), multiply by the weight, and add.

| Factor | Weight | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Skill fit | 3 | Can I already deliver this service to this type of client at a professional standard, with proof? |
| Access | 3 | Can I reach decision-makers in this niche directly, through my network, local presence, or communities? |
| Ability to pay | 2 | Do typical businesses in this niche have the margins to pay a monthly fee that covers my costs? |
| Recurring need | 2 | Is there work that needs doing every month, not just once? |
| Understanding | 1 | Do I understand how this business makes money and what its customers care about? |

The maximum score is 55. A niche scoring below about 30 usually has a fatal weakness, most often poor access or low ability to pay.

:::example Illustrative: scoring three niches
Marcus, a fictional marketer, spent four years running social media and local search for a regional chain of auto repair shops. He is considering three niches.

| Factor (weight) | Auto repair shops | Wedding photographers | Craft breweries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skill fit (3) | 5 = 15 | 3 = 9 | 3 = 9 |
| Access (3) | 4 = 12 | 2 = 6 | 3 = 9 |
| Ability to pay (2) | 4 = 8 | 2 = 4 | 3 = 6 |
| Recurring need (2) | 4 = 8 | 2 = 4 | 4 = 8 |
| Understanding (1) | 5 = 5 | 2 = 2 | 3 = 3 |
| **Total** | **48** | **25** | **35** |

Auto repair wins clearly, mostly because of skill fit and access: Marcus has proof, knows shop owners, and understands how they get customers. Wedding photographers fail on ability to pay and recurring need, since many are seasonal solo operators. He records breweries as a possible second niche once the first is working.
:::

## Writing a positioning statement

A positioning statement is a single sentence you can say aloud, put at the top of your website, and use to decide which work to accept. A reliable structure is:

> I help **[specific type of client]** with **[specific service]** so they can **[realistic outcome the client wants]**.

Three tests apply.

1. **Specificity test.** Could a competitor in a different niche say exactly the same sentence? If yes, narrow it.
2. **Honesty test.** Is the outcome something your service genuinely aims at, without implying a guarantee? "So they can rank number one on Google" fails; "so nearby drivers can find them and see current hours, photos, and reviews" passes.
3. **Recognition test.** Would a prospective client nod and say "that's me"? Use their words, not marketing jargon.

:::example Illustrative: from vague to specific
- Vague: "Full-service digital marketing for growing businesses."
- Better: "Social media and local search for auto repair shops."
- Best: "I help independent auto repair shops in the Tri-County area keep their Google Business Profiles accurate and active, so nearby drivers can find them, see real reviews, and call."
:::

## Choosing one core service

Your core service is the one thing you lead with in every conversation. It should be something you can deliver well today, that the niche needs every month, and that you can describe in a single short paragraph. Other services can be added later as upgrades, once the core is running smoothly and documented.

Equally important is a written list of what you will not do, at least for now. A "not now" list protects your focus when a friendly prospect asks for a logo, a full website rebuild, or a TikTok strategy outside your skill. Saying "that isn't something we offer, but I can recommend someone" builds more trust than delivering poor work.

:::case Dan Martell and focus in SaaS Academy
Dan Martell is a Canadian entrepreneur who, by his own account, built and sold three software companies (Spheric Technologies, Flowtown, and Clarity) before founding SaaS Academy, a coaching business. SaaS Academy is an example of extreme vertical focus: it serves founders of software-as-a-service businesses rather than business owners in general. That focus allows its marketing, content, and coaching frameworks to address one audience's specific problems, such as recurring revenue, churn, and hiring for a software company. Martell's book *Buy Back Your Time* (2023) draws heavily on examples from the founders he coaches.

The lesson here is not about coaching or software. It is that a narrow audience lets a provider build frameworks, content, and proof that compound, because each new client looks like the last one. A small agency serving one niche gets the same benefit at a smaller scale. These facts come from Martell's published book and public summaries of it; see References.
:::

# Chapter 3: Designing the Offer

:::objectives
- Distinguish a service description from an offer.
- Apply Alex Hormozi's Value Equation to strengthen an offer without exaggerating it.
- Package a core service into tiers with clear inclusions, exclusions, and turnaround times.
- Explain how a productized service works, using Design Pickle as a documented example.
:::

## A service is not an offer

A service description says what you do: "I manage Google Business Profiles." An offer says what the client gets, how quickly, with how much effort on their part, at what price, and with what assurance. Two agencies can deliver identical work and get very different responses, because one presents a vague service and the other presents a clear offer.

A complete offer answers six questions a buyer asks, usually silently:

1. What exactly will I get?
2. What result is this aimed at, and how will I know it is working?
3. How soon will I see something?
4. What do I have to do?
5. What does it cost, and how do I pay?
6. What happens if it is not right?

## The Value Equation

Alex Hormozi, an entrepreneur and author of *$100M Offers* (2021), proposes a simple model of perceived value that is widely used by small service businesses. In his formulation:

:::callout The Value Equation
**Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort and Sacrifice)**
:::

Hormozi describes the four drivers this way, in a post on his public page: the dream outcome is "the gap between their current reality and their dreams"; perceived likelihood is how likely the prospect believes it is "that they will achieve the result they're looking for if they make the purchase"; time delay is the "time between a client buying and receiving the promised benefit"; and effort and sacrifice are the "other costs accrued along the way." Value rises when the top of the fraction grows or the bottom shrinks.

The model is a thinking tool, not a law of economics, and it is easily abused. Inflating the dream outcome with promises you cannot keep raises perceived value for a month and destroys it, along with your reputation, after that. For an honest small agency the most powerful levers are usually on the bottom of the fraction and in the "likelihood" term, all of which you can improve without making a single exaggerated claim.

| Driver | Honest ways to improve it | What not to do |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | Describe the client's goal in their words ("more calls from nearby drivers") | Promise rankings, revenue, or follower counts |
| Perceived likelihood | Show relevant past work, explain your method step by step, offer a fair satisfaction term | Invent testimonials or imply results you did not produce |
| Time delay | Deliver a visible first win in week one (a corrected profile, a first report) | Claim instant results |
| Effort and sacrifice | Do the work for them; ask for information once, in one form; handle logins and posting | Hand clients a to-do list disguised as a service |

:::example Illustrative: rewriting an offer with the Value Equation
Before: "Google Business Profile management, $400 per month."

After: "Profile Care for Auto Repair Shops. In your first week we audit and correct your Google Business Profile (hours, services, categories, photos) and send you a one-page before-and-after summary. Every month after that we add fresh photos and posts, reply to every review within two business days using wording you approve, and send a plain-English report of calls, direction requests, and website clicks. You fill in one 15-minute intake form; we do the rest. $400 per month, cancel with 30 days' notice. If you are unhappy with the first month's work, tell us and we will redo it or refund that month."

Nothing in the rewrite promises a result the agency cannot control. The dream outcome is described in the client's terms, likelihood is raised by a visible method, time delay is shortened by the week-one audit, and effort is cut to a single form.
:::

## Packaging into tiers

Offering two or three tiers helps clients choose and shifts the conversation from "whether" to "which." Effective tiers differ in meaningful scope, not cosmetic extras, and each tier should be deliverable at a healthy margin on its own.

| Element | Essential | Standard | Plus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profile audit and correction | Included | Included | Included |
| Monthly posts | 2 | 4 | 8 |
| Review replies | Within 3 business days | Within 2 business days | Within 1 business day |
| Photos | Client supplies | 1 on-site visit per quarter | 1 on-site visit per month |
| Report | Monthly email | Monthly email | Monthly 20-minute call |
| Price per month (illustrative) | $250 | $400 | $700 |

Write the exclusions as clearly as the inclusions: for example, "does not include paid advertising spend, website changes, or responses to legal complaints." Exclusions prevent scope disputes later (Chapter 11).

:::case Design Pickle: a productized creative service
Design Pickle is a documented example of turning agency-style creative work into a productized service. Its founder, Russ Perry, has described in a published interview that he previously ran creative agencies and then worked as a consultant whose clients needed a steady flow of simple design requests. He set up a team and a digital request system for his consulting clients, and, in his words, "the system started working without me." According to the company's own history, Perry read *The 7 Day Startup* in December 2014 and launched Design Pickle in January 2015 as a flat-rate design subscription. Perry has written on the company blog that Design Pickle launched in 2015 as a "productized design service" offering "unlimited graphic design" for a flat monthly rate.

Two later developments are instructive. First, a podcast interview reported that turnaround time "served as a natural throttle" on unlimited requests, an example of how a productized service must build capacity limits into its design. Second, Perry later published a post titled "We invented unlimited design, and now we're killing it," announcing a change to the unlimited model. A productized offer is not fixed forever; its terms must be revised when they stop matching delivery reality.

These are Design Pickle's and Perry's own published accounts; see References. Their results are not a prediction for any other business.
:::

## Guarantees and satisfaction terms

A fair satisfaction term raises perceived likelihood without exposing you to risks you cannot control. Guarantee what you control: inputs, timeliness, and the quality of your work. Do not guarantee what you do not control: rankings, leads, sales, or the behavior of ad platforms. Examples of controllable guarantees include "every review answered within two business days or that month is free" and "if you are not satisfied with the first month's work, we will redo it or refund that month's fee." Put the exact terms in your agreement, and honor them without argument when invoked.

:::worksheet Build your offer
Offer name: ___
Client's goal, in their words: ___
What they get (inclusions): ___
[[box 3]]
What is excluded: ___
Visible first win and when it happens: ___
What the client must do (keep it short): ___
Price and payment terms: ___
Satisfaction term (controllable only): ___
:::

:::summary Chapter summary
- An offer answers what, why, how soon, how much effort, how much money, and what if it is wrong.
- Hormozi's Value Equation: raise dream outcome and likelihood; lower time delay and effort. Honest agencies win mostly on likelihood, speed to a first win, and low client effort.
- Use two or three tiers that differ in real scope, with written exclusions.
- Design Pickle shows how a productized service works, and that its terms must change when they stop matching capacity.
- Guarantee only what you control.
:::

# Chapter 4: Pricing and Unit Economics

:::objectives
- Calculate a price floor from your costs, target pay, and realistic billable capacity.
- Compare hourly billing, fixed pricing, and value-based pricing, and explain the incentives each creates.
- Use Dan Martell's Buyback Rate to decide when to delegate delivery work.
- Build a simple monthly profit model for a small agency.
:::

## Start with the floor

Every price conversation should begin with a number you will not go below: your **price floor**. It is the monthly fee at which serving one client covers its share of your costs and your own pay. Pricing below the floor means each new client makes the business poorer.

A practical floor calculation has five steps.

1. **Target owner pay.** Decide what you need to pay yourself per year, before tax.
2. **Overhead.** Add yearly costs not tied to any one client: software, insurance, accounting, phone, equipment, training, and marketing.
3. **Profit margin.** Add a margin for reserves and reinvestment; 15 to 20 percent of the total is a common planning figure for small service firms.
4. **Billable capacity.** Estimate the hours per year you can actually spend on client work. Most solo owners overestimate this. Selling, admin, and learning commonly consume 30 to 50 percent of working time.
5. **Divide.** Total yearly requirement divided by billable hours gives a minimum effective hourly rate. Multiply by the hours a client needs per month to get that client's floor price.

:::example Illustrative: computing a price floor
Priya, a fictional solo agency owner:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Target owner pay | $60,000 |
| Overhead (software $3,600, insurance $1,200, accounting $1,500, phone and equipment $1,700, marketing $2,000) | $10,000 |
| Subtotal | $70,000 |
| Margin (20% of subtotal) | $14,000 |
| Yearly requirement | $84,000 |
| Working weeks (48) × 40 hours | 1,920 hours |
| Billable share (60%) | 1,152 hours |
| Minimum effective hourly rate ($84,000 / 1,152) | $72.92 |

Her Standard package takes about 6 hours per client per month. Floor price: 6 × $72.92 = $437.50. Her planned $400 price is below the floor. She has three choices: raise the price to at least $440, cut delivery to about 5.5 hours through better systems, or accept that the package works only as a starter offer that leads to a larger engagement. She chooses to raise it to $450 and to document the process to bring hours down.
:::

## Three ways to set the price

**Hourly billing.** The client pays for time. It feels safe when scope is unclear, but it has structural problems. Income is capped by hours worked. Getting faster lowers your revenue. The client carries the risk of an estimate running long, and the incentives of the two parties point in opposite directions. Jonathan Stark's essay collection *Hourly Billing Is Nuts* makes this case at length, arguing that hourly billing "devalues the expertise of the consultant and encourages mistrust in the client because the financial incentives of the two parties are misaligned."

**Fixed pricing.** The client pays a set fee for a defined scope. The provider carries the risk of the work taking longer and keeps the benefit of doing it faster. Fixed pricing rewards systems and experience, and it gives clients the certainty most of them prefer. Its danger is scoping: a vague scope turns a fixed price into unlimited work.

**Value-based pricing.** The fee is set by reference to what the outcome is worth to the client, not what it costs to produce. Stark suggests in a recorded interview a practical starting point: calculate your floor from costs, estimate the annual value of the outcome to the client, take roughly one-tenth of that value, and charge the higher of the two numbers. Value pricing requires a genuine discovery conversation (Chapter 8) and a client who can articulate the value of the outcome. It is most useful for project work with a clear business goal, and less practical for small recurring packages.

| Method | Who carries the risk of overrun | What getting faster does | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Client | Lowers your revenue | Truly undefined advisory work, briefly |
| Fixed | Provider | Raises your margin | Productized packages and defined projects |
| Value-based | Provider | Raises your margin | Projects with a measurable business goal |

For most small agencies the practical recommendation is to sell fixed-price packages, set above a calculated floor, and use value-based reasoning to set the price of larger projects.

## Buying back delivery time

As an agency grows, the owner faces a recurring question: should I do this task myself or pay someone else to do it? Dan Martell's Buyback Rate offers a simple rule. In *Buy Back Your Time*, he proposes dividing your annual pay by 2,000 working hours to get an effective hourly rate, then dividing by four. Tasks that can be handed off at or below that rate should be handed off. Martell frames delegation as a roughly fourfold return trade rather than an expense.

:::example Illustrative: applying the Buyback Rate
Priya now pays herself $80,000 a year. Effective hourly rate: $80,000 / 2,000 = $40. Buyback Rate: $40 / 4 = $10 per hour.

She spends 20 hours a month scheduling posts and resizing images. A part-time assistant at $20 per hour would cost more than her Buyback Rate, so by Martell's strict test she should not hand off this work yet at that price. However, a scheduling tool and a template library costing $60 a month would cut the task to 8 hours, saving 12 hours at an effective cost of $5 an hour, well below the rate. She adopts the tool first and revisits hiring when her pay, and therefore her Buyback Rate, rises.
:::

## A monthly profit model

Keep a one-page monthly model. Update it at the end of every month.

| Line | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Clients | Count | 10 |
| Average monthly fee | Revenue / clients | $480 |
| Revenue | Clients × average fee | $4,800 |
| Direct delivery cost | Contractors, client-specific tools | $900 |
| Gross profit | Revenue − direct cost | $3,900 |
| Gross margin | Gross profit / revenue | 81% |
| Overhead | Monthly share of yearly overhead | $833 |
| Owner pay | Monthly draw | $2,500 |
| Net profit | Gross profit − overhead − owner pay | $567 |
| Delivery hours | Logged | 58 |
| Revenue per delivery hour | Revenue / hours | $82.76 |

Revenue per delivery hour is the most useful single number for a small agency. If it falls month after month, either prices are too low, scope is creeping, or systems are not improving.

:::warning Common pricing mistakes
- Pricing by copying a competitor's rate without knowing their costs or scope.
- Discounting the first month so steeply that the client anchors to the discounted price.
- Including unlimited revisions or "unlimited" anything without a capacity limit.
- Forgetting that you will spend unpaid hours selling, invoicing, and learning.
- Absorbing client ad spend on your own card, which creates cash-flow and collection risk.
:::

## Registration, tax, and banking

The steps below are common for a small US marketing business. Requirements differ by state, county, and city, and this list is a starting point for your own checks, not legal or tax advice.

1. **Choose a structure.** A sole proprietorship requires the least paperwork. A limited liability company (LLC) separates business and personal liability in many situations and is a common choice for service firms. The US Small Business Administration (SBA) publishes plain-language comparisons of structures. An accountant or attorney in your state can tell you which suits your situation.
2. **Register the name.** If you operate under a name other than your own legal name, most states and counties require an assumed-name or "doing business as" filing.
3. **Get an Employer Identification Number (EIN).** The IRS issues EINs free of charge through its website. Even a solo owner often uses one to open a business bank account and to avoid giving clients a Social Security number on tax forms.
4. **Check local licenses.** Many cities and counties require a general business license even for home-based service firms.
5. **Open a separate bank account.** Never mix business and personal money. Separation makes bookkeeping, tax preparation, and any later financing far simpler.
6. **Plan for tax.** Self-employed people in the US generally pay income tax and self-employment tax, often through quarterly estimated payments. The IRS Self-Employed Individuals Tax Center explains the basics. Set aside a fixed percentage of every payment received in a separate account until you know your actual liability.
7. **Consider insurance.** Professional liability (errors and omissions) insurance covers claims that your work caused a client financial loss. General liability covers injury and property damage. Some clients will require proof of coverage in their contracts.

## The ownership principle

The single most important operating rule for a marketing agency is this: **the client owns their accounts, their data, and their ad spend.** Their Google Business Profile, ad accounts, analytics properties, social media pages, domain name, website hosting, and email lists should be registered to the client, with your agency granted access as a manager or partner. When the relationship ends, the client should be able to remove your access without losing anything.

This principle protects the client, and it protects you. An agency that holds a client's accounts hostage invites disputes, chargebacks, and complaints. An agency that sets things up correctly from the first day signals professionalism and makes it easy for satisfied clients to stay.

Ad spend deserves particular care. Wherever possible, the client's own card should be attached to the ad account so that the platform bills the client directly. If you must pay ad costs and re-bill them, require a deposit in advance and state the arrangement in writing; floating a client's ad spend on your own card can drain your cash quickly if they pay late.

:::case Google's rules for agencies managing Business Profiles
Google publishes specific policies for third parties, such as agencies, that manage Business Profiles on behalf of businesses. Among the requirements stated in Google's Business Profile third-party policies:

- "All end customers must retain ownership or co-ownership of their Business Profile at all times."
- Within seven business days of a customer's notice, the agency must give the customer the ability to disassociate the agency's account and regain exclusive control.
- If you charge a management fee, you must tell customers that Business Profile "is a service provided at no extra cost," in writing before managing the profile and on invoices.
- "To respond to reviews on behalf of the end customer, you must have an explicit approval. Verbal consent isn't sufficient."
- Agencies must not make "false, misleading, or unrealistic claims" about their services or the results customers can expect.

These rules turn the ownership principle into a written obligation for one of the most common small-agency services. Read the current version of the policy before you offer profile management, because platform policies change. See References.
:::

# Chapter 6: Building Proof Before Your First Client

:::objectives
- Explain why proof drives perceived likelihood and how to build it honestly from zero.
- Produce three kinds of starter proof: own-account results, demonstration projects, and audits.
- Write a short, accurate case study using a standard structure.
- Apply the FTC's rules on endorsements, testimonials, and reviews.
:::

## The proof problem

A prospective client's central question is "will this work for me?" In the language of Chapter 3, they are estimating perceived likelihood. Relevant proof, evidence that you have done similar work for similar businesses, is the strongest answer. New agencies face a circular problem: they need clients to get proof and proof to get clients.

The solution is not to invent proof. It is to create genuine, clearly labeled evidence of skill before the first paid engagement, and to collect real evidence systematically once clients arrive.

## Three kinds of starter proof

**1. Your own accounts.** If you sell social media management, your agency's own accounts are a public sample of your judgment and consistency. If you sell local search, your own Business Profile (if eligible) is a sample of your standards. A neglected agency account undermines every claim on your website.

**2. Demonstration projects.** Create a realistic sample for a fictional business in your niche, or a clearly labeled redesign concept for a real public example: a month of sample posts for a fictional auto repair shop, a mock landing page, or a sample monthly report. Label demonstrations as demonstrations. Do not present them as client work.

**3. Audits.** A short, specific audit of a real business's public presence (their Google Business Profile, website basics, and review responses) demonstrates expertise and gives the prospect something useful. Audits double as an outreach tool in Chapter 7. Keep them brief, factual, and respectful.

A fourth option, discounted or free work for a small number of early clients, can produce real proof quickly, but treat it carefully. Set a clear end date and a written scope, agree in advance that you may describe the results (with the client's permission), and avoid discounts so deep that you anchor a long-term client to an unsustainable price.

## Writing an honest case study

Once you have real client work, document it in a standard one-page structure.

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Client | Type of business and location (named only with written permission) |
| Starting point | The situation before, with specific facts |
| What we did | The actions taken, in plain language |
| What changed | Measured changes over a stated period, with the data source |
| Caveats | Other factors that may have contributed; what the numbers do not show |
| Client's words | A quote, used with written permission |

:::example Illustrative: a case study written honestly
Client: A family-owned auto repair shop in a mid-sized town (name used with permission).

Starting point: Profile listed outdated hours, had 14 unanswered reviews, and showed three photos, two of them from 2019.

What we did: Corrected hours and services, added 25 current photos, replied to all reviews with wording approved by the owner, and posted weekly updates for three months.

What changed: Over the three months, the profile's reported phone calls rose from 61 to 88 per month, according to the Business Profile performance report. Direction requests rose from 140 to 171.

Caveats: The shop also began a radio spot in month two, and calls are seasonal; we cannot separate the effects. Calls are not the same as booked jobs.

This format is credible precisely because it is modest. Prospects trust a case study that names its limitations.
:::

## The legal rules on testimonials and reviews

US law treats endorsements and reviews in advertising seriously, and agencies are directly exposed because they often write marketing content and manage review responses for clients.

The Federal Trade Commission's *Guides Concerning the Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising* were revised in 2023. According to the FTC's announcement, the revisions address "procuring, suppressing, boosting, organizing, publishing, upvoting, downvoting, or editing consumer reviews so as to distort what consumers think of a product," incentivized reviews, employee reviews, and fake negative reviews of competitors. The guides state that material connections between an endorser and a seller must be disclosed when "a significant minority of the audience" does not expect the connection, and that online disclosures must be "unavoidable."

In August 2024 the FTC announced a final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials, which, among other things, prohibits fake reviews, including those attributed to people who do not exist, and the buying of positive or negative reviews.

For a small agency, the practical rules are clear:

- Never write, buy, or arrange fake reviews for a client, and never post negative reviews of a client's competitors.
- Never offer customers incentives conditioned on leaving a positive review.
- Disclose paid relationships in influencer and endorsement content clearly, in the content itself, not only on a profile page.
- Use testimonials only with permission and only when they reflect the person's honest experience.
- When a testimonial describes unusual results, do not present it as typical.

:::warning Your own marketing is covered too
The same rules apply to the agency's own website and social posts. Do not publish client logos, results, or quotes without written permission, and do not imply you have clients or results you do not have.
:::

# Chapter 7: Finding Clients

:::objectives
- Describe Alex Hormozi's Core Four lead channels and choose an order suited to a new agency.
- Run a warm-outreach campaign and a compliant cold-outreach campaign.
- Apply CAN-SPAM and telemarketing rules to outreach.
- Build a referral habit and track a simple sales pipeline.
:::

## The four ways to reach people

In *$100M Leads* (2023), Alex Hormozi organizes lead generation into four methods he calls the Core Four, defined by two questions: are you reaching one person at a time or many at once, and do they already know you?

| | One-to-one | One-to-many |
|---|---|---|
| **People who know you (warm)** | Warm outreach | Posting free content |
| **People who don't (cold)** | Cold outreach | Paid ads |

Each channel trades time, money, or both. Warm outreach costs mainly time and courage and usually produces the first clients fastest, because the people already know and trust you to some degree. Content is slow at first and compounds over time. Cold outreach and paid ads reach strangers and therefore depend most heavily on a clear, proven offer.

For a new agency, a sensible order is: warm outreach first, then one content channel, then cold outreach once the offer is proven, and paid ads only when you know what a client is worth and what you can afford to pay to acquire one.

## Warm outreach

Make a list of everyone you know who owns or influences a business in your niche, plus anyone who knows many such people: former colleagues, suppliers, family friends, members of local associations. Contact each person individually, by the channel they actually use, with a short, personal message. The aim is not to pitch but to tell people what you now do and to ask whether they know anyone it might help.

:::example Illustrative: a warm message
"Hi Dave, it's Marcus. Hope the shop's been busy. I've started a small business helping independent repair shops keep their Google profiles accurate and active: hours, photos, review replies, that kind of thing. I'm looking for two or three shops to work with this spring. Do you know anyone who might want help with that, or would a quick look at your own profile be useful? No pressure either way."

The message is personal, specific, honest about scale, and makes a small, easy request.
:::

## Content

Choose one platform where your niche's owners actually spend time, and publish useful material regularly: short explanations of common mistakes, before-and-after examples (with permission), checklists, and answers to questions you hear in sales calls. Content does three jobs. It shows your expertise to people who are not ready to buy, it warms up the people you later contact directly, and it gives prospects a reason to trust you before the first call.

## Cold outreach, done lawfully

Cold outreach means contacting businesses that do not know you. Done well, it is respectful, specific, and useful. Done badly, it is spam, and in some forms it is illegal.

**Email.** The US CAN-SPAM Act applies to commercial email, including business-to-business messages. The FTC's compliance guide lists requirements including: do not use false or misleading header information; do not use deceptive subject lines; identify the message as an advertisement where required; include a valid physical postal address; tell recipients how to opt out; honor opt-out requests promptly (within 10 business days); and monitor what others do on your behalf. Other countries' rules, such as Canada's anti-spam law and European privacy law, can be stricter and may require prior consent.

**Phone and text.** Calls and text messages are governed in the US by the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) and the national Do Not Call rules, which restrict autodialed and prerecorded calls and texts, especially to mobile numbers, without prior consent. In February 2024 the Federal Communications Commission ruled that AI-generated voices in robocalls count as "artificial" under the TCPA. For a small agency, the safe practice is to avoid automated calling and texting of prospects entirely and to make only individual, manually dialed calls to business lines, while checking state rules.

**The audit approach.** A respectful cold email offers something useful: a short audit of the prospect's public profile with two or three specific, fixable observations. Keep it brief, accurate, and free of exaggeration.

:::example Illustrative: a cold email with an audit
Subject: Two quick fixes for your Google profile

"Hi Ana, I help independent repair shops in the Tri-County area with their Google Business Profiles. I looked at yours and noticed two things that are easy to fix: your Saturday hours show as closed, though your website says 8 to 1, and nine recent reviews have no reply. I've put a one-page summary with a few more observations at the link below. If it's useful, I'm happy to talk; if not, no reply needed.

Marcus Lee, Lee Local Marketing, 123 Main St, Springfield. To stop hearing from me, reply 'no thanks.'"

The email is specific, verifiable, short, includes a physical address, and offers a simple opt-out.
:::

## Referrals and partnerships

Satisfied clients and complementary businesses are the most reliable long-term sources of new clients for small agencies. Ask for referrals at moments of visible success, such as after a strong monthly report, and make it easy: "If you know another shop owner who'd find this useful, I'd be grateful for an introduction." Build relationships with businesses that serve the same clients but do not compete with you: web developers, bookkeepers, equipment suppliers, and local trade associations. If you pay referral fees, disclose them where the law or the relationship requires it.

## Tracking the pipeline

Keep a simple pipeline with five stages and review it weekly.

| Stage | Definition | Weekly question |
|---|---|---|
| Contacted | First message sent | How many new people did I contact? |
| Replied | Any response | Which messages got replies? |
| Call booked | Discovery call scheduled | Am I booking enough calls? |
| Proposal sent | Written proposal delivered | Are proposals going out within two days? |
| Won or lost | Decision made | Why did I win or lose? |

The weekly discipline matters more than the tool. Owners who track outreach every week tend to keep selling during busy delivery months, which is what breaks the feast-and-famine cycle described in Chapter 1.

- Warm messages should be personal, specific, and make a small request.

# Chapter 8: Sales Conversations and Proposals

:::objectives
- Run a structured discovery call that diagnoses before it prescribes.
- Qualify prospects on fit, need, budget, and decision process.
- Present three options and write a short proposal that closes.
- Identify the essential terms of a service agreement.
:::

## Diagnose before you prescribe

Blair Enns, a consultant to creative firms, sets out twelve "proclamations" in *The Win Without Pitching Manifesto* (2010). The third is "We will diagnose before we prescribe." Enns describes four phases of a client engagement (diagnose the problem or opportunity, prescribe a therapy, apply the therapy, and reapply it as necessary) and argues that prescribing without diagnosis would be treated as malpractice in most other professions. When a client arrives self-diagnosed ("we just need more posts"), the professional response, in the spirit of the manifesto, is to validate that diagnosis before acting on it: "you may be right, but let's check."

The practical meaning for a small agency is that the first sales conversation is a structured diagnostic, not a presentation. Enns also writes that the objective of early conversations is not "to sell, convince or persuade" but "simply to determine if there exists a fit suitable enough to merit a next step." That framing lowers pressure on both sides and leads to better clients.

## A discovery call in five parts

Allow 30 to 45 minutes. Send a short agenda in advance.

1. **Context (5 minutes).** How the business works: services, typical customer, busy and slow seasons, how customers find them today.
2. **Goal (5–10 minutes).** What they want to change and why now. Ask for specifics: "When you say more customers, what kind of jobs, and roughly how many more a month would make a difference?"
3. **Current state (10 minutes).** What they have tried, what worked, what did not, and who does the marketing now. Look at their accounts together if possible.
4. **Constraints (5–10 minutes).** Budget range, timeline, decision-makers, and anything that would stop them proceeding. Raise money early; Enns's ninth proclamation is "We will address issues of money early."
5. **Next step (5 minutes).** Either agree to send a proposal by a specific date, recommend someone else, or agree there is no fit.

:::callout Questions that reveal value
- "If this worked well, what would be different in six months?"
- "What is a typical new customer worth to you over a year?"
- "What happens if you do nothing about this for another year?"
- "Who else needs to be comfortable with this decision?"
:::

## Qualifying

Not every prospect should become a client. Qualify on four criteria.

| Criterion | Proceed if | Pause or decline if |
|---|---|---|
| Fit | They are in your niche and need your core service | They need something you don't offer well |
| Need | There is a clear gap your service addresses | Their real problem is elsewhere (pricing, service quality, staffing) |
| Budget | Their range is at or above your floor | They want your Plus tier at your Essential price |
| Decision | You are speaking with the decision-maker, or can reach them | Decisions are vague or controlled by someone you cannot meet |

Declining politely, and recommending another provider where possible, protects your capacity for clients you can genuinely help.

## Three options

After the call, present three options that differ in scope and price: typically your Essential, Standard, and Plus tiers, adjusted to what you learned. Three options let the client choose how much help they want rather than whether to hire you, and they make the middle option easy to justify. Jonathan Stark recommends a similar structure of small, medium, and large options, each tied to the client's goal.

## The short proposal

A proposal should confirm what was discussed, not introduce new ideas. Keep it to two to five pages.

1. **Situation.** Two or three sentences restating their context in their words.
2. **Goal.** The outcome they described and how progress will be measured.
3. **Approach.** Your method, in plain steps.
4. **Options.** The three tiers with inclusions, exclusions, and prices.
5. **Timeline.** What happens in week one, month one, and month three.
6. **Terms.** Payment, minimum term, notice period, and satisfaction term.
7. **Next step.** How to accept (signature and first payment) and when work begins.

Send the proposal within two business days of the call, and offer a 15-minute follow-up call to walk through it. Proposals that arrive a week later lose momentum.

:::example Illustrative: proposal summary page
**Situation.** Rivera Auto has served the east side for 22 years. Most new customers come from referrals and Google searches. The profile has outdated hours and unanswered reviews, and Ana handles it when she has time, which is rarely.

**Goal.** Make sure nearby drivers searching for repairs see accurate, current information and real responses to reviews, measured by profile calls, direction requests, and website clicks each month.

**Options.** Essential $250/month; Standard $450/month (recommended); Plus $750/month. Three-month minimum, then month to month with 30 days' notice.

**Next step.** Sign and pay the first month by March 8; audit and corrections completed by March 15.
:::

## The service agreement

Every engagement needs a written agreement. Have a template reviewed by an attorney in your state once, then reuse it. Essential terms include:

- **Scope.** Inclusions and exclusions, referencing the chosen tier.
- **Fees and payment.** Amount, due date, method, late-payment terms, and how ad spend is handled.
- **Term and termination.** Minimum term, notice period, and what happens on termination (access handover, final report).
- **Ownership.** The client owns accounts, data, and finished deliverables once paid; you retain your templates and methods.
- **Approvals.** Who approves content and review replies, and how quickly.
- **Client responsibilities.** Information, access, and timely feedback.
- **Confidentiality and data.** How you handle client and customer information.
- **No guarantee of results.** A plain statement that results depend on factors outside your control.
- **Limitation of liability.** Commonly capped at fees paid over a period; ask your attorney.

# Chapter 9: Onboarding and Delivery Systems

:::objectives
- Design a two-week onboarding sequence that produces a visible first win.
- Document delivery tasks using Dan Martell's Camcorder Method.
- Structure recurring work in a project management system.
- Build quality control into delivery with checklists and approvals.
:::

## Onboarding sets the relationship

The first two weeks of an engagement shape how a client feels about the next twelve months. A well-run onboarding reassures the client that they chose well, collects everything you need in one pass, and delivers a visible first win quickly, which is the "time delay" lever from Chapter 3.

:::checklist Two-week onboarding sequence
- [ ] Day 0: Agreement signed, first payment received, welcome email sent with what happens next.
- [ ] Day 1: Intake form sent (brand details, logins via manager access, approvers, photos, priorities).
- [ ] Day 2: Kickoff call (30 minutes) to confirm goals, approvals, and communication rhythm.
- [ ] Days 3–5: Baseline captured (screenshots and current metrics) before any changes.
- [ ] Days 5–10: First visible improvements made (for example, profile corrected).
- [ ] Day 10: Before-and-after summary sent to the client.
- [ ] Day 14: Recurring schedule running; first month's calendar approved.
:::

Capturing a baseline before changing anything is essential. Without it, you cannot show later what changed, and you cannot write an honest case study.

## Documenting delivery: the Camcorder Method

Most small agency owners carry their processes in their heads. That works until they are sick, on holiday, overloaded, or trying to hire. Dan Martell's *Buy Back Your Time* describes what he calls the **Camcorder Method** for transferring work: record yourself doing a task (a screen recording with narration), then turn the recording into a written playbook that someone else can follow. The point is that documentation captures what you actually do, not what you think you do.

A useful playbook for each recurring task includes:

1. **Purpose.** Why the task matters and what "done well" looks like.
2. **Trigger and frequency.** When it happens.
3. **Inputs.** What is needed before starting.
4. **Steps.** Numbered, with screenshots or a link to the recording.
5. **Quality checklist.** What to verify before delivery.
6. **Time standard.** How long it should take once learned.

Start with the task you repeat most often. For many agencies that is the monthly report or the weekly posting cycle. A playbook written once saves hours every month and is the precondition for delegating the task, whether to an assistant, a contractor, or a supervised software tool.

## Structuring recurring work

Use one project or board per client, built from a standard template. A recurring template might look like this:

| Week | Tasks | Owner | Approval needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Draft next month's posts; collect photos | Producer | Client approves calendar |
| Week 2 | Schedule approved posts; reply to reviews | Producer | Review replies per approved templates |
| Week 3 | Mid-month check on metrics and profile changes | Owner | None |
| Week 4 | Build monthly report; plan next month | Owner | None |

Basecamp, discussed in Chapter 1, was built for this exact reason: 37signals' founders wrote that their "projects and client communications were a mess" when managed by email. Whatever tool you choose, the principle is the same: every task, file, and approval for a client lives in one place, and nobody has to search an inbox to know what is due.

## Approvals and quality control

Errors in client marketing are public. A misspelled business name, a wrong price, or a careless review reply can embarrass a client in front of their customers. Two controls prevent most problems.

**Approval rules.** Agree in writing what needs client approval before publishing (monthly calendars, new offers, replies to negative reviews) and what you may publish under pre-approved templates (thank-you replies to positive reviews, routine posts). Google's third-party policy, covered in Chapter 5, requires explicit approval to reply to reviews on a client's behalf, so get that approval in writing and keep it.

**Pre-publication checklist.** Before anything goes live, check: business name, address, and phone correct; prices and offers current and authorized; spelling and grammar; links working; images licensed or owned; no claims about results, health, or safety that the client cannot substantiate; required disclosures present.

In *Buy Back Your Time* (2023), Dan Martell presents the Camcorder Method as part of a broader argument that founders should hire to buy back their time rather than simply to grow headcount. In Martell's sequencing, delivery is the second rung, after administrative support. That ordering is directly relevant to agency owners, whose delivery work typically consumes most of their week. Recording and documenting delivery is what makes that second rung possible. These are Martell's frameworks as summarized in his book and in published reviews; see References.

# Chapter 10: Reporting and Measurement

:::objectives
- Distinguish activity, leading, and outcome metrics for a marketing engagement.
- Explain the limits of attribution and how to report honestly within them.
- Build a one-page monthly report clients actually read.
- Use reports to guide decisions and renewals.
:::

## Three levels of measurement

| Level | Examples | What it tells you | Who controls it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Activity | Posts published, reviews answered, ads launched | Whether the agreed work happened | The agency |
| Leading indicators | Profile views, calls, direction requests, clicks, form fills, cost per lead | Whether people are responding | Shared: agency, platform, market |
| Outcomes | Booked jobs, revenue, new customers | Whether the business benefited | Mostly the client |

A good report includes all three, clearly separated. Activity metrics prove you did the work. Leading indicators show whether the work is reaching people. Outcomes, which usually require data from the client, show whether it matters. Never present activity as if it were an outcome: "we posted 16 times" is not a result.

## The attribution problem

Attribution means assigning credit for a customer to a particular marketing activity. It is genuinely difficult. A customer may see a post, search the business name a week later, read reviews, ask a friend, and then call. Analytics platforms use models to assign credit, and different models give different answers. Phone calls, word of mouth, and offline activity are often invisible to the tools.

The honest approach is to state the limits plainly in your reports, use consistent definitions month to month, and where possible add simple client-side evidence, such as asking new customers "how did you hear about us?" at booking. Over several months, consistent measurement shows trends more reliably than any single month's attribution.

## The one-page monthly report

Clients rarely read long reports. A single page, delivered on the same day each month, works better.

1. **Headline.** One or two sentences: what happened this month and what it means.
2. **Work completed.** Activity metrics against the plan.
3. **Key numbers.** Three to five leading indicators, with last month and the same month last year where available.
4. **What we learned.** One or two observations.
5. **Next month.** What you will do and any decisions needed from the client.

:::example Illustrative: monthly report headline
"April was steady. Profile calls were 84, down slightly from 88 in March but up from 63 last April, according to the Business Profile performance report. We answered all 11 new reviews within two business days and published 4 posts. The post showing the new alignment machine had the most views. Next month we will add photos of the expanded waiting area; please send them by May 5."

The headline is factual, compares fairly, names its data source, and ends with a concrete request.
:::

## Using reports to make decisions

A report is a management tool, not just a deliverable. Use each month's report to answer three questions: Is the agreed work happening on time? Are leading indicators trending in the right direction over three months? Is there a change, in the offer, the content, or the channel, worth testing next month? Quarterly, review the engagement with the client against the goal agreed in the proposal. This review is also the natural moment to discuss renewal, expansion, or, when results are poor, a change of approach.

:::warning Honest reporting rules
- Report bad months as plainly as good ones.
- Keep metric definitions constant; if you change one, say so.
- Name the data source for every number.
- Do not claim credit for outcomes you cannot connect to your work.
- Do not cherry-pick date ranges to make results look better.
:::

Headline (two sentences): ___

[[box 3]]
What we learned: ___

# Chapter 11: Retention, Scope, and Growth

:::objectives
- Prevent and manage scope creep with a written change process.
- Calculate client lifetime value and explain why retention drives agency economics.
- Decide when and whom to hire, using Martell's Replacement Ladder and capacity math.
- Recognize when to raise prices, add a service, or stop serving a client.
:::

## Scope creep

Scope creep is the gradual expansion of work beyond what was agreed, without a matching change in price. It rarely arrives as a big request. It arrives as "while you're in there, could you also…" repeated a dozen times. Each favor seems small; together they turn a profitable client into an unprofitable one and teach the client that the scope is negotiable.

Three habits prevent most scope creep.

1. **Write exclusions as clearly as inclusions** in the proposal and agreement (Chapter 3).
2. **Use a change request process.** When a request falls outside scope, respond warmly and in writing: "Happy to help with that. It's outside our current plan, so here are two options: we can add it as a one-time project for $X, or move you to the Plus tier, which includes it." Never simply say no, and never simply say yes.
3. **Track hours per client.** If a client's delivery time rises month after month, the numbers will show it before your resentment does.

Jonathan Stark suggests using the agreed outcome as a filter: requests that contribute to the goal can be discussed; requests that do not are parked until after the goal is achieved. That filter keeps conversations about value rather than about favors.

## Why retention matters most

Winning a client costs time and money: outreach, calls, proposals, onboarding. Keeping one costs far less. The economics show up in **client lifetime value** (LTV): the gross profit a client produces over the whole relationship.

:::callout Lifetime value
**LTV = monthly gross profit per client × average months retained**
:::

:::example Illustrative: the value of retention
An agency's average client pays $450 a month, with delivery costs of $90, for monthly gross profit of $360.

- If clients stay an average of 6 months: LTV = $360 × 6 = $2,160.
- If clients stay an average of 18 months: LTV = $360 × 18 = $6,480.

Tripling retention triples lifetime value with no change in price or delivery. It also means the agency needs far fewer new clients each year to stay the same size. With 10 clients and 6-month retention, it must replace about 20 clients a year; with 18-month retention, about 7.
:::

Retention comes from the practices in earlier chapters: a clear offer, a strong onboarding with a first win, reliable delivery, honest reporting, and quarterly reviews that connect work to goals. It also comes from small habits: responding to messages within one business day, remembering what matters to the client, and telling them about problems before they discover them.

## Raising prices

Prices should rise as your proof, skill, and systems improve, and as costs rise. Raise prices for new clients first, which carries no risk to existing revenue. For existing clients, give written notice (30 to 60 days is common), explain briefly, and consider holding the current price for long-standing clients for a period. Some clients will leave; if your floor calculation was right, the ones who stay at the new price are more profitable than the ones who left.

## When to hire

Hiring is the main way to grow capacity past the owner's limit, and it is also the main way small agencies get into financial trouble. Use capacity math and Martell's sequencing together.

**Capacity math.** Hire when you are consistently at or above about 85 percent of capacity for two to three months, have a pipeline that will fill the new capacity, and have documented the work the new person will do (Chapter 9). Hiring before documentation means you will spend your reclaimed hours training by improvisation.

**Sequencing.** Martell's Replacement Ladder (administrative assistant, delivery or operations, marketing, sales, leadership) suggests that agency owners usually benefit first from administrative help and then from a delivery person who takes over recurring production. Contractors are a lower-risk first step than employees for many small firms, but the legal distinction between contractor and employee depends on the actual working relationship; the IRS and the US Department of Labor publish guidance, and state rules differ.

:::example Illustrative: the first hire decision
Priya has 12 clients and works 50 hours a week. Delivery takes 34 hours, admin 8, selling 8. Her playbooks cover posting, review replies, and reports.

A contractor producer at $25 an hour for 20 hours a week costs about $2,170 a month. Taking 20 delivery hours off her desk frees time for selling and quarterly reviews. If she uses half the freed time to win and serve three more Standard clients at $450 each, revenue rises by $1,350 a month while her own hours fall to about 40 a week. The hire does not pay for itself immediately in cash; it pays in capacity, quality, and the owner's time, which Martell argues is the right way to frame it. Priya decides to proceed only after confirming three months of cash reserves.
:::

## Adding services

Add a second service only when the first is documented, profitable, and running without daily owner involvement. The best second service is one existing clients already ask for and that uses similar skills. Mailchimp's history, discussed in Chapter 1, is an extreme version of this pattern: its founders built the email tool because their own agency clients needed it.

## Ending client relationships

Some clients should not be retained: those who repeatedly pay late, demand work far outside scope, treat staff disrespectfully, or ask you to do something unethical or unlawful, such as posting fake reviews. End the relationship professionally: give the notice your agreement requires, deliver a final report, hand over all access promptly (within Google's seven-business-day window for Business Profiles), and keep a written record.

# Chapter 12: Using AI Responsibly in an Agency

:::objectives
- Identify agency tasks where AI tools help and where they create risk.
- Apply Google's published guidance on AI-generated content.
- Set review, disclosure, and data rules for AI use with clients.
- Explain the regulatory risks of AI claims, AI reviews, and AI voice calls.
:::

## Where AI helps, and where it does not

AI writing and image tools can speed up many routine agency tasks: first drafts of posts, variations of ad copy, summaries of reports, outlines for blog articles, image resizing, and transcriptions. Used well, they lower delivery hours per client, the key efficiency lever from Chapter 4.

They also create real risks. Language models can state false facts with confidence. They can produce generic content that sounds like every other business. They can repeat errors at scale. And when they are given client data, they raise confidentiality questions.

| Task | Good use of AI | Human must |
|---|---|---|
| Social posts | Draft options from a brief | Check facts, voice, offers, and prices; approve |
| Review replies | Draft a polite reply | Personalize, check for sensitive issues, follow approval rules |
| Reports | Summarize metrics into a draft headline | Verify every number against the source |
| Blog articles | Outline and first draft | Add real expertise and examples; fact-check |
| Ad copy | Generate variations to test | Check platform policies and claim substantiation |

## Google's guidance on AI content

Google has published guidance on AI-generated content for search. In a February 2023 post, Google Search Central wrote that "appropriate use of AI or automation is not against our guidelines," but that "using automation—including AI—to generate content with the primary purpose of manipulating ranking in search results is a violation of our spam policies." The post adds that "using AI doesn't give content any special gains," and that content succeeds if it is "useful, helpful, original," and demonstrates expertise, experience, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness. Google's later documentation warns that generating many pages "without adding value for users may violate Google's spam policy on scaled content abuse" and says it is "critical to manually factcheck and review all AI-generated content for accuracy and trustworthiness before publishing."

For an agency, the implication is straightforward: use AI to assist people who know the client's business, not to mass-produce pages.

## Agency AI rules

Write a short policy and share it with clients.

1. **Human review.** Every AI-assisted item is reviewed by a person who checks facts, voice, and claims before it goes to the client or the public.
2. **No fabrication.** AI tools must never be used to create fake reviews, testimonials, customer stories, statistics, or endorsements.
3. **Data care.** Do not paste confidential client or customer information into tools whose terms allow that data to be used for training or shared; check each tool's data settings.
4. **Disclosure.** Tell clients, in your agreement, that you use AI tools to assist production and that a person reviews all output. Follow platform rules on labeling AI-generated images and media.
5. **Accountability.** The agency is responsible for what it publishes, regardless of which tool drafted it.

:::case Regulators and AI marketing claims
US regulators have made clear that existing law applies to AI. In September 2024 the FTC announced "Operation AI Comply," a set of law enforcement actions against companies using AI claims or tools to deceive consumers, including a case involving a service that generated fake reviews. The FTC's August 2024 final rule on fake reviews also covers fake reviews regardless of how they are produced. In February 2024 the FCC ruled that AI-generated voices in robocalls are "artificial" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, meaning such calls require the same consent as other prerecorded calls.

For an agency, the lessons are: do not exaggerate what your AI-assisted service can achieve; never generate reviews; and do not use AI voice tools for outbound calls without legal advice. See References.
:::

# Appendix B: Discovery Call Script

Use this as a guide, not a script to read aloud. Adapt the wording to your own voice.

**Opening (1 minute).** "Thanks for making time. I'd suggest we spend about 30 minutes: I'll ask some questions about the business and what you're hoping to change, you can ask me anything, and at the end we'll decide together whether a next step makes sense. If it doesn't look like a fit, I'll tell you, and I'll try to point you to someone better suited. Does that work?"

**Context.**

- "Tell me about the business. What do you do, and who is a typical customer?"
- "Where do most new customers come from today?"
- "What are your busy and slow periods?"

**Goal.**

- "What made you want to look at this now?"
- "If this went well, what would be different in six months?"
- "Roughly how many more of what kind of customer would make a real difference?"
- "What's a typical new customer worth to you over a year?"

**Current state.**

- "What have you tried so far? What worked, and what didn't?"
- "Who handles marketing now, and how much time does it take?"
- "Can we look at your profile or accounts together for a minute?"

**Constraints.**

- "Have you set aside a budget range for this? Our packages run from $[low] to $[high] a month."
- "Who else is involved in a decision like this?"
- "Is there anything that would stop you moving ahead, even if the proposal looked right?"

- If fit: "Based on what you've said, I think the [option] option is the best match. I'll send a short proposal with three options by [date]. Could we book 15 minutes on [date] to go through it?"

# Glossary

**Activity metric.** A count of work performed, such as posts published or reviews answered. Proves work happened; does not prove results.

**Attribution.** Assigning credit for a customer or sale to a particular marketing activity.

**Buyback Rate.** Dan Martell's delegation threshold: annual pay divided by 2,000 hours, divided by four.

**Camcorder Method.** Martell's technique of recording yourself doing a task and turning the recording into a written playbook.

**CAN-SPAM Act.** US law setting rules for commercial email, including accurate headers, a postal address, and a working opt-out.

**Core Four.** Alex Hormozi's four lead channels: warm outreach, posting content, cold outreach, and paid ads.

**Discovery call.** A structured first conversation to diagnose a prospect's situation and decide whether there is a fit.

**Fixed pricing.** Charging a set fee for a defined scope, regardless of hours.

**Floor price.** The lowest price at which serving a client covers its share of costs, owner pay, and margin.

**Gross margin.** Gross profit divided by revenue.

**Leading indicator.** A metric that signals response, such as calls or clicks, before outcomes such as sales are known.

**Lifetime value (LTV).** Monthly gross profit per client multiplied by average months retained.

**Material connection.** A relationship between an endorser and a seller (payment, free product, family tie) that could affect how an endorsement is weighed; must be disclosed under FTC guidance when the audience would not expect it.

**Pain line.** Martell's term for the point at which a founder's personal capacity can no longer fuel the business.

**Playbook.** A written, step-by-step procedure for a recurring task.

**Positioning statement.** One sentence stating whom you serve, with what, toward what outcome.

**Productized service.** A service sold as a named package with fixed scope, price, and process.

**Replacement Ladder.** Martell's sequence of roles to hand off: administrative assistant, delivery or operations, marketing, sales, leadership.

**Retainer.** A fixed monthly fee for ongoing work.

**Revenue per delivery hour.** Monthly revenue divided by hours spent on client delivery.

**Scope creep.** Gradual expansion of work beyond what was agreed, without a price change.

**TCPA.** The US Telephone Consumer Protection Act, which restricts autodialed and prerecorded calls and texts without consent.

**Value Equation.** Hormozi's model: value equals dream outcome times perceived likelihood, divided by time delay times effort and sacrifice.

**Value-based pricing.** Setting a fee by reference to the outcome's worth to the client rather than the cost of delivery.
