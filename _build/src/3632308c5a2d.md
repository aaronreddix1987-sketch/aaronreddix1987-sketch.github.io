# How to Use This Book

Authority marketing is the practice of earning a buyer's trust by teaching them something useful, showing how you know what you know, and making an appropriate next step easy to take. It is not a claim to be the best, the biggest, or the most famous. In its most durable form, authority is a reputation for being reliably helpful and honest about a specific problem. A plumber who explains why a water heater rumbles, an accountant who publishes what bookkeeping actually costs, and a consultant who writes a frank guide to choosing a consultant are all practicing authority marketing.

This book treats the subject the way a university course in marketing communication would, while staying grounded in the daily realities of small firms with limited time and budgets. It is written for owners of local service businesses, independent professionals, consultants, and small teams who need a manageable way to explain their work and attract buyers who are a good fit. It draws on published guidance from regulators and platforms and on publicly documented cases of companies and individuals who built authority by teaching.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this book you should be able to:

- Explain the components of trust and how authority differs from popularity.
- Select a buyer question your business can credibly own.
- Assemble a "proof shelf" of evidence that supports your claims.
- Write a definitive, people-first answer to a buyer's question.
- Design demonstrations that let buyers test your expertise.
- Turn one body of work into several useful formats without diluting it.
- Use speaking, podcasts, and guest content to reach new audiences.
- Collect and present reviews and testimonials lawfully and honestly.
- Shape an offer and next step that fit what your teaching has promised.
- Measure qualified contact rather than vanity metrics.
- Apply truth-in-advertising and endorsement rules to your marketing.
- Execute a 90-day plan that turns these ideas into a routine.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, explains its concepts, and gives a step-by-step framework. A worked example then applies the framework with numbers; worked examples are illustrative and hypothetical, and the businesses in them are invented for teaching. Case studies describe real, publicly documented companies, people, and enforcement actions, with sources listed in the References chapter. Where a real company or person is discussed, their results are their own, not TTI's, and are not a prediction of what any reader will achieve. Each chapter includes a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Appendices provide templates you can adapt.

# Chapter 1: The Foundations of Authority and Trust

:::objectives
- Define authority marketing and distinguish it from popularity and self-promotion.
- Identify the components of trust buyers use when evaluating a provider.
- Explain how search platforms describe the qualities of trustworthy content.
- Analyze how transparency built authority for an early search-marketing firm.
:::

## Authority versus popularity

Popularity measures how many people know about you. Authority measures how much the right people trust you on a particular subject. The two are related but distinct. A celebrity may be popular without being an authority on tax law; a respected structural engineer may be an authority known to only a few hundred architects. For a small business, authority in a narrow domain is both more achievable and more commercially useful than broad popularity, because buyers do not hire the most famous provider; they hire the one they trust to solve their problem.

Authority marketing therefore focuses on a defined audience and a defined set of problems. Its tools are teaching, evidence, and consistency. Its central bargain is that the business gives away genuinely useful knowledge, accepting that some readers will solve the problem themselves, in exchange for being the trusted choice when a reader does need help.

## The components of trust

Scholars of persuasion since Aristotle have observed that audiences weigh a speaker's character and competence alongside the argument itself. Contemporary marketing practice breaks trust into several practical components:

| Component | Buyer's question | How a business demonstrates it |
|---|---|---|
| Competence | Do they know how to solve this? | Clear explanations, worked examples, credentials |
| Experience | Have they actually done this? | First-hand detail, photos, documented projects |
| Honesty | Will they tell me the truth? | Discussing limits, prices, and downsides openly |
| Consistency | Will they show up reliably? | A regular publishing rhythm and steady quality |
| Fit | Are they right for someone like me? | Specific audience and clearly stated scope |
| Accountability | What if something goes wrong? | Transparent policies, contact details, licensing |

Notice that most of these components are demonstrated rather than claimed. Writing "we are honest" proves nothing; publishing your price ranges and the reasons a customer might not need you proves a great deal.

## How platforms describe trustworthy content

Search engines are, among other things, systems for estimating which sources deserve trust. Google's public guidance for creators states that its automated ranking systems are designed to prioritize helpful, reliable information created to benefit people rather than content created to manipulate search rankings [2]. The same guidance describes a set of qualities it calls E-E-A-T, experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness, and states that of these, trust is most important; Google also notes that E-E-A-T itself is not a specific ranking factor [2]. Its self-assessment questions ask, for example, whether content provides original information or analysis, whether it clearly demonstrates first-hand expertise, and whether a reader will leave feeling they have learned enough to achieve their goal [2].

For an authority marketer, the significance of this guidance is not that it reveals a ranking trick. It is that the qualities a large platform says it looks for are the same qualities a thoughtful buyer looks for. Content written to help people is aligned with both.

:::case Moz and transparency in a secretive industry
Rand Fishkin has described how the company that became Moz began in 2004 as SEOmoz.org, a site he built to host his thoughts, struggles, and discoveries while learning search engine optimization, at a time when his family's small web-design and consulting firm was in deep debt [4]. In a later interview, Fishkin recalled that the SEO industry was very secretive at the time, that this did not sit well with him, and that his mission became making something transparent, open, and accessible for everyone to learn SEO; he said speaking invitations beginning in 2005 led to clients that helped the company dig its way out of debt [5]. According to Fishkin's own history of the company, it was debt-free by July 2007, launched a subscription product in February 2007, and received $1.1 million in investment in November 2007 [4]. Moz's results are its own, not TTI's, and reflect a particular industry at a particular moment, but the case illustrates a durable principle: in a field where competitors hoard knowledge, openly teaching can become a distinctive position.
:::

## Lessons from the case

The Moz story shows authority emerging from a sustained practice of public teaching rather than from a single campaign. The blog was not initially a marketing plan; it was a record of learning. Transparency differentiated the company in an industry known for secrecy. And authority created several kinds of value, including speaking invitations, consulting clients, and eventually an audience for software, that the founder did not fully anticipate at the start.

## The authority foundation framework

**Step 1: Define your audience** precisely: who they are, what they are trying to accomplish, and what they fear.

**Step 2: Define your domain**: the specific problems on which you intend to be trusted.

**Step 3: Audit your trust components** using the table above. Which can you already demonstrate? Which are weak?

**Step 4: Identify what competitors will not say**, such as prices, downsides, or how to choose among providers.

**Step 5: Commit to a teaching rhythm** you can sustain for at least a year.

**Step 6: Decide what you will never claim**: guarantees, superlatives you cannot prove, or results that are not yours.

:::example Illustrative: a trust audit
A hypothetical two-person residential electrical contractor rates itself from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) on each trust component. Competence: 5 (licensed master electrician). Experience: 3 (many projects, few documented). Honesty: 2 (website lists no prices or limits). Consistency: 1 (no regular content). Fit: 2 (site claims to serve "everyone"). Accountability: 4 (license number and warranty posted). Total: 17 of 30. The owners decide to begin documenting projects with permission, publish typical price ranges for common jobs, and narrow their stated focus to older homes in two counties. Ratings and decisions are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choosing the Question You Can Own

:::objectives
- Explain why narrow focus builds authority faster than broad coverage.
- Generate and evaluate buyer questions systematically.
- Select a primary question and supporting questions.
- Analyze how a plain-language niche built an audience for a retailer.
:::

## The power of a narrow focus

A small business cannot be the authority on everything. It can, however, become the most useful source on a specific set of questions asked by a specific group of people. Narrow focus has three advantages. It makes the content more useful, because it can go deeper. It makes the business more memorable, because people associate it with one thing. And it makes the work manageable, because the team does not have to cover an entire industry.

## Where buyer questions come from

The best questions come from real conversations. Every sales call, service visit, support email, and complaint contains questions. Useful sources include:

- Questions prospects ask in the first five minutes of a sales conversation.
- Objections raised before buying and regrets expressed after.
- Search suggestions and "people also ask" boxes in search engines.
- Online forums and community groups where your buyers gather.
- Reviews of competitors, which reveal what buyers wished they had known.
- Staff who answer phones and emails.

## Evaluating questions

| Criterion | Question to ask | Score 1–5 |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | How often do buyers ask this? | ___ |
| Commercial relevance | Does answering it relate to what we sell? | ___ |
| Credibility | Can we answer it from real experience? | ___ |
| Gap | Are existing answers poor, evasive, or missing? | ___ |
| Stakes | Does a good answer save the buyer money, time, or risk? | ___ |

Questions that score high on frequency, relevance, credibility, and gap are the ones a business can own. Questions about price, problems, comparisons, and how to choose a provider often score highest, precisely because many businesses avoid answering them.

:::case Wine Library TV: making a category approachable
According to Gary Vaynerchuk's official biography, he and his father renamed the family's New Jersey liquor store Wine Library and launched one of the first e-commerce sites for alcohol in the country, and he grew the business from roughly $4 million to $60 million in annual sales during his tenure [6]. The biography states that in 2006 he started WineLibraryTV, one of the first long-form episodic video shows on YouTube, in which he reviewed and commented on wines sold in his father's store, and that he produced an episode almost every day for five years [6]. The biography attributes the growth to a combination of content, e-commerce, email marketing, and search advertising, not to video alone [6]. Wine Library's results are its own, not TTI's. The case is instructive because the show owned a clear question, "Which of these wines should I buy, and why?", answered in plain language for people who found traditional wine writing intimidating.
:::

## Lessons from the case

Wine Library TV narrowed a vast subject, wine, to a specific and recurring buyer question tied directly to products the store sold. It chose a voice that made the subject approachable to an underserved audience. And it demonstrated consistency on an unusual scale. Readers should also notice the biography's own caveat: content worked alongside e-commerce, email, and advertising. Authority marketing rarely works in isolation.

## The question selection framework

**Step 1: Collect** at least 50 real buyer questions from the sources listed above.

**Step 2: Group** them into themes (price, process, problems, comparisons, choosing a provider).

**Step 3: Score** each question with the evaluation table.

**Step 4: Select** one primary question with the highest total score.

**Step 5: Choose** five to ten supporting questions in the same theme.

**Step 6: Test** the primary question by answering it in a sales conversation and noting the buyer's reaction.

:::example Illustrative: scoring questions
A hypothetical independent HR consultant collects 64 questions from discovery calls. Three finalists: "Do I need an employee handbook if I have five employees?" (frequency 5, relevance 4, credibility 5, gap 4, stakes 4 = 22); "What is the best payroll software?" (4, 2, 3, 1, 3 = 13); "How do I fire someone without getting sued?" (4, 5, 4, 3, 5 = 21). She selects the handbook question as primary and the termination question as a supporting theme, avoiding the software question, where the gap is small and her experience limited. Scores are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 3: Building a Proof Shelf

:::objectives
- Define a proof shelf and the types of evidence it contains.
- Apply the FTC's substantiation principles to marketing claims.
- Organize evidence so it can be used quickly and honestly.
- Learn from an enforcement action involving selectively displayed reviews.
:::

## What a proof shelf is

A proof shelf is an organized collection of evidence that supports what you say about your knowledge and your work. It might be a folder, a spreadsheet, or a section of your website. It contains credentials, documented projects, data you have collected, permissions to use customer statements, and the outside sources you rely on. The shelf serves two purposes: it makes your teaching more credible, and it keeps your claims within what you can support.

## Types of evidence

| Evidence type | Examples | Strength and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Credentials | Licenses, certifications, degrees | Verifiable; state them precisely |
| Documented work | Before-and-after photos, project summaries | Strong; require customer permission |
| Original data | Your own counts, averages, timelines | Strong if method is explained |
| Outside sources | Government data, standards, research | Strong if current and cited accurately |
| Customer statements | Reviews, testimonials | Must be genuine, representative, and disclosed if incentivized |
| Third-party recognition | Awards, press coverage, invitations to speak | Useful; avoid implying more than occurred |

## Substantiation: the legal standard for claims

The FTC's guidance for small businesses states that under the FTC Act, advertising must be truthful and not deceptive, advertisers must have evidence to back up their claims, and ads cannot be unfair [3]. Before a company runs an ad, it must have a "reasonable basis" for its claims, meaning objective evidence; at a minimum, an advertiser must have the level of evidence it says it has [3]. The FTC adds that letters from satisfied customers usually are not sufficient to support claims that require objective evaluation, and that a money-back guarantee is not a substitute for substantiation [3]. The agency looks at both express claims and implied claims that a reasonable consumer would take from an ad [3].

For authority marketers, this means every number, comparison, and result in your content should trace back to an item on your proof shelf.

:::case Fashion Nova and the incomplete review shelf
In January 2022, the FTC announced that online retailer Fashion Nova would pay $4.2 million to settle allegations that it blocked negative reviews from appearing on its website [7]. According to the FTC, the company used a third-party review management interface to automatically post four- and five-star reviews while holding lower-rated reviews for approval, and from late 2015 until November 2019 it never approved or posted hundreds of thousands of lower-starred reviews [7]. The FTC described the case as its first involving a company's efforts to conceal negative customer reviews, and the settlement prohibited Fashion Nova from suppressing customer reviews and from misrepresenting reviews or other endorsements [7]. Fashion Nova's circumstances are its own; the company did not admit the allegations. The case shows that selective presentation of genuine evidence can itself be deceptive.
:::

## Lessons from the case

An honest proof shelf is complete as well as accurate. Showing only favorable evidence creates an impression that is not true, even when each piece is real. For a small business, the lesson extends beyond reviews: if you report that "most clients" achieved something, the claim must reflect all clients, not a selected subset; if you show a successful project, you must not imply it is typical unless it is.

## The proof shelf framework

**Step 1: Inventory** your credentials, licenses, and memberships, with numbers and expiration dates.

**Step 2: Document** completed work with customer permission (Appendix B).

**Step 3: Record** original data with a description of how it was collected.

**Step 4: Collect** outside sources with full citations and dates accessed.

**Step 5: Gather** customer statements through a lawful, unbiased process (Chapter 8).

**Step 6: Map** every claim in your marketing to a shelf item, and remove claims that lack support.

:::example Illustrative: mapping claims to evidence
A hypothetical bookkeeping firm reviews its homepage. Claim 1: "Certified QuickBooks ProAdvisors" (supported by certificates, filed with renewal dates). Claim 2: "Clients save an average of 10 hours a month" (no data; removed and replaced with a plan to survey clients). Claim 3: "Trusted by 40 local businesses" (client list shows 37 active clients; revised to "more than 35"). Claim 4: a five-star testimonial (permission on file; customer received no incentive). The revised page makes fewer claims, each supportable. Details are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Proof shelf
- Credentials listed with numbers and expiration dates.
- Project documentation has written customer permission.
- Original data includes a short method description.
- Outside sources cited with title, author, date, and URL.
- Customer statements are genuine and representative.
- Incentives for reviews or testimonials are recorded and disclosed.
- Every marketing claim maps to a shelf item.
:::

:::summary
A proof shelf organizes the evidence behind your claims. The FTC requires a reasonable basis for advertising claims before they run, and customer letters usually cannot substitute for objective evidence. The Fashion Nova case shows that presenting only favorable evidence can be deceptive, so a proof shelf must be complete as well as accurate.
:::

# Chapter 4: Writing the Definitive Answer

:::objectives
- Explain what makes an answer definitive and people-first.
- Structure a cornerstone article around a buyer's real question.
- Address price, problems, and limits candidly.
- Analyze how a pool company used candid answers to recover from a crisis.
:::

## What makes an answer definitive

A definitive answer is the one a reader would choose if they could read only one. It answers the question directly and early, explains the reasoning, covers the variations that matter, acknowledges what depends on circumstances, and tells the reader what to do next. Google's self-assessment questions describe similar qualities: content that provides a substantial, complete, or comprehensive description of the topic, insightful analysis beyond the obvious, and a descriptive, helpful title [2].

## The anatomy of a cornerstone answer

| Section | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Direct answer | State the answer in the first paragraph, including ranges or conditions |
| Why it varies | Explain the factors that change the answer |
| Worked example | Walk through a realistic scenario with numbers |
| Common mistakes | Describe what goes wrong and how to avoid it |
| When not to buy | Say honestly when the reader does not need you |
| How to choose | Give criteria for evaluating providers, including competitors |
| Next step | Offer a fitting, low-pressure way to continue |
| Sources and author | Cite evidence; state who wrote it and why they are qualified |

## Candor as a strategy

Many businesses avoid publishing prices, describing downsides, or explaining how to choose among providers, fearing that candor will drive buyers away. Yet these are often the questions buyers care about most. Answering them signals confidence and honesty, and it filters out poor-fit buyers before they consume a salesperson's time. Candor must be accurate: a price range should reflect real jobs, and a description of problems should be fair to the product.

:::case River Pools and the price question
The New York Times reported in 2013 that by early 2009, River Pools and Spas, a 20-employee installer of fiberglass pools in Virginia and Maryland, had seen orders fall from about six a month to barely two, and that the company overdrew its bank account three weeks running [8]. Co-owner Marcus Sheridan told the Times that the company had been spending about $250,000 a year on radio, television, and pay-per-click advertising, and that it cut this to about a tenth and focused on informational blog posts and videos [8]. The first question he answered was the one customers asked within minutes of every conversation: how much a fiberglass pool costs, a subject pool companies avoided online [8]. Sheridan told the Times, as quoted by Pool Pro magazine, that within about 24 hours the article ranked first for cost-related searches and that he had been able to track a minimum of $1.7 million in sales to it [9]. The Times reported that the company recovered to exceed its peak pre-2007 revenue [8]. River Pools' results are its own, not TTI's; they reflect a particular market and a period when few competitors answered such questions online.
:::

## Lessons from the case

River Pools chose the question buyers asked most and competitors avoided most. It answered with honest ranges rather than evasions. It used analytics to connect content to sales, rather than assuming. And it treated content as a replacement for a large portion of advertising spending, not an add-on. Readers should note that Sheridan's results were tracked and reported by him; they illustrate a method rather than prove what others will achieve.

## The definitive answer framework

**Step 1: Restate** the buyer's question in their words as the title.

**Step 2: Answer** directly in the first paragraph.

**Step 3: Explain** the factors that change the answer, with examples.

**Step 4: Include** limits, downsides, and when the reader should not buy.

**Step 5: Offer** criteria for choosing a provider, applied fairly.

**Step 6: Cite** sources, sign the piece, and add a fitting next step.

:::example Illustrative: a candid price article
A hypothetical roofing company writes "How Much Does a Roof Replacement Cost in Our County?" The first paragraph gives a range of $11,000 to $24,000 for typical single-family homes, based on its last 60 completed jobs. The article explains five factors (size, pitch, material, layers to remove, and decking repairs), works through a 1,800-square-foot example at $15,400, and includes a section titled "When a repair is enough." Over six months, 14 inquiries mention the article, and the sales team reports that these prospects arrive better informed. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Definitive answer review
- Title uses the buyer's own words.
- Direct answer appears in the first paragraph.
- Factors that change the answer are explained.
- A worked example uses realistic numbers from your records.
- Downsides and "when not to buy" are addressed.
- Criteria for choosing a provider are fair.
- Author, qualifications, and sources are stated.
- Next step is appropriate and low-pressure.
:::

:::summary
A definitive answer addresses the buyer's question directly, explains what changes the answer, covers limits and alternatives, and cites its sources. Candor about price and problems builds trust and filters poor-fit buyers. The River Pools case shows how answering the question competitors avoided, and measuring the results, helped a company recover, though its results are its own.
:::

# Chapter 5: Demonstrations Buyers Can Test

:::objectives
- Explain why demonstrations persuade more than descriptions.
- Design a demonstration that is honest, repeatable, and relevant.
- Choose demonstration formats suited to services as well as products.
- Analyze a product demonstration series that became a widely studied marketing case.
:::

## Show, do not tell

A demonstration lets the buyer see evidence with their own eyes. It converts an abstract claim ("our blenders are powerful," "our inspections are thorough") into something observable. Demonstrations are especially valuable for authority marketing because they reveal competence in action. They also carry a duty of honesty: a demonstration that is staged, edited to mislead, or unrepresentative is a deceptive claim.

## Demonstration formats

| Format | Best for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Live test | Physical products | Stress test, side-by-side comparison |
| Process walk-through | Services | Narrated inspection or audit |
| Before and after | Visible results | Renovation, cleanup, design |
| Diagnostic | Expertise | Explaining what a symptom means |
| Calculator or tool | Financial or technical services | Interactive estimate with stated assumptions |
| Teardown | Technical products | Showing internal quality |
| Sample deliverable | Professional services | Redacted report or plan, with permission |

## Making demonstrations honest

- Use ordinary units and conditions, not specially prepared ones, unless disclosed.
- Show the typical result, or clearly label an exceptional one.
- Avoid edits that remove failures or exaggerate speed.
- State assumptions behind any calculator or estimate.
- Obtain permission for any customer property, data, or likeness.

:::case Blendtec's "Will It Blend?" demonstrations
BYU Magazine reports that in 2006, Blendtec marketing director George Wright saw founder Tom Dickson testing blender durability by blending unusual objects, and spent about $50 on a lab coat, marbles, a rake, soda, a fast-food meal, and golf balls to film demonstrations; the series began in October 2006, Dickson recalled 6 million views in the first week, retail blender sales rose 700 percent within two years, and the campaign won a Clio Award in 2008 [10]. Wright told InformationWeek in 2007 that online sales had increased five-fold over the previous record and that a salesperson had closed a commercial contract by showing the videos [11]. Blendtec's results are its own, not TTI's. What matters for authority marketing is that every episode demonstrated the product's central claim, power and durability, in a way viewers could see for themselves.
:::

## Lessons from the case

The demonstration was simple, cheap, repeatable, and directly tied to the product's core benefit. It did not require the audience to trust a claim; it let them watch. It also fit the founder's real practice, since Dickson actually tested blenders this way. Service businesses can apply the same idea by filming or documenting the real work that proves competence: a thorough inspection, a clear diagnostic, or a careful process.

## The demonstration design framework

**Step 1: Identify** the claim buyers most doubt or care about.

**Step 2: Find** an observable test or process that proves it.

**Step 3: Design** the demonstration to be repeatable and fair.

**Step 4: Record** it honestly, including limits.

**Step 5: Explain** what viewers are seeing and why it matters.

**Step 6: Offer** a way for buyers to verify it themselves, where possible.

:::example Illustrative: a service demonstration
A hypothetical home-inspection firm wants to show thoroughness. It records a narrated 9-minute walk-through of its standard attic inspection on a volunteer's home (with written permission), showing the moisture meter readings, the insulation depth measurement, and the photo log that goes into every report. It publishes a redacted sample report alongside the video. Over a quarter, 22 percent of booked inspections cite the video or sample report. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::summary
Demonstrations turn claims into observable evidence and reveal competence in action. They must be honest, representative, and repeatable. # Chapter 6: One Body of Work, Many Formats

:::objectives
- Explain the logic of repurposing expert content.

- Analyze how a software company turned blog essays into a book, paperback, and conferences.

## Why repurpose

Creating original insight is the hardest part of authority marketing. Once that insight exists, presenting it in several formats lets it reach people who prefer different media and serves buyers at different stages. A detailed article might become a short video, a checklist, a talk, a workshop, a podcast episode, and eventually a chapter in a book. Each format should add value for its audience rather than merely copying text.

## Formats and their uses

| Format | Strength | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Long article | Depth, search visibility | Cornerstone answers |
| Short video | Personality, demonstration | Social and video platforms |
| Checklist or template | Practical use | Downloadable resource |
| Email series | Sequence and timing | Nurturing interested readers |
| Talk or webinar | Live interaction, credibility | Events, associations |
| Podcast episode | Long-form conversation | Commuters, listeners |
| Book or guide | Comprehensive authority | Paid product or premium resource |
| Workshop | Hands-on learning | Paid or partner events |

:::case 37signals and "Getting Real"
In March 2006, software company 37signals released "Getting Real," a 171-page PDF book priced at $19 that described the company's business, design, programming, and marketing principles, with four essays offered as free samples [12]. Co-founder Jason Fried reported 30 days later that the company had sold roughly 5,750 copies and earned about $120,000 in revenue [13]. In a 2008 post, Fried explained that the book was a bundle of the company's best blog posts about its software philosophy, and estimated that the same body of ideas had produced about $350,000 from the PDF, about $65,000 from a paperback, and about $250,000 from related conferences over a few years [14]. 37signals' results are its own, not TTI's; the company already had a large audience for its software and blog when it published.
:::

## Lessons from the case

The 37signals case illustrates a sequence: ideas developed in public as blog essays, then curated and organized into a book, then extended into print and live events. Each format served a different audience need. The company's existing audience was essential; the book did not create that audience from nothing. For a small business, the transferable lesson is to build a body of public work first and then package the best of it.

## The repurposing framework

**Step 1: Start** with one strong cornerstone piece.

**Step 2: Identify** two or three audiences or stages that would benefit from a different format.

**Step 3: Adapt** the content to each format's strengths, not just its length.

**Step 4: Update** facts and examples during adaptation.

**Step 5: Link** formats to each other so readers can go deeper.

**Step 6: Review** which formats produce qualified contact, and invest accordingly.

A hypothetical estate-planning attorney publishes "What Happens If You Die Without a Will in Our State." She adapts it into a 6-minute video, a one-page checklist of documents to gather, a 30-minute talk for a local library, a three-email series for people who download the checklist, and a section of a longer guide for new parents. Over a year, the library talk produces 9 consultations and the checklist 23 downloads per month. She invests more in community talks. Figures are illustrative; the attorney follows her state bar's advertising rules.

# Chapter 7: Speaking, Podcasts, and Earned Media

:::objectives

## Borrowed platforms

A business's own channels reach people who already know it. Speaking at events, appearing on podcasts, contributing guest articles, and being quoted by journalists reach audiences someone else has built. These "borrowed platforms" carry an implicit endorsement from the host, which is why they are valuable and why honesty about any payment or relationship is essential.

## Choosing platforms

| Platform | Reach | Credibility | Effort | Disclosure considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Local association talk | Small, targeted | High | Moderate | Disclose if you paid to sponsor |
| Industry conference | Medium | High | High | Disclose sponsored sessions |
| Podcast guest | Varies | Moderate to high | Low to moderate | Disclose if you paid for placement |
| Guest article | Varies | Depends on publication | Moderate | Paid content must be labeled |
| Media quote | Potentially large | High | Low | Avoid misrepresenting the coverage |
| Webinar with partner | Targeted | Moderate | Moderate | Disclose referral fees |

## Preparing a talk

A strong talk for authority purposes teaches one idea well. It follows a simple structure: a problem the audience recognizes, the insight that reframes it, evidence and stories, a practical framework, and a clear takeaway. Avoid turning the talk into a sales pitch; most hosts forbid it, and audiences resent it. Offer a resource at the end, such as a checklist, so interested listeners can continue.

:::case Brené Brown and a hometown talk
The Alcalde, the University of Texas alumni magazine, reported that in June 2010, University of Houston research professor Brené Brown gave a 20-minute talk titled "The Power of Vulnerability" at the first TEDxHouston, drawing on years of research and expecting an audience of about 500 people; four months later, on October 6, 2010, the talk was posted online and quickly spread [15]. The magazine reported that Brown had self-published her first book in 2004 because she could not get a literary agent, and that after the talk her next book, "Daring Greatly," reached the top of the New York Times best-seller list and her speaking events began selling out [15]. TED's website lists the talk with more than 71 million plays [16]. Brown's results are her own, not TTI's, and are extraordinary; the talk is among the most watched ever.
:::

## Lessons from the case

Three lessons transfer to ordinary practitioners. First, the talk rested on years of genuine research; the authority preceded the platform. Second, a small, local stage can be recorded and travel far beyond the room, so every talk deserves full preparation. Third, a body of work, in Brown's case books and research, was ready to serve the people the talk attracted. Most talks will reach dozens, not millions, but the same preparation applies.

## Disclosure on borrowed platforms

If you pay to appear, sponsor a session, or have a business relationship with the host, the audience should know. The FTC's 2016 Lord & Taylor case, discussed in Chapter 8, involved a paid magazine article and social posts presented without disclosure [17]. The FTC's influencer guidance says disclosures should be clear and placed where people will notice them [18].

## The borrowed platform framework

**Step 1: List** events, podcasts, and publications your buyers follow.

**Step 2: Prepare** one signature talk and two article topics from your cornerstone content.

**Step 3: Pitch** hosts with a specific, audience-centered idea.

**Step 4: Deliver** value without a sales pitch, and offer a resource.

**Step 5: Disclose** any payments or relationships.

**Step 6: Record** results: contacts, resource downloads, and follow-up conversations.

:::example Illustrative: a speaking season
A hypothetical commercial insurance broker prepares a talk titled "Five Gaps in Small-Business Insurance Policies." He pitches 12 local business associations and delivers 6 talks over a year to a total of about 240 attendees. He offers a policy-review checklist; 71 attendees request it and 11 schedule reviews. He discloses that he is a licensed broker who earns commissions. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist Talk and pitch

- Any payment, sponsorship, or referral relationship disclosed.

- Follow-up process ready for interested attendees.
:::

:::summary
Borrowed platforms such as talks, podcasts, guest articles, and media coverage extend authority to new audiences and carry an implicit endorsement, so honesty about relationships matters. A strong talk teaches one idea well and offers a resource. Brené Brown's TEDxHouston talk shows how authority built through research can travel far from a local stage, though her reach is exceptional.

# Chapter 8: Reviews, Testimonials, and Honest Disclosure

:::objectives
- Explain the FTC's principles for endorsements and testimonials.
- Design a lawful, unbiased process for requesting reviews.
- Disclose material connections clearly.
- Analyze an enforcement action involving undisclosed paid endorsements.
:::

## Why endorsements are regulated

Buyers give weight to what other people say because they assume those people are independent. When an endorser has a hidden relationship with the business, such as payment, free products, employment, or family ties, that assumption is false. The FTC's Endorsement Guides and its 2024 rule on consumer reviews exist to keep endorsements honest.

## Key principles

The FTC's endorsement FAQ explains that a material connection between an endorser and a business should be disclosed, that disclosures should be clear and hard to miss, and that a disclosure at the beginning of content is better than one at the end [1]. Its guidance for social media influencers says to disclose financial, employment, personal, or family relationships with a brand, and to avoid vague or easily missed terms [18]. The 2024 rule on reviews and testimonials prohibits fake reviews, including AI-generated ones; prohibits compensation conditioned on a particular sentiment, positive or negative; restricts undisclosed insider reviews; prohibits review suppression through threats or misrepresentation; and bans buying or selling fake social media indicators such as bot followers [19].

| Practice | Acceptable? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Asking all customers for an honest review | Yes | Ask everyone, not only happy customers |
| Offering a small thank-you for any review, positive or negative | Generally, with care | Must not be conditioned on sentiment; consider disclosure |
| Paying only for five-star reviews | No | Prohibited conditional incentive [19] |
| Employees reviewing their employer without disclosure | No | Insider reviews must disclose connection [19] |
| Writing reviews for customers | No | Fake or false testimonial [19] |
| Hiding negative reviews on your own site | No | Misrepresents the body of reviews [7][19] |
| Featuring a genuine testimonial with permission | Yes | Must be representative or clearly qualified |
| Buying followers or views | No | Fake social media indicators [19] |

:::case Lord & Taylor's undisclosed influencer campaign
In March 2016, the FTC announced a settlement with retailer Lord & Taylor over its 2015 Design Lab clothing launch [17]. According to the FTC, the company gave a paisley dress to 50 online fashion influencers and paid each between $1,000 and $4,000 to post a photo of themselves wearing it on Instagram over one weekend, required them to use the company's Instagram handle and campaign hashtag, but did not require them to disclose that they had been paid or given the dress, and none of the posts included such a disclosure [17]. The FTC also charged that the company paid for an article and an Instagram post in the online magazine Nylon without disclosure that they were paid promotions [17]. The settlement prohibited Lord & Taylor from misrepresenting that paid ads come from independent sources, required it to ensure influencers disclose compensation, and established a monitoring program for its endorsement campaigns [17]. The case shows that responsibility for disclosure rests with the business, not only with the endorser.
:::

## Lessons from the case

Lord & Taylor controlled important details of the posts, including the handle and hashtag, yet did not require a disclosure. The FTC treated that omission as the company's responsibility. Any business that gives free products, pays for posts, or has referral arrangements should build disclosure requirements into its agreements and check that endorsers comply.

## A lawful review process

**Step 1: Ask every customer**, at a consistent point after service, for an honest review.

**Step 2: Avoid conditions**: never tie incentives to positive sentiment, and consider whether any incentive is wise.

**Step 3: Record** any incentives, relationships, and permissions.

**Step 4: Publish** reviews completely on your own site, removing only content that is unlawful, obscene, or unrelated, under a written policy.

**Step 5: Respond** professionally to negative reviews, without threats.

**Step 6: Require disclosure** in writing from anyone you compensate.

:::example Illustrative: fixing a review program
A hypothetical dental practice discovers that its front desk asks only patients who seem happy to leave reviews, and that the office manager once posted a review under a personal account. The practice changes its process: every patient receives the same follow-up message two days after a visit asking for an honest review; the manager's review is removed; staff receive a one-page policy. Over six months, its average rating falls from 4.9 to 4.7 while the number of reviews rises from 62 to 140, giving prospective patients a more representative picture. Figures are illustrative; dental practices should also consider health-privacy rules when responding to reviews.
:::

:::checklist Endorsement compliance
- Review requests go to all customers, not a selected group.
- No incentives conditioned on positive sentiment.
- Staff and family reviews avoided or clearly disclosed.
- Written agreements require endorsers to disclose compensation.
- Disclosures are clear, early, and hard to miss.
- Negative reviews are not suppressed or threatened.
- No purchased followers, views, or engagement.
:::

:::summary
Endorsements are regulated because buyers assume endorsers are independent. The FTC requires clear disclosure of material connections, and its 2024 rule prohibits fake reviews, sentiment-conditioned incentives, undisclosed insider reviews, review suppression, and fake social indicators. The Lord & Taylor case shows that businesses are responsible for ensuring the people they pay disclose the relationship.
:::

# Chapter 9: Making the Offer Fit the Teaching

:::objectives
- Explain the relationship between educational content and commercial offers.
- Design next steps appropriate to each buying stage.
- Avoid bait-and-switch and pressure tactics.
- Analyze how an educational site added a subscription offer that fit its audience.
:::

## Teaching earns the right to offer

Authority content builds trust; an offer converts that trust into business. The two must fit. If an article teaches homeowners how to evaluate roof damage, a fitting offer is an inspection, not an unrelated product. If a guide explains how to choose an accountant, the firm's offer should match the criteria the guide recommends. A mismatch, or a bait-and-switch in which the content promises one thing and the offer delivers another, undermines the trust the content built.

## Offers by buying stage

| Stage | Buyer's mindset | Fitting next step |
|---|---|---|
| Learning | "What is going on?" | Related article, checklist, newsletter |
| Evaluating options | "What are my choices?" | Comparison guide, calculator, webinar |
| Choosing a provider | "Who should I hire?" | Consultation, assessment, sample deliverable |
| Ready to buy | "How do I start?" | Clear pricing, scheduling, proposal |
| After purchase | "Did I choose well?" | Onboarding guide, check-in, referral request |

## Principles of a fitting offer

- **Relevance**: the offer solves the problem the content discussed.
- **Proportion**: the commitment asked matches the trust built so far.
- **Clarity**: price, scope, and terms are stated plainly.
- **Honesty**: no fake scarcity, countdown timers that reset, or exaggerated claims.
- **Exit**: buyers can easily decline or unsubscribe.

:::case SEOmoz adds a subscription
In his published history of the company, Rand Fishkin wrote that by early 2007 consulting had been a growing source of revenue for SEOmoz, but the team decided it was not as scalable or far-reaching as a recurring revenue model, and in February 2007 it launched a PRO membership after three to four months of developing tools, resources, and guides, at an initial price of $29 a month [4]. In a separate interview, Fishkin explained that what made the shift work was that the blog had already attracted an audience that was not the core customer for consulting but was the core customer for a software product [5]. Moz's results are its own, not TTI's. The case shows how an offer can be designed around the audience that the teaching has actually attracted.
:::

## Lessons from the case

SEOmoz's audience consisted largely of people who wanted to learn and do SEO themselves, which made them poor candidates for consulting but natural subscribers to tools and guides. The offer fit the teaching. Small businesses should examine who their content actually attracts. If the audience is mostly do-it-yourselfers, an inexpensive guide, workshop, or tool may fit better than a high-touch service; if it is mostly decision-makers seeking help, a consultation may fit.

## The offer design framework

**Step 1: Analyze** who your content actually attracts, using inquiries and comments.

**Step 2: Map** each cornerstone piece to a buying stage.

**Step 3: Design** a fitting next step for each stage.

**Step 4: State** price, scope, and terms plainly.

**Step 5: Remove** pressure tactics and unsupported claims.

**Step 6: Test** and refine based on qualified contact.

:::example Illustrative: matching offer to audience
A hypothetical landscape designer finds that 70 percent of readers of her planting guides are homeowners who want to do the work themselves. Her only offer is a $4,000 full design service. She adds a $250 two-hour on-site consultation with a written planting plan. Over six months, 31 readers book consultations and 5 later hire her for full designs. Figures are illustrative.
:::

Cornerstone piece 2, stage, next step: ___
Cornerstone piece 3, stage, next step: ___

:::

:::summary
Offers must fit the teaching that preceded them, matching relevance, proportion, clarity, and honesty to the buyer's stage. SEOmoz's subscription launch shows how an offer designed for the audience the content actually attracted can succeed where a mismatched offer would struggle.

# Chapter 10: Measuring Qualified Contact

:::objectives
- Distinguish vanity metrics from meaningful measures.
- Define and track qualified contact.
- Allow realistic time for authority to build.
- Analyze how an educational video series took years to become a top performer.
:::

## Vanity versus meaning

Page views, followers, and likes are easy to count and easy to inflate, but they do not pay bills. Authority marketing should be judged by qualified contact: conversations with people who fit your audience, have the problem you solve, and could plausibly buy. Secondary measures, such as time on page, return visits, and resource downloads, help diagnose why content is or is not producing qualified contact.

| Metric | Type | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Qualified inquiries | Primary | Fit buyers starting a conversation |
| Consultations booked | Primary | Willingness to commit time |
| Sales attributed to content | Primary | Commercial value |
| Resource downloads | Secondary | Interest in going deeper |
| Return visitors | Secondary | Growing trust |
| Time on page | Diagnostic | Whether content is read |
| Page views and followers | Context only | Reach, not trust |

## Defining "qualified"

Write a short definition before you measure. For example: "a qualified inquiry comes from a business owner in our service area with 5 to 50 employees who describes a problem we solve." Then record how each inquiry found you. Simple methods include asking "How did you hear about us?" on forms and calls, using distinct links for major resources, and noting inquiries that mention specific articles or talks.

## Patience and time horizons

Authority accumulates slowly. Content published this month may produce inquiries years from now, and a body of work becomes more valuable as it grows. Measurement should therefore look at trends over quarters and years, not days.

:::case Whiteboard Friday's slow climb
In a podcast interview, Rand Fishkin recalled that Moz's Whiteboard Friday video series began almost accidentally around 2007 when a colleague filmed him explaining a concept, and that a major reason for continuing was that it saved him one night a week of blogging [20]. He said the early episodes were poor in quality, that for years they were the worst-performing posts on the blog, and that only around the fourth or fifth year did they begin to climb; by the time he left the company, he said, Whiteboard Friday was its top-performing content, and he credited the value of delivering an episodic format consistently and building a back catalog [20]. Moz's results are its own, not TTI's. The case illustrates why short measurement windows can lead businesses to abandon formats prematurely.
:::

## Lessons from the case

Had Moz judged Whiteboard Friday by its first year, it would have cancelled the series. Consistency, gradual quality improvement, and a growing archive changed the outcome. The lesson is not that every format will eventually succeed, but that formats should be evaluated over periods long enough for authority to accumulate, with clear diagnostic measures guiding improvement along the way.

## The measurement framework

**Step 1: Define** a qualified contact in one or two sentences.

**Step 2: Capture** source information on every inquiry.

**Step 3: Track** primary measures monthly and review trends quarterly.

**Step 4: Use** secondary and diagnostic measures to improve weak content.

**Step 5: Set** evaluation horizons of at least two to four quarters for new formats.

**Step 6: Report** honestly, including what is not working.

:::example Illustrative: a quarterly review
A hypothetical IT services firm reviews its first year. Page views grew from 800 to 5,200 a month, but qualified inquiries attributed to content rose only from 1 to 4 a month. Analysis shows most traffic goes to a general article about password managers that attracts consumers, not businesses. The firm updates its business-focused articles, adds a "Small Office Security Checklist," and sees qualified inquiries reach 7 a month two quarters later. Figures are illustrative.
:::

:::

:::summary
Authority marketing should be measured by qualified contact rather than vanity metrics. Define "qualified" in advance, capture sources on every inquiry, and review trends over quarters. The Whiteboard Friday case shows that formats may take years to mature, so evaluation horizons must be long enough for authority to accumulate.

# Chapter 11: Using AI and Automation Responsibly

:::objectives
- Identify appropriate and inappropriate uses of AI in authority content.
- Apply platform and regulatory guidance to AI-assisted work.
- Maintain first-hand experience and accountability in AI-assisted content.
- Establish an editorial review process.
:::

## The temptation and the risk

Generative AI tools can draft articles, summarize research, suggest outlines, and produce images in seconds. For a small business with limited time, the temptation is to publish large volumes of AI-generated content. Yet authority rests on first-hand experience, accuracy, and accountability, precisely the qualities that unreviewed machine output lacks. AI can be a useful assistant to an expert; it cannot substitute for one.

## What platforms and regulators say

Google's guidance on AI-generated content states that its ranking systems aim to reward original, high-quality content demonstrating E-E-A-T, however it is produced, and that using automation, including AI, to generate content primarily to manipulate search rankings violates its spam policies [21]. Google's broader guidance encourages creators to consider "Who, How, and Why" in relation to their content, including whether it is clear who created it and how automation was used [2]. The FTC's 2024 rule on reviews and testimonials specifically addresses fake reviews attributed to people who do not exist, such as AI-generated reviews, and prohibits businesses from creating, selling, or buying them [19].

| Use of AI | Generally appropriate? | Safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| Brainstorming questions and outlines | Yes | Validate against real buyer questions |
| Editing for clarity and grammar | Yes | Review meaning is preserved |
| Summarizing sources you have read | With care | Check every fact against the source |
| Drafting text an expert then rewrites | With care | Expert adds experience and verifies claims |
| Publishing unreviewed drafts at volume | No | Risks errors and spam-policy violations |
| Generating fake reviews or testimonials | No | Prohibited [19] |
| Inventing case studies or statistics | No | Deceptive; unsupported claims |
| Creating fictional author personas | No | Misrepresents who created the content |

:::case Regulators and platforms respond to AI-generated content
Two public documents show how institutions have responded to AI-generated marketing content. In February 2023, Google's Search Central blog stated that it rewards high-quality content "however it is produced," while warning that using AI to generate content primarily to manipulate rankings violates its spam policies [21]. In August 2024, the FTC announced a final rule, approved by a 5–0 vote, that prohibits fake or false consumer reviews and testimonials, including those attributed to people who do not exist, such as AI-generated fake reviews, and that allows the agency to seek civil penalties against knowing violators [19]. Together, these documents suggest a consistent principle: the method of production matters less than whether content is genuine, accurate, and created to help people rather than to deceive.
:::

## Lessons from the case

Neither Google nor the FTC prohibits AI tools as such. Both focus on purpose and truthfulness. An expert who uses AI to organize notes and then writes from experience is acting consistently with this guidance; a business that publishes hundreds of unreviewed articles to capture search traffic, or that generates testimonials, is not.

## The responsible AI framework

**Step 1: Decide** which tasks AI may assist with and which require a human expert.

**Step 2: Require** that every published piece be reviewed and approved by a named, qualified person.

**Step 3: Verify** every fact, figure, and citation against original sources.

**Step 4: Add** first-hand experience: examples, photos, and judgments only the expert can supply.

**Step 5: Disclose** AI use where readers would reasonably want to know.

**Step 6: Prohibit** AI-generated reviews, testimonials, case studies, and personas.

:::example Illustrative: an editorial policy in practice
A hypothetical financial-planning firm adopts a one-page AI policy. AI may suggest outlines and edit drafts; a credentialed planner must write the substance and approve each piece; every figure must link to a primary source; client stories require written permission and are never generated. In the first quarter, the planner rejects two AI-suggested statistics that could not be traced to any source. Details are illustrative; financial firms should also follow their regulators' advertising rules.
:::

:::checklist Responsible AI use
- Written policy defines permitted and prohibited AI tasks.
- Named expert reviews and approves every piece.
- Every fact and citation verified against the original source.
- First-hand experience added by the expert.
- No AI-generated reviews, testimonials, cases, or personas.
- AI use disclosed where readers would want to know.
:::

:::summary
AI can assist experts but cannot replace the first-hand experience and accountability that authority requires. Google rewards helpful content however produced but treats AI content created to manipulate rankings as spam, and the FTC's 2024 rule prohibits AI-generated fake reviews. A written policy, expert review, and fact verification keep AI-assisted work trustworthy.
:::

# Appendix B: Customer Permission and Testimonial Form

The following sample is provided for discussion with your attorney. It is not legal advice.

> I, [name], give [business name] permission to quote my statements and, if checked, use my name, photo, and project details in its marketing, including its website, social media, talks, and printed materials. I confirm that my statements are truthful and reflect my own experience. I [did / did not] receive any payment, discount, free product, or other incentive in connection with this statement: [describe]. I understand that I may withdraw permission for future uses by contacting [contact].
>
> ☐ Name ☐ Photo ☐ Project details ☐ Quote only, anonymous
>
> Signature: ________ Date: ________

:::checklist Testimonial use
- Permission signed before use.
- Statement genuine and unedited in meaning.
- Incentives recorded and disclosed.
- Results described as the customer's own, not typical, unless supported.
:::

# Glossary

**Authority.** Trust on a specific subject among a specific audience.

**Borrowed platform.** An audience built by someone else, such as an event, podcast, or publication.

**Cornerstone content.** A definitive, comprehensive answer to a central buyer question.

**Demonstration.** Content that lets buyers observe evidence of a claim directly.

**E-E-A-T.** Experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness; qualities Google describes in its guidance on helpful content.

**Endorsement.** A message from someone other than the advertiser that consumers are likely to believe reflects that person's opinion or experience.

**Fake social media indicators.** Followers, views, or engagement generated by bots or hijacked accounts.

**Material connection.** A relationship between an endorser and a business, such as payment, free products, employment, or family ties, that might affect the weight consumers give the endorsement.

**Proof shelf.** An organized collection of evidence supporting a business's claims.

**Qualified contact.** A conversation with a person who fits the audience, has the problem the business solves, and could plausibly buy.

**Reasonable basis.** The objective evidence an advertiser must have before making a claim.

**Repurposing.** Adapting one body of work into multiple formats for different audiences.

**Review suppression.** Preventing or hiding negative reviews through threats, filtering, or misrepresentation.

**Vanity metric.** A measure that is easy to count but weakly connected to business outcomes.
