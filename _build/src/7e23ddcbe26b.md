# How to Use This Book

Most business owners know that relationships matter. Referrals, partnerships, suppliers, advisors, first customers, and early warnings about market changes often arrive through people rather than through advertising. Yet many owners approach networking with either reluctance or a sales script. They attend an event, hand out cards, collect a few more, and wonder why nothing happens. Others avoid networking entirely because it feels transactional or uncomfortable.

This book takes a different approach. It treats networking as a disciplined, respectful practice grounded in more than fifty years of research on social networks. That research, from Mark Granovetter's work on weak ties to a 2022 experiment involving twenty million LinkedIn users, shows that the structure of your relationships, not just their number, shapes the information and opportunities you receive. It also shows that the most effective networkers tend to give generously, ask clearly, and follow up reliably, while respecting the time and boundaries of others.

## Who this book is for

The book is written for owners of small businesses, independent professionals, consultants, and early-stage founders who want a steady flow of useful conversations, introductions, and referrals without resorting to pressure, spam, or gimmicks. It assumes no prior training in sociology or sales. It is also useful for employees who represent their company in the community and for anyone re-entering the workforce who needs to rebuild a professional network.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this book you should be able to explain why weak and dormant ties carry new information; set a specific networking goal tied to your business; map your existing network and find its gaps; reconnect with dormant contacts in a natural way; choose events, groups, and online spaces that fit your goal; give help in ways that build trust without burning out; ask for conversations and introductions clearly and politely; hold a focused, useful meeting; follow up and stay in touch without becoming a nuisance; build referral partnerships and peer groups; network online within platform rules; and measure your practice so you can improve it.

## How each chapter works

Each chapter opens with learning objectives and closes with a summary and review questions. In between you will find an explanation of the relevant research, a step-by-step framework, a worked example, a table, and a checklist or worksheet. Worked examples are labeled illustrative: the people, businesses, and numbers in them are invented to demonstrate method. Case-study boxes describe real, publicly documented research, organizations, or individuals, with numbered citations to the References section. Their findings and results are their own, not Total Transformation Inc.'s, and they are not predictions of what you will achieve.

## How to use the worksheets

The worksheets are designed to be completed. Copy them into a notebook, spreadsheet, or contact-management tool so you can reuse them. Your first network map or outreach list will be incomplete; the value comes from returning to it each month. The appendices collect templates, scripts, and a 30-day plan in one place.

# Chapter 1: Why Networks Matter

:::objectives
- Explain Granovetter's distinction between strong and weak ties.
- Describe what the 2022 LinkedIn experiment found about tie strength and job mobility.
- Define brokerage and structural holes.
- Apply three research-based design principles to your own networking.
:::

Networking advice often begins with tactics: what to say, what to wear, how many cards to bring. This book begins with structure, because research shows that the shape of a network determines much of its value. Two owners can know the same number of people and receive very different information depending on who those people are and how they connect to one another.

## Strong and weak ties

In 1973 sociologist Mark Granovetter published "The Strength of Weak Ties," one of the most cited papers in the social sciences [1]. He defined the strength of a tie as a combination of the time people spend together, emotional intensity, intimacy, and reciprocal services [1]. Strong ties are close friends, family, and longtime colleagues. Weak ties are acquaintances: people you know but see occasionally or rarely.

Granovetter's key insight was that strong ties tend to cluster. Your close friends are likely to know one another, move in the same circles, and hear the same news. Weak ties, by contrast, often bridge to other clusters. An acquaintance from a trade association or a former customer lives in a different social world and therefore hears about opportunities you would never hear about otherwise [1]. Information that reaches you through weak ties is more likely to be new.

He illustrated the point with a study of professional, technical, and managerial workers who had recently changed jobs. Among those who found their job through a personal contact, only 16.7 percent said they saw that contact often at the time; 55.6 percent saw the contact occasionally and 27.8 percent rarely [1]. In other words, most useful job information came from people the job seeker did not see regularly.

## Testing weak ties at scale

For decades, Granovetter's idea was supported mainly by observational studies. In 2022 researchers from LinkedIn, MIT, Stanford, and Harvard published a large-scale test in the journal Science [2]. Over five years, LinkedIn's "People You May Know" algorithm was varied in ways that changed the mix of strong and weak ties recommended to about twenty million users. Across roughly two billion new ties and 600,000 new jobs, the researchers found that weak ties increased job mobility more than strong ties [2]. The relationship was not linear: moderately weak ties were the most beneficial, and the weakest ties were less useful [2]. The effect was also stronger in more digital industries [2].

For a business owner, the lesson is not that close relationships are unimportant. Strong ties provide trust, advice, and emotional support. The lesson is that a network made only of strong ties is likely to be an echo chamber, and that acquaintances you can reach with modest effort are a valuable source of new information.

## Brokerage and structural holes

Sociologist Ronald Burt extended this work with the concept of structural holes: gaps between groups that have little contact with each other [3]. People whose relationships span those gaps, whom Burt calls brokers, are exposed to different ways of thinking and behaving. In a study of managers in a large American electronics company, Burt found that compensation, positive performance evaluations, promotions, and good ideas were disproportionately held by people whose networks spanned structural holes; their ideas were more likely to be expressed, less likely to be dismissed, and more likely to be judged valuable [3].

Small business owners often sit at natural brokerage points. A bookkeeper who serves contractors and restaurants, a commercial cleaner who knows facility managers across several industries, or a consultant who works with both nonprofits and small manufacturers can see patterns and make introductions that people inside one group cannot.

:::case The LinkedIn weak-ties experiment
Rajkumar and colleagues analyzed data from multiple large-scale randomized experiments on LinkedIn's "People You May Know" feature, which affected about twenty million people over five years and produced roughly two billion new ties and 600,000 job changes [2]. Users who were recommended more weak ties experienced more job mobility, but the benefit peaked at moderately weak ties and declined for the weakest ones [2]. The findings are the researchers' own and concern job mobility, not sales or business results, and they are not TTI's. They do, however, provide unusually strong causal evidence that acquaintances carry valuable information.
:::

## Three design principles

The research supports three practical principles for networking.

1. Diversity over volume. A network that reaches several distinct groups is more valuable than a larger network concentrated in one.
2. Moderate ties deserve attention. People you know somewhat, such as former colleagues, past customers, or association members, are often more useful sources of new information than strangers or close friends.
3. Bridging creates value. When you connect people from different groups who can help one another, you create value for them and strengthen your position as a trusted broker.

| Tie type | Typical examples | Main value | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong | Family, close friends, business partner | Trust, support, candid advice | Overlapping information |
| Moderately weak | Former colleagues, past customers, association peers | New information, introductions | Needs occasional maintenance |
| Very weak | Someone met once, distant online connections | Occasional reach | Low trust, low response |
| Bridging | Contacts who span different industries or groups | Novel ideas, broker opportunities | Takes deliberate effort to build |

:::example Illustrative: an echo-chamber network
Marcus runs a small plumbing company. When he lists the twenty people he talks to most about business, he finds that fifteen are other tradespeople from the same union hall, and most of them know one another. When a property management company in a neighboring town starts outsourcing maintenance, Marcus hears about it three months late, after a competitor has signed the contract. A single acquaintance in property management would likely have told him sooner.
:::

# Chapter 2: Setting a Networking Goal

:::objectives
- Distinguish operational, personal, and strategic networking.
- Write a specific networking goal tied to a business outcome.
- Identify the types of people who can help you reach that goal.
- Set a realistic weekly time budget for networking.
:::

Networking without a goal tends to become socializing or collecting contacts. Both have their pleasures, but neither reliably produces business value. A goal focuses your choices: which events to attend, whom to contact, what to ask for, and what to offer.

## Three forms of networking

Herminia Ibarra and Mark Hunter studied thirty emerging leaders and identified three distinct but interdependent forms of networking [4]. Operational networking builds relationships with people you need to get current work done, such as staff, suppliers, and customers. Personal networking connects you with kindred spirits outside your organization through associations, alumni groups, and clubs, providing referrals, information, and developmental support. Strategic networking builds relationships that help you figure out future priorities and win support for them [4].

Ibarra and Hunter found that while their managers differed in how well they pursued operational and personal networking, almost all of them underused strategic networking [4]. Small business owners face a similar trap. They maintain operational relationships out of necessity and may join a local group for personal networking, but rarely invest in the relationships that would help them see where their market is heading.

| Form | Purpose | Typical contacts for a small business | Common gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operational | Get today's work done | Staff, suppliers, key customers, subcontractors | Neglected until something breaks |
| Personal | Learn, find referrals, grow | Peers, association members, mentors | Becomes purely social |
| Strategic | Anticipate change, win support | Industry leaders, adjacent businesses, investors, civic leaders | Rarely pursued deliberately |

## From vague to specific goals

A vague goal such as "network more" offers no guidance. A specific goal names an outcome, a group of people, and a time frame. Compare the following:

- Vague: "Meet more people."
- Better: "Build relationships with property managers."
- Specific: "Within 90 days, hold conversations with eight property managers in the county to learn how they choose maintenance vendors, and ask for one introduction from each."

The specific version tells you whom to find, what to learn, and what to ask for. It also allows you to measure progress.

## Four types of helpful contacts

Most business goals can be advanced by four kinds of people:

1. Decision makers: people who buy or influence purchases of what you sell.
2. Connectors: people who know many decision makers and enjoy introducing others, such as association directors, bankers, accountants, and attorneys.
3. Experts: people who know the market, regulations, or technology better than you.
4. Peers: other owners at a similar stage who can share experience and accountability.

A balanced plan includes some of each. Owners who focus only on decision makers often come across as salespeople; those who focus only on peers enjoy the conversations but may not advance their business.

## Setting a time budget

Networking competes with operations, sales, and family. A realistic budget for most small business owners is two to four hours per week: perhaps one event or group meeting, two or three one-on-one conversations, and thirty minutes of follow-up. Consistency matters more than intensity. A modest weekly rhythm sustained for a year builds far more relationships than an intense month followed by silence.

## Six steps to a networking goal

1. Name the business outcome you want in the next 90 days, such as new customers in a segment, a supplier, a partner, or market knowledge.
2. Identify which form of networking the outcome needs: operational, personal, strategic, or a mix.
3. Describe the people who can help: decision makers, connectors, experts, and peers.
4. Write a specific goal with a number, a group, and a time frame.
5. Set a weekly time budget and put it on your calendar.
6. Decide how you will measure progress, such as conversations held or introductions received.

:::case Ibarra and Hunter's emerging leaders
Ibarra and Hunter followed thirty managers making the transition to leadership roles [4]. They found that managers who thought of themselves as good networkers were often operating only at the operational or personal level, and that almost all of them underused strategic networking, the form most closely tied to anticipating future priorities [4]. The managers worked in established organizations, not small businesses, and their experiences are their own, not TTI's. The study nonetheless offers a useful diagnostic: if all your networking concerns current work or social connection, you may be missing the relationships that help you see what comes next.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a bookkeeper's goal
Lena runs a two-person bookkeeping practice. Her vague goal is "get more clients." After working through the six steps, she writes: "Within 90 days, hold one-on-one conversations with six business attorneys and four commercial bankers to learn what bookkeeping problems their small business clients face, and offer to be a resource. Budget: three hours per week, Tuesday and Thursday mornings." Her goal targets connectors rather than buyers and includes a clear offer.
:::

# Chapter 3: Mapping Your Existing Network

:::objectives
- Build a simple map of your current network.
- Identify clusters, connectors, and gaps.
- Apply the self-similarity and proximity principles to diagnose your network.
- Choose priority relationships to strengthen.
:::

Most owners already know more useful people than they realize. Before seeking new contacts, it pays to map existing ones. A map reveals clusters of people who know each other, connectors who link clusters, and gaps where your goal requires relationships you do not have.

## Why networks become clustered

Brian Uzzi and Shannon Dunlap observe that most personal networks are highly clustered: your friends are likely to be friends with each other [5]. Two forces drive this. The self-similarity principle means people tend to connect with others who share their background, views, and experiences. The proximity principle means people connect with those they see often, such as coworkers or neighbors [5]. Both feel natural, and both limit the diversity of information you receive.

Uzzi and Dunlap argue that the best way to break through these limits is the shared-activities principle: strong, diverse networks are forged through relatively high-stakes activities, such as volunteer boards, community projects, sports teams, or cross-functional work, that bring you into contact with different people around a common purpose [5]. People who work together on something meaningful get to know one another's character and competence in a way that a cocktail reception does not allow.

## Building a network map

A network map does not require software. A spreadsheet or a sheet of paper is enough. List the people you have had meaningful contact with in the past two to three years. For each, note how you know them, how often you are in touch, which group they belong to, and whether they know others on your list.

Then sort the list into groups or clusters, such as "customers," "trade association," "former employer," "neighborhood," or "church." Look for three things:

1. Clusters: groups where most people know each other. These provide trust but overlapping information.
2. Connectors: people who appear in more than one cluster or who know many people outside your list. Uzzi and Dunlap call people who link otherwise separate groups superconnectors or information brokers [5].
3. Gaps: groups relevant to your goal where you have no one, or only very weak ties.

## Tie strength and recency

Add two simple ratings for each person. Tie strength can be rated from 1 (met once) to 5 (close and trusted). Recency can be rated by when you last had a real conversation: within a month, within a year, or more than a year ago. People with moderate strength and low recency are candidates for reconnection, a topic covered in the next chapter.

| Column | What to record | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Person's name | Dana Ruiz |
| How we know each other | Origin of the relationship | Former coworker at supply company |
| Group or cluster | Main group | Building supply industry |
| Tie strength (1–5) | Closeness and trust | 3 |
| Last real conversation | Recency | About two years ago |
| Knows people in | Other groups they reach | Contractors, property managers |
| Relevance to goal | High, medium, low | High |
| Next step | Planned action | Coffee to catch up |

## Six steps to map your network

1. Export or list contacts from your phone, email, LinkedIn, and customer records.
2. Narrow the list to people you have had meaningful contact with in the past three years.
3. Assign each person to a group or cluster.
4. Rate tie strength and recency.
5. Mark connectors who reach multiple groups and note gaps relevant to your goal.
6. Choose ten to fifteen priority relationships to strengthen over the next 90 days.

:::case Paul Revere's network
Uzzi and Dunlap open their article with a historical comparison: on the night of April 18, 1775, both Paul Revere and William Dawes rode out to warn colonists that British troops were coming, but only Revere raised a militia and only his name became famous [5]. They attribute much of the difference to the type of social network each man had cultivated, with Revere connected across many different groups in Boston [5]. Historical accounts are always partial, and this example is the authors' illustration, not a TTI result. It captures the core idea of the chapter: a message travels further through a network that bridges many groups.
:::

:::example Illustrative: finding a hidden connector
When Priya maps the network for her small commercial cleaning company, she finds two dense clusters: her customers, mostly dental offices, and members of her neighborhood business association. One person appears in both: her accountant, who also serves several property management firms. Priya had never asked him about those firms. Her map identifies him as a connector to the group most relevant to her goal of winning office-building contracts.
:::

Most networks are clustered because of the self-similarity and proximity principles. A simple network map, with clusters, tie strength, recency, connectors, and gaps, shows where to invest your limited networking time.

# Chapter 4: Reactivating Dormant Ties

:::objectives
- Define dormant ties and explain why they can be valuable.
- Summarize research on reconnecting with former contacts.
- Recognize the comfort bias in choosing whom to reconnect with.
- Write a natural, low-pressure reconnection message.
:::

Every owner has dozens or hundreds of dormant ties: former colleagues, classmates, past customers, and old acquaintances with whom contact has lapsed. Many people feel awkward reaching out after years of silence and assume the relationship has lost its value. Research suggests the opposite.

## What the research shows

Daniel Levin, Jorge Walter, and Keith Murnighan asked executive MBA students to reconnect with people they had not communicated with for at least three years and to seek advice on an important work project [6][7]. In the study described in their MIT Sloan Management Review article, 224 executives each contacted two dormant ties [7]. The advice they received was as useful as, and often more useful than, advice from their current contacts, and reconnecting was efficient, requiring little time [7].

The researchers identified three reasons. First, dormant ties are sources of novel insight, because the other person has been learning and doing new things while out of touch. Second, reconnecting is efficient; conversations are often short but productive. Third, reconnecting is not like starting from scratch, because feelings of trust and shared perspective fade little, if at all [7][8]. Reconnecting previously strong ties produced benefits usually associated with both weak ties (novelty and efficiency) and strong ties (trust and shared perspective) [6].

## The comfort bias

A follow-up study asked 156 executives to seek advice from two dormant ties: their first choice and one selected randomly from their next nine choices [8]. Nearly all reconnections, 94 percent, produced at least some valuable advice [8]. The most valuable reconnections were with people who offered novelty, often higher-status contacts and people the executives had not spent much time with in the past, and engagement, meaning they were trustworthy and willing to help [8]. Yet the executives preferred contacts with whom they had spent a lot of time and found less intimidating, choosing comfort over value [8].

The practical lesson is to resist the urge to contact only the most familiar people. When choosing whom to reconnect with, include some people you knew less well and some who have since advanced or moved into different fields.

## Why reconnections fade

The same researchers found that although executives hoped to stay in touch after reconnecting, communication in the following year was typically infrequent [7]. A reconnection is an opportunity, not a relationship by itself. Chapter 9 covers how to stay in touch without becoming a burden.

## Five steps to reconnect

1. From your network map, list twenty people you have not spoken with in more than two years.
2. Mark those likely to offer novelty (different field, higher role, people you knew less well) and engagement (likely to be willing to help).
3. Choose five to contact this month, deliberately including at least two who feel slightly uncomfortable.
4. Send a short, honest message that names the connection, explains why you are reaching out now, and makes a small request or offer.
5. If they respond, have a 20- to 30-minute conversation focused on catching up and one specific question; then follow up with thanks.

## Writing the message

A reconnection message should be brief, specific, and free of pressure. Avoid pretending no time has passed and avoid opening with a sales pitch. A useful structure is: remind them how you know each other, mention something you remember or have noticed about their work, explain briefly what you are working on now, and make a small request.

For example: "Hi Dana, it has been a few years since we worked together at Westside Supply. I saw that you are now managing operations at a property management firm. Congratulations. I started a commercial cleaning business last year and I am trying to understand how property managers choose vendors. Would you have 20 minutes in the next few weeks to share your perspective? Either way, it would be good to catch up."

| Message element | Purpose | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| Shared history | Reactivates trust | "When we worked together at..." |
| Acknowledgment | Shows genuine interest | "I saw that you are now..." |
| Honest context | Explains the timing | "I started a business and..." |
| Small request | Easy to say yes to | "Would you have 20 minutes..." |
| Release | Removes pressure | "Either way, it would be good to catch up." |

:::case Dormant ties research
In Levin, Walter, and Murnighan's research, executives were prompted to contact people they had not communicated with for three years or more, in person or by phone, and to seek information or advice for an important work project [7]. The advice from these dormant ties was as useful as, and often more useful than, the advice from current relationships, and the conversations were efficient [7]. A later study of 156 executives found that 94 percent of reconnections provided at least some valuable advice, but that participants tended to choose comfortable contacts over the more valuable novel ones [8]. These are research findings about executives, not TTI client results, and individual outcomes vary.
:::

:::example Illustrative: overcoming the comfort bias
Tom, who runs a small IT support company, lists twenty dormant ties. His instinct is to call three old friends from his first job. Following the research, he also contacts a former manager who is now a regional director at a healthcare system and a classmate who became a commercial real estate broker. Both conversations feel awkward for the first minute, then turn useful: the broker mentions two building owners who are unhappy with their current IT vendor.
:::

# Chapter 5: Choosing Where to Connect

:::objectives
- Compare types of networking venues by fit and effort.
- Evaluate a group or event before committing time and money.
- Explain how structured referral groups work.
- Choose two or three venues that match your goal.
:::

Once you know your goal and have mapped your network, the next question is where to meet new people. The options are many: chambers of commerce, trade associations, referral groups, industry conferences, volunteer boards, alumni associations, online communities, and informal meetups. Each attracts different people and requires a different investment.

## Matching venues to goals

The best venue is the one where the people relevant to your goal gather and where you can participate repeatedly. One-time events can introduce you to people, but relationships usually require repeated contact. Uzzi and Dunlap's shared-activities principle suggests that venues where you work alongside others, such as committees, boards, and volunteer projects, build stronger and more diverse ties than events where people simply mingle [5].

| Venue type | Who you meet | Effort and cost | Strength | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chamber of commerce | Local owners, civic leaders | Moderate dues, monthly events | Broad local reach | Events can be mostly social |
| Trade or industry association | Peers, suppliers, experts | Dues, conferences | Deep industry knowledge | May be full of competitors |
| Customer's association | Decision makers in your target market | Dues, associate membership | Direct access to buyers | Must add value, not just sell |
| Structured referral group | Owners in noncompeting fields | Weekly meetings, dues | Steady referral exchange | High time commitment |
| Volunteer board or committee | Diverse community leaders | Time, sometimes donations | Strong trust through shared work | Requires real commitment |
| Online community | Peers and experts worldwide | Low cost, ongoing time | Reach and knowledge | Easy to lurk without connecting |
| Conferences | Industry leaders, prospects | High cost, travel | Concentrated access | Hard to follow up at scale |

## Evaluating before committing

Before joining a group, attend as a guest if possible. Ask yourself whether the people in the room include decision makers, connectors, experts, or peers relevant to your goal; whether members seem to know and help one another; whether the group meets often enough to build relationships; whether there is a way to contribute, such as a committee or speaking slot; and whether the cost in dues and time fits your budget.

It also helps to talk with two or three current members. Ask what they have gained, how long it took, and what they wish they had known when they joined. Their answers will tell you more than any brochure.

## How structured referral groups work

Structured referral groups meet regularly with a set agenda designed to help members learn about one another's businesses and pass referrals. Many limit membership to one person per profession, so members collaborate rather than compete. These groups can work well for local service businesses, but they require a significant and sustained time commitment, and the results reported by any group are not guarantees for an individual member.

:::case BNI
BNI was founded by Ivan Misner in 1985; its first meeting brought together about twenty people in a coffee shop in Arcadia, California [10]. The organization now reports about 355,000 members in roughly 11,800 chapters, and says its members generated more than 17.8 million referrals and more than $26.9 billion in member-generated business [9]. Chapters meet weekly with a structured agenda, and each chapter admits only one person from each profession [9]. Its core philosophy is called "Givers Gain" [10]. These figures are self-reported by BNI, describe totals across hundreds of thousands of members, and are not predictions of individual results or TTI's results. The model illustrates how structure, repetition, and a giving norm can make referral exchange routine.
:::

## Five steps to choose venues

1. List every venue where people relevant to your goal gather, using your network map and asking connectors for suggestions.
2. Rate each by fit with your goal, frequency of contact, opportunity to contribute, and cost.
3. Visit the top three or four as a guest.
4. Commit to two or three venues for at least six months, including at least one shared-activity venue.
5. Review after six months: which venues produced useful conversations and relationships, and which did not?

:::example Illustrative: choosing venues for a landscaping business
Carlos owns a landscaping company and wants more commercial clients. He lists the chamber of commerce, a regional association of community managers, a referral group, and the parks foundation board. He visits each. He joins the community managers' association as an associate member, volunteers on its education committee, and joins the parks foundation's planning committee, where he works alongside a city council member, a developer, and two business owners. He decides the referral group's weekly schedule does not fit his peak season.
:::

# Chapter 6: Giving Without Burning Out

:::objectives
- Describe Adam Grant's givers, takers, and matchers.
- Explain why givers appear at both the top and bottom of performance.
- Use the five-minute favor to give efficiently.
- Set boundaries that keep generosity sustainable.
:::

Many people dislike networking because it feels like taking: asking strangers for favors, pitching, or using people. Research suggests that the most effective networkers flip this pattern. They focus first on what they can give, and they do so in ways that are efficient and sustainable.

## Givers, takers, and matchers

Organizational psychologist Adam Grant describes three styles of reciprocity at work [11]. Givers contribute to others without seeking anything in return, offering assistance, sharing knowledge, or making introductions. Takers try to get other people to serve their ends while guarding their own expertise and time. Matchers aim for an even exchange [11]. Most people are matchers.

In his research, Grant found that givers go to both extremes: in data on salespeople, engineers, and medical students, givers were overrepresented at both the bottom and the top of performance [12]. The difference lies in how they give. Givers who sink to the bottom tend to give indiscriminately, say yes to everyone, and neglect their own goals. Givers who rise to the top set boundaries, give in ways that use their strengths, and are willing to ask for help themselves [11][12].

## The five-minute favor

Grant tells the story of Adam Rifkin, a serial entrepreneur whom Fortune named its best networker, and who practices what he calls the five-minute favor [12]. Rifkin's view is that you do not have to be a saint to be a giver; you just have to find small ways to add large value to other people's lives, such as introducing two people who could benefit from knowing each other, sharing knowledge, giving brief feedback, or recognizing someone whose work has gone unnoticed [12].

The five-minute favor solves a practical problem for busy owners. You cannot spend hours helping everyone, but you can routinely offer small, high-value help. Over time these favors build a reputation and a network of people who are glad to hear from you.

## Ways a small business owner can give

| Type of help | Example | Time required |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Connect a client with a trusted supplier | 5 minutes |
| Information | Share an article, regulation update, or vendor recommendation | 5 minutes |
| Feedback | Review a website, flyer, or pitch and offer three suggestions | 15–30 minutes |
| Recognition | Write a genuine public recommendation or thank-you | 10 minutes |
| Referral | Send a qualified customer to a partner | Varies |
| Expertise | Answer a question in your field | 5–15 minutes |
| Platform | Invite someone to speak at your group or appear in your newsletter | 15 minutes |

## Sustainable boundaries

Grant suggests that generous people can protect themselves by setting boundaries on when, how, and whom they help, and by learning to advocate for themselves as well as for others [11]. In practice, this might mean reserving a fixed block each week for favors, focusing help on areas where you have unusual expertise, saying no to requests that would take hours without clear purpose, and being wary of people who repeatedly take without reciprocating.

Grant also emphasizes that help seeking matters. He reports that somewhere between 75 and 90 percent of giving in organizations starts with a request [12]. Givers who never ask for help deprive others of the chance to contribute. Asking clearly, a topic covered in the next chapter, is part of a healthy giving culture.

## Six steps to a giving practice

1. List what you can offer easily: expertise, introductions, information, recognition, and referrals.
2. Reserve a weekly block of 30 to 60 minutes for favors.
3. In every networking conversation, listen for one thing you could help with.
4. Prefer five-minute favors; take on larger requests only when they fit your goals or values.
5. Track favors given and received in your contact list, not to keep score but to notice patterns.
6. Ask for help when you need it, and be specific.

:::case Adam Rifkin and the five-minute favor
In his TED talk, Adam Grant describes Adam Rifkin, a serial entrepreneur named Fortune's best networker, whose "secret weapon is the five-minute favor" [12]. Rifkin's approach is to find small ways to add large value, such as making introductions, sharing knowledge, or giving feedback [12]. Grant presents Rifkin as an example of how givers can set boundaries and protect themselves from burnout [12]. Rifkin's reputation and network are his own and reflect decades of work; they are not TTI results and not a prediction of what any reader will achieve.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a week of five-minute favors
Aisha, a freelance graphic designer, reserves Friday mornings for favors. In one week she introduces a bakery owner to a printer, sends a nonprofit director a link to a free grant-writing workshop, gives a new coffee shop three quick suggestions about its menu board, and writes a recommendation for a former client. Total time: about 45 minutes. None of these favors is a sales pitch, but two of the recipients later mention her to people who need design work.
:::

:::checklist Giving practice

- Reserved a weekly time block for favors.
- Listened for one way to help in each conversation.
- Kept most favors to five minutes or less.
- Declined requests that did not fit my goals or values.

:::

:::summary

The five-minute favor, illustrated by Adam Rifkin, makes generosity efficient. A weekly giving practice builds trust and reputation without exhausting your time.

# Chapter 7: Asking for Conversations and Introductions

:::objectives
- Write clear, specific requests that are easy to accept.
- Use the double opt-in introduction.
- Ask for introductions in ways that protect the connector's reputation.
- Handle declines and silence gracefully.
:::

Networking eventually requires asking: for a conversation, advice, an introduction, or a referral. Many owners avoid asking because they fear rejection or seeming pushy. Others ask vaguely, which makes it hard for people to help. A clear, specific, low-pressure request is both more respectful and more effective.

## Why weak ties will help

Granovetter's research shows that much useful information reaches people through contacts they see only occasionally or rarely [1]. Those contacts are often willing to help when asked, but they will not guess what you need. As noted in the previous chapter, Grant reports that most helping in organizations begins with a request [12]. A good request is therefore not an imposition; it is an invitation for someone to contribute.

## Elements of a good request

A good request is specific (what exactly you are asking for), bounded (how much time or effort it requires), relevant (why you are asking this person), and easy to decline (no pressure). Compare these two requests:

- Vague: "I would love to pick your brain sometime."
- Specific: "I am trying to understand how small manufacturers choose an IT provider. Since you have run operations at two plants, would you be willing to share your perspective in a 20-minute call next week? No pitch, just questions. If the timing does not work, no problem."

The second request tells the person exactly what is involved and why they were chosen.

## The double opt-in introduction

A double opt-in introduction asks both parties for permission before connecting them. When you want an introduction, you ask the connector, who then checks with the other person before making the connection. When you introduce others, you do the same. This practice protects everyone's time and reputation; no one is surprised by an unwanted connection.

To make it easy for a connector, provide a short forwardable note: two or three sentences about who you are, why you want to meet the person, and what you are asking for. The connector can forward it with a line of their own.

| Step | Who acts | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Ask | You | Ask the connector if they would be comfortable introducing you, and why |
| 2. Forwardable note | You | Send a short note the connector can pass along |
| 3. Check | Connector | Forwards the note and asks the other person if they are open to connecting |
| 4. Connect | Connector | If yes, introduces both parties by email or message |
| 5. Move | You | Reply promptly, thank the connector, and suggest times |
| 6. Close the loop | You | Tell the connector how the conversation went |

## Asking for referrals

Referral requests require extra care because the referrer is putting their reputation on the line. Make it easy by describing your ideal customer precisely, explaining the problem you solve in plain words, and suggesting a simple next step. Avoid asking people to "keep you in mind," which is too vague to act on. Instead, ask: "Do you know anyone who manages two or more rental buildings and has mentioned problems with maintenance response times?"

## Handling declines and silence

Not every request will be accepted, and many will receive no reply. Busy people miss messages. A single polite follow-up after a week or two is reasonable. If there is still no response, let it go and do not take it personally. Never send repeated follow-ups or messages designed to create guilt. Respecting a no, or silence, protects your reputation in a network where people talk to one another.

## Six steps to ask well

1. Decide exactly what you want: a conversation, advice, an introduction, or a referral.
2. Choose the person and note why they are relevant.
3. Write a request that is specific, bounded, relevant, and easy to decline.
4. For introductions, provide a short forwardable note.
5. Follow up once after one to two weeks if there is no response.
6. Thank everyone who helps and close the loop.

:::case Granovetter's job seekers
In his study of professional, technical, and managerial workers who found jobs through personal contacts, Granovetter found that most of those contacts were people the job seeker saw only occasionally (55.6 percent) or rarely (27.8 percent) [1]. Often the contact was someone marginally included in the current network, such as an old college friend or a former workmate [1]. The study concerned job changes in one U.S. metropolitan area in the late 1960s, and its findings are the researcher's, not TTI's. For networking, the implication is that clear requests to acquaintances can reach information that close friends do not have.
:::

:::example Illustrative: an introduction request
Jordan runs a small commercial HVAC company. He sees that a former customer, Maria, is connected to the facilities director of a regional school district. He writes: "Hi Maria, I hope the new building is working well. I noticed you know Sam Chen at the school district. I am trying to learn how districts plan HVAC maintenance budgets. Would you be comfortable asking Sam if he would be open to a 20-minute conversation? I have included a short note below you could forward. If it is not a good fit, no problem at all." Maria forwards the note, Sam agrees, and Jordan thanks Maria after the call.
:::

# Chapter 8: Holding a Useful Conversation

:::objectives
- Prepare for a networking conversation in 15 minutes.
- Use a simple agenda that balances learning, giving, and asking.
- Ask questions that produce useful information.
- End conversations with clear next steps.
:::

A conversation is the basic unit of networking. Whether it happens over coffee, on a video call, or at the edge of an event, a well-run conversation leaves both people better informed and glad they talked. A poorly run one feels like a pitch or an aimless chat.

## Preparation

Spend about fifteen minutes preparing. Review what you know about the person: their role, business, recent news, and any shared connections. Clarify your purpose: what do you hope to learn, and what might you offer? Prepare three or four questions. Decide in advance what you will ask for at the end, if anything, and be ready to skip the request if the conversation does not support it.

## A simple agenda

For a 20- to 30-minute conversation, a useful agenda has five parts:

1. Opening (2–3 minutes): thank them, restate the purpose, and confirm the time available.
2. Their story (8–10 minutes): ask about their work, priorities, and challenges.
3. Your story (3–5 minutes): explain briefly what you do and what you are working on.
4. Exchange (5–10 minutes): discuss the specific question, share ideas, and look for ways to help each other.
5. Close (2–3 minutes): summarize, agree on next steps, and thank them.

Letting the other person speak first and longest serves two purposes. It shows respect, and it gives you the information you need to offer relevant help.

## Asking good questions

Open-ended questions produce more useful information than yes-or-no questions. Questions about recent changes, priorities, and problems tend to surface opportunities. Questions about whom else to talk to extend your network.

| Purpose | Example question |
|---|---|
| Understand their work | "What does a typical week look like in your role right now?" |
| Surface priorities | "What are you focused on improving this year?" |
| Surface problems | "What part of your work takes more time than it should?" |
| Learn the market | "How have your customers' needs changed in the past two years?" |
| Find ways to help | "Is there anyone you are trying to meet or anything you are trying to find?" |
| Extend the network | "Who else would you suggest I talk to about this?" |

## Brokerage in conversation

Burt's research shows that people who bridge different groups are more likely to have ideas that others value [3]. In conversation, you can act as a broker by sharing what you have learned from other industries or groups and by suggesting connections. A comment such as "A restaurant owner I know solved a similar staffing problem by..." can be more useful to the other person than anything about your own business.

## Accessibility and boundaries

Make meetings easy to attend. Offer video or phone options, choose accessible locations, and respect the time you agreed to. End on time even if the conversation is going well; offer to continue later if both of you want to. If someone becomes pushy or inappropriate, you can end the meeting politely.

## Ending with next steps

Before ending, summarize what you heard and agree on any next steps: an introduction you will make, an article you will send, or a follow-up date. Write these down immediately after the conversation. A conversation without a recorded next step is easily forgotten by both people.

:::case Burt's brokers
In Burt's study of managers in a large electronics company, managers were asked to describe their best idea for improving the company's supply chain, and the ideas were then evaluated by senior executives [3]. Managers whose networks spanned structural holes were more likely to express ideas, less likely to have them dismissed, and more likely to have them judged valuable [3]. The study concerns managers inside one large firm, and its results are the researcher's own, not TTI's. For networking conversations, it suggests that sharing insights from other groups is itself a form of value.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a 25-minute coffee
Renee, who runs a small marketing agency, meets a credit union's community relations manager. She opens by thanking him and confirming they have 25 minutes. She asks about his priorities and learns the credit union wants to reach more small business owners. She briefly describes her agency, then mentions how a local chamber ran small business workshops with a bank. They agree she will introduce him to the chamber's program director and send a one-page summary of the workshop format. She records both next steps in her contact list before leaving the parking lot.
:::

:::checklist Conversation preparation
- Reviewed the person's role, business, and shared connections.
- Clarified what I hope to learn and might offer.
- Prepared three or four open-ended questions.
- Decided whether and what I will ask for.
- Confirmed time, place, and accessibility.
- Planned to record next steps immediately afterward.
:::

:::summary
Prepare for about fifteen minutes, use a five-part agenda, and let the other person speak first and longest. Open-ended questions about priorities, problems, and changes surface opportunities. Acting as a broker, sharing insights from other groups, adds value. End on time with clear, recorded next steps.
:::

# Chapter 9: Following Up and Staying in Touch

:::objectives
- Send effective follow-up messages within 48 hours.
- Build a simple contact system with review dates.
- Design a stay-in-touch rhythm by relationship priority.
- Avoid follow-up practices that damage trust.
:::

Most networking value is lost after the first conversation. People exchange contact information, intend to stay in touch, and then life intervenes. Research on reconnecting dormant ties found the same pattern: although executives hoped to stay in contact, communication in the following year was typically infrequent [7]. A simple system turns good intentions into reliable follow-through.

## The 48-hour follow-up

Within 48 hours of a conversation, send a short follow-up. Thank the person, mention one specific thing you appreciated, and complete any promised action, such as an introduction or a link. If they promised something, you can gently restate it, but do not press. Keep the message brief.

For example: "Thanks for your time yesterday. Your point about vendors who answer the phone stuck with me. As promised, here is the chamber program director's contact information; I have also let her know you might reach out. Good luck with the spring launch."

## A simple contact system

You do not need expensive software. A spreadsheet or basic customer relationship management tool can track each contact's name, organization, how you met, last contact date, notes, next action, and next review date. The key field is the next review date, which ensures you see the contact again at the right time.

## Stay-in-touch rhythm

Not every relationship needs the same attention. A simple tier system helps you allocate time.

| Tier | Who | Contact rhythm | Example touches |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Top 15–25 relationships relevant to your goal | Every 4–6 weeks | Coffee, call, introduction, useful article |
| B | Valuable but less central contacts | Every 2–3 months | Brief note, comment on their news, event invitation |
| C | Broader acquaintances | Once or twice a year | Holiday note, congratulations, newsletter if they opted in |

## Value-adding touches

Each contact should offer something: information, an introduction, congratulations, an invitation, or a genuine question. "Just checking in" messages add little. Watch for natural reasons to reach out: a promotion, a business anniversary, a news article about their industry, an event they might enjoy, or a resource related to something they mentioned.

## Practices that damage trust

Some follow-up practices undermine relationships. Avoid adding people to a marketing email list without their permission; commercial email in the United States is governed by the CAN-SPAM Act, which requires, among other things, accurate header information, a clear way to opt out, and prompt honoring of opt-out requests [17]. Avoid automated sequences that pretend to be personal, repeated follow-ups after no response, and messages that turn every conversation into a sales pitch.

## Six steps to a follow-up system

1. Choose a tool: spreadsheet or simple relationship manager.
2. Enter every new contact within 24 hours, with notes and next action.
3. Send a follow-up within 48 hours of each conversation.
4. Assign each contact to tier A, B, or C and set a review date.
5. Each week, review contacts due and send value-adding touches.
6. Each quarter, move contacts between tiers as your goals change.

:::case The fading reconnection
In the dormant-ties research, Levin, Walter, and Murnighan observed that although executives had high hopes for staying connected after reconnecting, communication during the following year was typically infrequent [7]. The finding comes from executive MBA participants and is the researchers' own, not a TTI result. It is a useful warning: the value of a conversation depends on what happens afterward, and without a system most relationships drift back into dormancy.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a weekly follow-up hour
Ben, who owns a small accounting firm, blocks Monday from 8:00 to 9:00 a.m. for follow-up. He opens his spreadsheet, filters for review dates in the current week, and sends six brief messages: congratulations on a promotion, an article about a tax change for a restaurant owner, an introduction between a client and a banker, and three event invitations. He updates the last-contact and next-review dates. The habit takes about an hour a week.
:::

# Chapter 10: Networking Online Within the Rules

:::objectives
- Choose online platforms based on where your audience is.
- Build a credible online presence for networking.
- Engage in online communities without spamming.
- Follow platform rules on invitations and automation.
:::

Online platforms extend your reach beyond local events. They allow you to reconnect with dormant ties, follow industry conversations, and meet people in other cities. They also make it easy to behave badly: mass invitations, copy-and-paste pitches, and automated messages that damage your reputation and can violate platform rules.

## Where people are

According to a 2025 Pew Research Center survey of more than 5,000 U.S. adults, 84 percent use YouTube, 71 percent use Facebook, and half use Instagram [14]. Professional platforms, industry forums, and private communities may matter more for specific goals. The right platform is the one where the people relevant to your goal are active and where you can contribute consistently.

## A credible presence

Before reaching out online, make sure your profile answers the questions a new contact will have: who you are, what you do, whom you help, and why you are credible. Use a clear photo, a plain-language headline, a short description of your work, and examples or recommendations where possible. Your profile is often the first thing a dormant tie or new acquaintance will check.

## Engaging in communities

Online communities reward contribution. Answer questions in your area of expertise, share useful resources, congratulate others, and comment thoughtfully on their posts. Avoid dropping links to your services in every conversation. Over time, consistent contribution makes you a recognized and trusted member, and people will begin to approach you.

## Following platform rules

Platforms set limits to protect users from spam. LinkedIn, for example, limits the number of invitations a user can send and may restrict accounts that send too many invitations or have many invitations ignored or marked as unknown [15]. LinkedIn also prohibits the use of third-party software, browser extensions, and bots that automate activity such as sending invitations or messages or that collect data from the site [16]. Read the current rules for any platform you use; violating them can lead to restricted or suspended accounts.

| Online practice | Effective and within the rules | Counterproductive or prohibited |
|---|---|---|
| Invitations | Personalized notes to people you have a reason to know | Mass invitations to strangers |
| Messaging | Specific, relevant, written by you | Copy-and-paste pitches or automated sequences |
| Content | Useful posts, thoughtful comments | Constant self-promotion |
| Groups | Answering questions, sharing resources | Posting ads in discussion threads |
| Tools | Platform's own features | Bots, scraping tools, automation extensions |

## Online to offline

Online relationships often deepen when they move to a call or meeting. After several useful exchanges, invite the person to a short video call or, if local, a coffee. Use the same conversation agenda as in Chapter 8.

## Six steps to network online

1. Identify the one or two platforms where people relevant to your goal are active.
2. Update your profile so it answers who you are, what you do, and whom you help.
3. Spend 15 minutes a few times a week contributing to conversations.
4. Send personalized invitations only to people you have a reason to connect with.
5. Move promising relationships to calls or meetings.
6. Review platform rules quarterly.

:::case Platform rules on automation
LinkedIn's help center states that it does not allow the use of third-party software or browser extensions that automate activity on its website, including bots that send invitations or messages [16]. It also limits the number of invitations users can send and may restrict accounts that send many invitations that go unaccepted [15]. These are LinkedIn's own policies, not TTI's, and they can change at any time. They illustrate a general point: platforms are designed to reward genuine engagement and to penalize automated volume.
:::

Nadia, an employment attorney, spends 15 minutes three times a week answering questions in an online community for small business owners. After she answers several questions about hiring rules, a restaurant owner sends her a personal message thanking her. They exchange a few messages, then schedule a 20-minute call. # Chapter 11: Referral Partnerships and Peer Groups

:::objectives
- Identify complementary businesses for referral partnerships.
- Structure a referral partnership fairly and transparently.
- Design or join a peer group for learning and accountability.
- Apply lessons from Franklin's Junto to modern peer groups.
:::

Some of the most valuable relationships for a small business are ongoing partnerships with complementary businesses and peer groups of fellow owners. Both turn occasional networking into a structured, recurring exchange.

## Complementary businesses

A complementary business serves the same customers as you but sells something different. A wedding photographer and a florist, a residential remodeler and a real estate agent, or a bookkeeper and a business attorney are natural partners. Because they do not compete, they can refer customers to each other confidently.

To identify partners, ask: Who serves my customers right before or right after I do? Whom do my customers ask me to recommend? Whose poor work makes my job harder, and whose good work makes it easier?

## Structuring a referral partnership

A referral partnership works best when expectations are clear. Discuss what kinds of customers each partner serves, how referrals will be made (double opt-in introductions are best), how you will communicate about outcomes, and how you will handle a referral that goes wrong. If any payment or fee is involved, be aware that some professions and industries restrict referral fees and that disclosure may be required; check the rules for your field and location, and consult an attorney when in doubt. Many partnerships work well with no fees at all, relying on mutual benefit.

| Partnership element | Questions to agree on |
|---|---|
| Ideal customers | Whom does each partner serve best? Whom should not be referred? |
| Referral method | How will introductions be made? Who follows up? |
| Communication | How and when will partners update each other? |
| Quality standards | What service standards must each partner meet? |
| Problems | What happens if a referred customer is unhappy? |
| Fees | Are any fees involved? Are they permitted and disclosed? |
| Review | How often will the partnership be reviewed? |

## Peer groups

A peer group is a small group of owners who meet regularly to share experience, solve problems, and hold one another accountable. Groups typically have six to twelve members, meet monthly or more often, follow a set agenda, and agree on confidentiality. Members often come from different industries so they can speak candidly.

## Six steps to start a peer group

1. Define the purpose: learning, accountability, problem-solving, or a mix.
2. Invite five to ten owners at a similar stage from noncompeting businesses.
3. Agree on meeting frequency, length, location, and confidentiality.
4. Use a consistent agenda, such as updates, one member's problem in depth, and commitments.
5. Rotate facilitation and the in-depth slot.
6. Review the group's value every six months and adjust membership or format.

:::case Benjamin Franklin's Junto
In his autobiography, Benjamin Franklin describes forming most of his "ingenious acquaintance into a club of mutual improvement," which he called the Junto, and which met on Friday evenings [13]. Members came from different trades and were expected to bring questions on morals, politics, or natural philosophy for discussion, with debates conducted in a spirit of inquiry rather than dispute [13]. Franklin credits the group with practical benefits; members helped one another, and the club's pooled books inspired the subscription library he later organized [13]. Franklin's account is his own, written decades later, and it is not a TTI result. The Junto remains a useful model for modern peer groups: diverse members, regular meetings, prepared questions, and norms of respectful discussion.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a referral partnership
Elena, a residential interior designer, and Raj, who owns a small cabinetry shop, agree to refer clients to each other. They write a one-page agreement: Elena refers clients who need custom cabinets; Raj refers clients who want design help; both use double opt-in introductions; they meet for coffee every quarter to review how referrals went; no fees are exchanged. # Chapter 12: Measuring and Sustaining Your Practice

:::objectives
- Track leading and lagging networking indicators.
- Run a monthly networking review.
- Recognize and correct common networking mistakes.
- Build a networking practice that lasts for years.
:::

Networking is a long-term practice, and its results are often delayed and indirect. An introduction made in March may lead to a client in October, through a person you met once. Measurement helps you see whether your practice is working and where to adjust, without turning relationships into transactions.

## Leading and lagging indicators

Lagging indicators are outcomes such as referrals received, partnerships formed, and revenue from referred customers. They matter, but they arrive slowly and are influenced by many factors. Leading indicators are activities you control, such as conversations held, favors given, follow-ups sent, and introductions made. Tracking both shows whether low results reflect too little activity or activity aimed at the wrong people.

| Indicator | Type | Example monthly target |
|---|---|---|
| One-on-one conversations | Leading | 6–10 |
| Reconnections with dormant ties | Leading | 3–5 |
| Five-minute favors given | Leading | 8–12 |
| Introductions made for others | Leading | 2–4 |
| Follow-ups sent within 48 hours | Leading | 100 percent of conversations |
| Introductions received | Lagging | Track, no target |
| Referrals received | Lagging | Track, no target |
| Referred customers won | Lagging | Track, no target |

Targets for lagging indicators are deliberately omitted: you can control your effort, not other people's decisions. The example targets for leading indicators are illustrative; set your own based on your time budget.

## The monthly review

Once a month, spend 30 minutes reviewing your networking. Count your leading and lagging indicators. Ask which conversations were most useful and why; which venues produced value and which did not; which tier A relationships need attention; whether your network is becoming more diverse; and what you will change next month.

## Common mistakes

Grant's research suggests that givers who fail tend to give indiscriminately and neglect their own goals [11][12]. Other common mistakes include networking only with peers who cannot help with your goal; collecting contacts without following up; pitching too early; neglecting existing customers, who are often the best source of referrals; and stopping when results are slow.

## Sustaining the practice

The most effective networkers make networking part of their routine rather than a campaign. They keep a weekly time budget, protect their favor block and follow-up hour, review monthly, and revisit their goals each quarter. They also accept that some relationships will never produce business and value them anyway. Over years, a consistent, generous practice builds a network that provides information, opportunities, and support when they are needed most.

## Six steps to sustain your practice

1. Choose five leading indicators and track them weekly.
2. Track lagging indicators without setting targets.
3. Hold a 30-minute monthly review.
4. Update your 90-day networking goal each quarter.
5. Refresh your network map twice a year.
6. Protect your weekly networking time on your calendar.

:::case Givers at the top and bottom
In his TED talk, Adam Grant reports that in data he gathered from hundreds of salespeople tracking their revenue, givers made up the majority of people with the lowest revenue but also appeared among those with the highest; the same patterns appeared for engineers' productivity and medical students' grades [12]. He argues that successful givers protect themselves from burnout and are willing to ask for help [12]. These are Grant's research findings, not TTI results, and they describe patterns across groups, not guarantees for individuals. For networking, they suggest that generosity works best when paired with boundaries and clear goals.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a monthly review
At the end of the month, Lena, the bookkeeper from Chapter 2, reviews her spreadsheet. She held seven conversations, reconnected with four dormant ties, gave eleven favors, and made three introductions. She received two introductions and one referral. She notices that conversations with attorneys were more productive than those with bankers, so she shifts next month's focus. She also sees that she has not contacted two tier A relationships in eight weeks and schedules calls.
:::

:::checklist Monthly networking review
- Counted leading indicators.
- Recorded lagging indicators.
- Identified the most useful conversations and why.
- Evaluated venues.
- Checked tier A relationships for overdue contact.
- Assessed network diversity.
- Chose one change for next month.
:::

:::summary
Track leading indicators you control and lagging indicators you do not. A monthly 30-minute review shows what is working and what to change. Common mistakes include indiscriminate giving, poor follow-up, pitching too early, and quitting when results are slow. A sustained weekly practice, reviewed monthly and refreshed quarterly, builds a network that provides value for years.
:::

# Appendix A: Message Templates

These templates are starting points. Personalize every message; never send them in bulk.

Reconnection with a dormant tie:

"Hi [Name], it has been a few years since we [shared history]. I saw that you are now [current role or news]. Congratulations. I am currently [brief honest context] and I am trying to learn about [topic]. Would you have 20 minutes in the next few weeks to share your perspective? Either way, it would be good to catch up."

Request for a conversation with a new contact:

"Hi [Name], [how you found them or shared connection]. I am [brief description] and I am trying to understand [specific question]. Given your experience with [relevant experience], would you be open to a 20-minute call in the next two weeks? No pitch, just questions. If the timing does not work, I understand."

Request for an introduction:

"Hi [Connector], I noticed you know [Person]. I am trying to learn about [topic], and [Person]'s experience with [area] would be very helpful. Would you be comfortable asking whether they would be open to a short conversation? I have included a short note below you could forward. If it is not a good fit, no problem at all."

Forwardable note:

"[Your name] runs [business], which [what you do] for [whom]. They are trying to learn about [topic] and would value 20 minutes of your perspective on [specific question]. No sales pitch."

Follow-up within 48 hours:

"Thanks for your time [day]. I especially appreciated [specific point]. As promised, [completed action]. [Optional: brief next step]. Good luck with [something they mentioned]."

Single follow-up after no response:

"Hi [Name], I wanted to follow up once on my note below in case it got buried. If now is not a good time, no problem; I will not follow up again."

Closing the loop with a connector:

"Thanks again for introducing me to [Person]. We talked on [day] and [brief outcome]. I appreciate your help, and please let me know if I can return the favor."

# Appendix C: Conversation Guide

Before the conversation:

- Person's role, business, and recent news.
- Shared connections.
- What I hope to learn.
- What I might offer.
- Three or four questions.
- What I will ask for, if anything.

During the conversation (20–30 minutes):

1. Opening: thanks, purpose, time check.
2. Their story: work, priorities, challenges.
3. My story: brief and relevant.
4. Exchange: specific question, ideas, ways to help.
5. Close: summary, next steps, thanks.

After the conversation:

- Record notes and next steps immediately.
- Send follow-up within 48 hours.
- Complete promised actions.
- Set next review date.

# Glossary

**Broker.** A person whose relationships span groups that are otherwise unconnected [3].

**Comfort bias.** The tendency to choose familiar, unintimidating contacts over more valuable novel ones when reconnecting [8].

**Complementary business.** A business that serves the same customers with a different, noncompeting offering.

**Connector.** A person who knows many people across groups and enjoys introducing others; Uzzi and Dunlap use the term superconnector [5].

**Dormant tie.** A former contact with whom communication has lapsed, often for years [6].

**Double opt-in introduction.** An introduction made only after both parties have agreed to be connected.

**Five-minute favor.** A small act of help, such as an introduction or brief feedback, that takes little time but adds value [12].

**Giver, taker, matcher.** Grant's three reciprocity styles: contributing without expecting return, seeking to get more than one gives, and seeking an even exchange [11].

**Lagging indicator.** An outcome measure, such as referrals received, that arrives after the activities that influence it.

**Leading indicator.** An activity measure you control, such as conversations held or favors given.

**Operational, personal, and strategic networking.** Ibarra and Hunter's three forms of networking: for current work, for personal development and referrals, and for future priorities [4].

**Peer group.** A small group of owners who meet regularly for learning and accountability.

**Proximity principle.** The tendency to form ties with people one sees often [5].

**Self-similarity principle.** The tendency to form ties with people who are similar to oneself [5].

**Shared-activities principle.** The idea that diverse, strong ties form through meaningful joint activities [5].

**Strong tie.** A close relationship characterized by frequent contact, emotional intensity, intimacy, and reciprocity [1].

**Structural hole.** A gap between groups with little contact; bridging it creates brokerage opportunities [3].

**Weak tie.** An acquaintance relationship with infrequent contact; often a bridge to new information [1].
