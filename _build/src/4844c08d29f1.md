# How to Use This Book

Every small business that takes phone calls misses some of them. The plumber is under a sink, the hairstylist is mid-cut, the dental front desk is checking in a patient, and the owner of a cleaning company is driving between jobs. A missed call is not a failure in itself. What matters is what happens next: whether the caller hears a useful greeting, whether someone calls or texts back while the need is still fresh, whether the follow-up is lawful and respectful, and whether the owner can see, week by week, how many callers were helped and how many slipped away.

This book is a practical text on that sequence. It is written for owners, office managers, and front-desk staff in service businesses: trades, home services, salons, clinics, studios, repair shops, and professional offices. It treats the missed call as a small operational system with five parts: triage, greeting, callback, follow-up, and measurement. Each chapter takes one part of that system, explains the reasoning behind it, and then gives you tools you can use the same day.

The book differs from a general guide to answering technology. It does not ask you to buy software. Most of the tools here work with a phone, a printed tracker, and a calendar. Where automation helps, such as an automatic text sent after a missed call, the book explains the consent rules and limits that apply, because a fast reply that breaks the law or annoys a customer is not an improvement.

## How the chapters are built

Each chapter follows the same pattern so that you can find what you need quickly. It begins with learning objectives and key definitions. It then explains the concept, offers a numbered framework, and works through an illustrative example with real arithmetic. Every chapter includes one real, cited case study drawn from public sources, such as court decisions, government records, published research, or reputable reporting. Each chapter closes with a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. The appendices collect the printable tools: voicemail greetings, intake cards, reply scripts, callback trackers, weekly count sheets, consent and opt-out logs, an escalation card, and a 30-day setup plan.

## The running example

The worked examples follow one fictional business, Oakline Heating and Air, a residential heating and air-conditioning company in Raleigh, North Carolina. The owner, Denise Carter, runs three technicians and employs a part-time office manager named Marcus. The company's average repair ticket is $380, and it occasionally sells a full system replacement averaging $8,500. Oakline, Denise, and Marcus are illustrative only. They are not TTI customers, and their numbers are not benchmarks, forecasts, or promises. They exist so that each method can be shown with concrete arithmetic you can replace with your own figures.

:::callout Scope and limits
This book is educational. It is not legal, tax, or financial advice, and it does not promise any number of calls, bookings, or revenue. Case studies describe the documented experience of the named organizations; their results are theirs, not TTI's, and they do not predict what any reader will achieve. Laws on calling, texting, recording, and privacy vary by state and change over time. The descriptions of federal rules here are summaries as of 2026; confirm current requirements with official sources or a qualified attorney before you rely on them, especially before sending automated or marketing texts.

# Chapter 1: Why Missed Calls Matter

:::objectives
- Explain why the time between a missed call and a reply matters.
- Distinguish a missed call, a lost caller, and an unreturned caller.
- Estimate what unreturned calls may cost your business, using your own numbers.
- Describe what published research does and does not show about response speed.
:::

A ringing phone in a service business is usually a person with a problem and a deadline. The furnace stopped on a cold night; a tooth is aching; a pipe is leaking into the ceiling; a wedding is next Saturday and the dress needs altering. When no one answers, the caller rarely waits. Many call the next business on the list. Some leave a message and hope. A few give up. The owner who treats missed calls as a nuisance to clear at the end of the day is, in practice, deciding that some of those people will be served by someone else.

## Key definitions

A missed call is any inbound call that is not answered by a person or a working system while the caller is on the line. A lost caller is a person who called, was not helped, and did business elsewhere or abandoned the need. An unreturned caller is a person who called, was missed, and never received a reply. Speed to lead is the elapsed time between the moment a prospective customer reaches out and the moment the business makes a meaningful reply. A callback is a return call made by the business to a person who called and was missed. Text-back is a text message sent to a caller after a missed call, either manually or automatically.

## What the research shows

The best-known research on response speed comes from an article in Harvard Business Review by James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington. The authors audited 2,241 U.S. companies by submitting a web-generated test lead and timing the response. They found that 37 percent responded within an hour, 16 percent within one to 24 hours, 24 percent took more than 24 hours, and 23 percent never responded at all; among companies that responded within 30 days, the average response time was 42 hours [1]. In a separate study of 1.25 million sales leads received by 42 U.S. companies, firms that tried to contact a prospect within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead, defined as having a meaningful conversation with a key decision maker, as firms that tried even an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited 24 hours or longer [1].

Three cautions apply. First, the research measured web leads, not missed phone calls, although the underlying idea, that interest fades quickly, is plausible for callers too. Second, "qualifying" a lead meant reaching a decision maker for a meaningful conversation, not closing a sale. Third, the study is observational; faster firms may differ from slower firms in other ways. The responsible reading is that speed matters and that many businesses are slow, not that any specific reply time will produce a specific result.

## The three ways a caller is lost

A caller can be lost at three points. The first is the greeting: a caller who reaches a full mailbox, a generic carrier message, or a greeting that does not say what to do next may hang up and call a competitor. The second is the gap: a caller who leaves a message but hears nothing for hours or days may book elsewhere. The third is the reply itself: a callback that is rushed, confusing, or pushy may lose someone who was ready to book. Each of the following chapters addresses one of these points.

|---|---|---|---|
| Greeting | Full mailbox or unclear message | Short, specific greeting with a promised reply time | 3 |
| Triage | Urgent caller waits behind routine calls | A simple priority rule | 2 |
| Gap | Long silence after leaving a message | Callback blocks and text-back with consent | 4, 5 |

## A framework for sizing the problem

1. Count all inbound calls for two typical weeks, using your phone system's call log.
2. Count how many were missed, meaning not answered live.
3. Remove spam, robocalls, wrong numbers, and vendors to find real missed callers.
4. For each real missed caller, record whether they received a reply and how long it took.
5. Estimate how many of those callers were potential new jobs rather than existing customers.
6. Multiply potential jobs by a realistic booking rate and your average ticket to estimate the value at stake.
7. Treat the estimate as a range, not a forecast, and revisit it after four weeks of tracking.

:::example Oakline's two-week call count (illustrative)
Marcus exports Oakline's call log for two weeks in January. There were 410 inbound calls. Of these, 96 were missed, or 96 ÷ 410 = 23.4 percent. After removing 31 spam calls and robocalls, 6 wrong numbers, and 7 vendor calls, 52 real callers were missed. Marcus finds that 34 received a callback the same day, 9 received a callback the next day, and 9 never received one. Of the 52, he estimates 30 were potential new jobs. Denise's records show that when Oakline reaches a new caller the same day, about half book a visit. If the 9 never-returned callers included 5 potential new jobs, and half of those would have booked at the average ticket of $380, the value at stake over two weeks is roughly 5 × 0.5 × $380 = $950, or about $24,700 over 52 weeks if the pattern held. Denise treats this as a rough range, because one replacement sale ($8,500) would change the number substantially, and because some of those callers may have booked later on their own.
:::

:::case The Short Life of Online Sales Leads (real, cited)
In the March 2011 issue of Harvard Business Review, James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington reported two related studies of response speed [1]. In the first, they submitted test inquiries through the websites of 2,241 U.S. companies and timed the replies: 37 percent responded within an hour, 23 percent never responded, and the average response among companies that replied within 30 days was 42 hours [1]. In the second, an analysis of 1.25 million leads received by 29 business-to-consumer and 13 business-to-business companies, firms that attempted contact within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that attempted contact an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited 24 hours or longer [1]. These findings describe the companies studied, not TTI or its customers, and they concern web inquiries rather than phone calls. They are useful here for one reason: they show that slow replies were common even among established companies, and that the first hour was associated with much better odds of a real conversation.

# Chapter 2: Triage in Thirty Seconds

:::objectives
- Sort missed calls into priority groups quickly and consistently.
- Recognize spam, robocalls, and spoofed numbers without wasting time.
- Decide what to return first when time is short.
- Protect callers with urgent safety issues.
:::

Not every missed call deserves the same response. A customer whose heat is out in freezing weather needs a reply before someone asking about next spring's maintenance plan. A silent robocall needs no reply at all. Triage is the habit of deciding, in a few seconds, which group a missed call belongs to and what to do about it. Without triage, a busy owner either returns calls in the order they arrived, which can leave urgent callers waiting, or returns none until the end of the day.

## Key definitions

Triage is the sorting of calls by urgency and type so that the most important are handled first. A robocall is a call made with an autodialer or a prerecorded or artificial voice. Caller ID spoofing is the practice of falsifying the number that appears on caller ID. Call labeling is a carrier's practice of marking calls as "spam likely" or similar. An urgent call involves an active problem, a safety risk, or a time-sensitive appointment. A routine call involves information, scheduling, billing, or vendors.

## Three buckets

Most small businesses can work with three buckets. Bucket A is urgent: active customers with a problem today, safety or property concerns, or a same-day appointment at risk. Bucket B is new business: callers asking about availability, pricing, service area, or booking. Bucket C is routine and noise: billing questions, suppliers, sales pitches, and robocalls. The rule is simple: return A immediately at the next safe pause, return B within the next scheduled callback block, and batch C at the end of the day or ignore it if it is spam.

One rule overrides all others: a caller describing a safety emergency, such as a gas smell, smoke, a medical emergency, or flooding near electrical equipment, should be told to call 911 or the relevant utility emergency line. Your greeting should say so, and your callback script should repeat it. A small business should not try to manage an emergency by text.

## Recognizing noise

Unwanted calls, including illegal and spoofed robocalls, are the FCC's top consumer complaint, and the agency advises consumers not to answer calls from unknown numbers and to hang up if a caller asks for money or personal information [2]. That advice cuts both ways for a small business. Your own callers may be wary of answering an unfamiliar number, which is one reason a text or a voicemail that names your business can help. At the same time, some of your missed calls will be robocalls, and spending time returning them wastes the minutes you need for real callers.

Carriers now help. The FCC requires voice service providers to implement the STIR/SHAKEN caller ID authentication framework in their internet-protocol networks, which allows the receiving carrier to verify that a call really comes from the number displayed and supports blocking and labeling decisions [3]. Under the TRACED Act, Congress directed the FCC to mandate that framework, and the Commission required implementation by June 30, 2021 [4]. In practice, a call labeled "spam likely" by your carrier, with no voicemail, can usually go straight to Bucket C.

| Signal | Likely bucket | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Existing customer, voicemail says "no heat" or "leak" | A | Call back at next safe pause |
| Message mentions gas, smoke, sparks, or injury | A, emergency | Call back and direct to 911 or utility |
| New number, voicemail asks about price or availability | B | Return in next callback block |
| New number, no voicemail, local area code | B | Text or call in next block |
| Supplier or billing | C | Batch at end of day |
| Labeled "spam likely," no voicemail | C | No return needed |
| Prerecorded voicemail offering a loan or warranty | C | Delete; do not call back |

## A framework for triage

1. Write the three buckets and their rules on one card next to every phone.
2. Check missed calls at fixed times, such as every hour or after each job.
3. Listen to or read every voicemail before deciding on a bucket.
4. Return Bucket A at the next safe pause, even if it interrupts other work.
5. Return Bucket B in the next scheduled callback block.
6. Batch Bucket C at the end of the day; delete obvious spam without calling back.
7. Record the bucket on the daily tracker, so that patterns become visible.
8. Review misclassified calls weekly and adjust the card.

:::case STIR/SHAKEN and the fight against spoofed robocalls (real, cited)
Caller ID spoofing lets scammers display a false number, often a local one, to persuade people to answer. The FCC's response has been to require caller ID authentication. In 2020, the Commission adopted rules requiring voice service providers to implement the STIR/SHAKEN framework in the internet-protocol portions of their networks by June 30, 2021, and it has since expanded the obligation to gateway and certain intermediate providers [3]. STIR/SHAKEN allows calls traveling through IP networks to have their caller ID "signed" as legitimate by the originating carrier and validated by other carriers before reaching consumers, and it supports carriers' blocking and labeling efforts [3]. The TRACED Act directed the FCC to mandate the framework as part of a broader effort against illegal robocalls [4]. These facts describe a federal program, not TTI's work. For a small business, the case has two practical lessons: carrier labels can help you sort noise from real callers, and your own legitimate calls can be mislabeled, so a recognizable business name in your voicemail and text matters.
:::

:::example Oakline's triage card (illustrative)
Marcus reviews the 52 real missed callers from Chapter 1. Eight were Bucket A (no heat or no cooling for an existing customer), 30 were Bucket B (new inquiries), and 14 were Bucket C (billing, suppliers, warranty questions). Under the old habit of returning calls in order at the end of the day, the 8 urgent callers waited an average of 3.5 hours. With a triage card and hourly checks, Denise estimates that urgent callers would wait no more than 60 minutes, and new inquiries no more than 2 hours. The 31 spam calls that had previously cost Marcus about 2 minutes each to listen to and dismiss now take about 15 seconds each, saving roughly 31 × (120 − 15) ÷ 60 = 54 minutes over two weeks.
:::

Triage sorts missed calls into urgent, new business, and routine or noise, so that the most important callers are helped first. Safety emergencies should always be directed to 911 or the relevant utility. Unwanted robocalls are the FCC's top consumer complaint, and carrier caller ID authentication under STIR/SHAKEN can help identify noise, though legitimate calls can be mislabeled. A one-card triage rule, checked at fixed times, is enough for most small businesses.

# Chapter 3: Voicemail That Works

:::objectives
- Write a short voicemail greeting that tells callers what to do and when to expect a reply.
- Apply plain-language principles to greetings and messages.
- Create separate greetings for business hours, field work, and after hours.
- Test greetings for clarity and length.
:::

The voicemail greeting is often the first thing a new customer hears from your business. Many businesses leave the carrier's default greeting, which says only that the person is unavailable, or record a long message with every detail of the business's history. Neither helps the caller. A good greeting answers three questions in about 20 seconds: Did I reach the right business? What should I do now? When will I hear back?

## Key definitions

A voicemail greeting is the recorded message a caller hears when a call is not answered. A promised reply time is a specific time frame stated in the greeting, such as "within two business hours." Plain language is writing or speech that the intended audience can understand the first time they read or hear it. A call to action is a specific instruction, such as "leave your name, address, and what is going on."

## Plain language

The federal government offers a useful model. The Plain Writing Act of 2010, signed on October 13, 2010, requires federal agencies to write covered documents in plain language, and guidance from the Office of Management and Budget describes plain writing as "concise, simple, meaningful, and well-organized," avoiding "jargon, redundancy, ambiguity, and obscurity" [5]. The government's plain language guide says the first rule is to write for your audience and recommends active voice, short sentences, lists, and organizing information so readers know what to expect [6]. These principles apply directly to a voicemail greeting, which a caller hears once, often while stressed.

## What a greeting should include

A strong greeting includes the business name, a one-line acknowledgment, a clear instruction, a promised reply time, and, where relevant, an emergency instruction. It should not include a long menu, a sales pitch, or a promise you cannot keep. If you say you will call back within an hour, you must have a routine that makes that true.

| Greeting element | Purpose | Example wording |
|---|---|---|
| Business name | Confirms the right number | "You've reached Oakline Heating and Air." |
| Acknowledgment | Shows the call matters | "Sorry we missed you; we're out on jobs." |
| Instruction | Tells the caller what to leave | "Please leave your name, address, and what's going on." |
| Reply time | Sets an honest expectation | "We return calls within two business hours." |
| Text option | Offers a faster channel | "You can also text this number." |
| Emergency line | Protects safety | "If you smell gas, leave the house and call 911." |

## Three greetings

Most businesses need three greetings. The business-hours greeting is used when the office is open but the call was not answered. The field greeting is used when the owner or crew is on jobs and calls are checked between appointments. The after-hours greeting is used when the business is closed and sets expectations for the next business day, or explains how to reach an on-call technician if one exists. Each should be about 20 seconds, or roughly 50 to 60 spoken words. Appendix A provides templates.

## A framework for greetings

1. Write a draft that includes the six elements in the table.
2. Cut every word that does not help the caller act.
3. Read it aloud and time it; aim for 20 seconds.
4. Make sure the promised reply time matches your callback routine.
5. Record it in a quiet place, at a normal pace.
6. Call your own number from another phone and listen.
7. Ask two people outside the business what they would do after hearing it.
8. Record separate greetings for business hours, field work, and after hours.

:::case The Plain Writing Act of 2010 (real, cited)
On October 13, 2010, President Obama signed the Plain Writing Act, which was designed to promote "clear Government communication that the public can understand and use" [5]. The Office of Management and Budget's initial guidance required that, by October 13, 2011, agencies write in plain language any documents necessary for obtaining a federal benefit or service, documents that provide information about such benefits or services, and documents that explain how to comply with federal requirements; it described plain writing as "concise, simple, meaningful, and well-organized" and warned that "a lack of clarity may prevent people from becoming sufficiently aware of programs or services" [5]. The government's plain language guide adds that plain language does not mean "dumbing down," but writing for the audience: using active voice, short sentences, lists, and clear organization [6]. These facts describe a federal law and its guidance, not TTI. The lesson for a small business is direct: if the federal government found that unclear communication discouraged people from using its services, an unclear voicemail greeting can discourage callers from using yours.
:::

:::example Oakline's greeting rewrite (illustrative)
Oakline's old greeting ran 48 seconds and opened with the company's founding year, its service area in eight towns, and a list of brands it services, before asking callers to "leave a detailed message." Marcus counts 131 words. He rewrites it: "You've reached Oakline Heating and Air. Sorry we missed you; our team is out on jobs. Please leave your name, address, and what's going on, and we'll call you back within two business hours. You can also text this number. If you smell gas, leave the house and call 911." The new greeting has 52 words and runs about 19 seconds. Over the next two weeks, the share of missed callers who leave a voicemail rises from 21 of 52 (40 percent) to 29 of 50 (58 percent). Denise notes that other factors, such as weather, could have contributed, and keeps tracking.

# Chapter 4: The Callback Conversation

:::objectives
- Structure a callback so that the caller feels heard and the next step is clear.
- Use a simple script without sounding scripted.
- Handle common situations: voicemail, wrong timing, price questions, and no answer.
- Balance speed with the quality of the conversation.
:::

Speed gets you to the conversation; the conversation decides what happens next. A callback that opens with a rushed apology and a list of questions can feel like an interrogation. A callback that opens with the caller's name, a reference to what they said, and a clear offer of help feels like service. This chapter gives you a structure that works for most service businesses and explains how to adapt it.

## Key definitions

A callback script is a written outline of what to say when returning a missed call. An opener is the first 10 to 15 seconds of the call, in which you identify yourself and the reason for calling. Discovery is the part of the call in which you learn what the caller needs. A next step is a specific, agreed action, such as a booked appointment, a quote visit, or a promised follow-up. A callback attempt is one try to reach the caller, whether or not they answer.

## The five-part callback

A good callback has five parts. The opener names you, your business, and the reason for the call: "Hi, this is Marcus from Oakline Heating and Air, returning your call from about twenty minutes ago." The acknowledgment refers to what the caller said, if they left a message: "You mentioned the upstairs unit isn't cooling." Discovery asks a few focused questions: what is happening, since when, the address, and any access details. The offer proposes a specific next step: "We have a technician available tomorrow between 8 and 10, or Thursday afternoon." The close confirms what happens next and how the caller will hear from you.

A script is a guide, not a recitation. Its purpose is to make sure nothing important is missed, such as the address or the safety question, while leaving room for the caller to speak. Staff should practice until they can deliver the parts naturally, in their own words.

## When the caller does not answer

Many callbacks reach voicemail. Leave a short message: your name, business, the reason, and how to reach you, plus the next time you will try. If you have the caller's consent to text, or if the caller texted you first, a short text after the voicemail can help, as Chapter 5 explains. Limit attempts to a reasonable number, such as three over two days, and record each one on the tracker. Repeated calls to someone who does not respond can feel like pressure, and if the purpose shifts toward marketing, calling rules apply (Chapter 6).

## Price questions

Callers often ask about price first. A vague answer, such as "it depends," can end the call. A better answer gives a range or a fixed diagnostic fee and explains what is included: "Our diagnostic visit is $89, and if you go ahead with the repair, we apply that toward the cost. Most repairs like this run between $250 and $600." Be honest; never quote a price you will not honor.

| Callback part | Goal | Typical length | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opener | Identify yourself and reason | 10 to 15 seconds | Opening with a long apology |
| Acknowledgment | Show you heard them | 5 to 10 seconds | Asking them to repeat everything |
| Discovery | Learn the need and details | 1 to 3 minutes | Too many questions |
| Offer | Propose specific options | 30 seconds | "When works for you?" with no options |
| Close | Confirm next step | 15 to 30 seconds | Ending without confirming |

## A framework for callbacks

1. Before calling, read the voicemail or text and the tracker notes.
2. Open with your name, business, and reason within 15 seconds.
3. Acknowledge what the caller said.
4. Ask no more than five discovery questions, including the safety question for urgent issues.
5. Offer two specific options for the next step.
6. Answer price questions with a fixed fee or honest range.
7. Confirm the next step and how the caller will hear from you.
8. Record the outcome on the tracker immediately.

:::case Zappos and the record-length call (real, cited)
Online retailer Zappos built a reputation for customer service in which representatives were encouraged to stay on the phone as long as a customer needed. In December 2012, a Zappos spokesperson told The Huffington Post that the company had set a record for its longest customer service call, at 10 hours and 29 minutes; the representative spent much of the call talking with the caller about life in the Las Vegas area, and the conversation eventually led to the sale of a pair of boots [8]. In 2016, Business Insider reported that a Zappos employee, Steven Weinstein, broke the record with a call lasting 10 hours and 43 minutes, which began when a customer needed help with an order of a few items and continued after the issue was resolved [7]. These are Zappos's documented experiences, not TTI's, and a small service business cannot and should not spend 10 hours on a callback. The case is useful as an extreme illustration of a principle: when a company measures success by the quality of the conversation rather than its brevity, staff behave differently. A callback script should make room for listening, not just for speed.
:::

:::example Oakline's callback math (illustrative)
Marcus tracks 50 callbacks to new callers over two weeks. Under his old approach, which started with "What can I do for you?", the average call lasted 2.5 minutes and 19 of 50 callers (38 percent) booked a visit. Using the five-part script, the average call lasts 4 minutes, and 26 of 50 (52 percent) book. The extra time is 50 × 1.5 = 75 minutes over two weeks. The extra bookings are 7, and at the average ticket of $380, that is 7 × $380 = $2,660 in potential revenue. With a gross margin of about 50 percent, the contribution is roughly $1,330 for 75 extra minutes. Denise recognizes that seasonal demand can affect booking rates and plans to compare the same weeks next year.
:::

# Chapter 5: Text-Back and Consent

:::objectives
- Explain when a text after a missed call is appropriate.
- Distinguish informational and marketing texts and the consent each requires.
- Honor opt-out requests promptly and in any reasonable form.
- Write text-back messages that are clear, short, and lawful.
:::

Many callers prefer text. A short text after a missed call, such as "Sorry we missed your call; this is Oakline Heating and Air. How can we help?", can start a conversation when the caller is in a meeting or cannot talk. Many phone systems can send such a text automatically. But text messages are covered by federal rules, and mistakes can be costly. This chapter explains the basics so that you can use text-back responsibly.

## Key definitions

Text-back is a text message sent to a caller after a missed call. An informational text relates to a transaction or request the person initiated, such as replying to their call or confirming an appointment, and contains no advertising. A marketing text promotes a product, service, or offer. Prior express consent is permission given by the person to be contacted at that number. Prior express written consent is a signed agreement, which may be electronic, that clearly authorizes marketing calls or texts using automated technology. Opt-out, or revocation of consent, is a person's request to stop receiving messages.

## What the federal rules say

The FCC explains that robocalls include calls and text messages made using an autodialer or containing a prerecorded or artificial voice, and that these are subject to its rules implementing the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) [9]. According to the FCC, its rules require consumer consent for texts sent using an autodialer, unless the message is for emergency purposes or an exemption applies; autodialed texts that contain telemarketing require prior written consent, while for informational texts consent may be oral [9]. The FCC also notes that its rules apply even if the mobile number is not on the National Do Not Call Registry [9].

What counts as an autodialer has been litigated, as Chapter 9 explains, and whether a particular text-back tool falls within the rules can depend on how it works. A cautious small business treats any automated text as potentially covered: it sends only informational replies to people who called it, keeps marketing out of those replies, and obtains clear written consent before sending any promotional texts.

## Opt-outs

The FCC adopted rules in 2024 to make it easier to revoke consent. Under its order, a person may revoke consent "in any reasonable manner," replies such as "stop," "quit," "end," "revoke," "opt out," "cancel," or "unsubscribe" count as a reasonable way to revoke, callers must honor revocation requests within a reasonable time not to exceed 10 business days, and a sender may send a one-time text confirming the request [12]. Most of these requirements took effect on April 11, 2025; the FCC delayed until April 11, 2026 the part of the rule requiring that a revocation in response to one type of message apply to all future robocalls and robotexts from that caller on unrelated matters [13]. A simple practice satisfies the spirit of the rules: if anyone asks you to stop texting, in any words, stop, record it, and do not text them again except for one confirmation.

| Type of text | Example | Consent needed (summary) | Practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reply to a missed call | "Sorry we missed you. How can we help?" | Caller initiated contact; keep it informational | Send once; no offers |
| Appointment confirmation | "Your visit is Tuesday 8 to 10." | Consent, which may be oral | Ask "Is it OK to text you about your appointment?" |
| Promotion | "20 percent off tune-ups this month" | Prior express written consent for automated texts | Separate written opt-in; never in text-back |
| Review request | "Would you leave us a review?" | Treat as marketing when in doubt | Ask in person or by email unless written consent |
| Opt-out confirmation | "You won't receive more texts." | One message allowed | Send once, record, stop |

## A framework for text-back

1. Decide whether you will send text-back manually or automatically.
2. Write one informational text-back message with your business name and no offer.
3. Send text-back only to people who called you, and only once per missed call.
4. Ask for permission before texting about appointments.
5. Collect written consent separately before any promotional texts.
6. Honor any request to stop, in any words, and record it in an opt-out log.
7. Review your phone system's settings so that it does not send repeated or promotional texts.
8. Have a qualified professional review your consent language before any marketing program.

:::case The Jiffy Lube text-message settlement (real, cited)
In April 2011, Heartland Automotive Services, the largest Jiffy Lube franchisee in the United States, and mobile marketer TextMarks allegedly sent a promotional text message to millions of consumers who had not consented to receive it [10]. MediaPost reported that the message offered a "1 time offer" of 45 percent off an oil change, was sent to 2.3 million consumers, and that the court had earlier rejected Heartland's argument that the TCPA unconstitutionally limited its free speech rights [11]. In 2012, the parties agreed to a proposed settlement valued at approximately $47 million in services (about $35 million in cash value) to class members, plus $4.75 million for plaintiffs' attorneys, and Jiffy Lube agreed to obtain and retain "informed written consent" through "affirmative action on the part of the consumer" before sending text advertisements [10]. These facts come from published legal reporting and concern Jiffy Lube's franchisee, not TTI. The case shows how a single promotional text, sent without clear consent, can create very large liability, and why text-back messages should stay informational.
:::

:::example Oakline's text-back setup (illustrative)
Denise turns on her phone system's missed-call text feature with one message: "Hi, this is Oakline Heating and Air. Sorry we missed your call. Reply here or we'll call you back within two business hours. Reply STOP to opt out." She disables the vendor's default follow-up sequence, which would have sent two more texts with a seasonal discount. Over two weeks, 50 real missed callers receive the text; 22 reply by text, and 14 of those book through text, while 4 reply "stop" or similar and are recorded in the opt-out log. Marcus calls the other 28 within the callback block. Denise estimates the texts saved about 22 × 3 = 66 minutes of phone time that would have been spent on callbacks and voicemail.
:::

:::checklist Text-back compliance
- One informational text-back message with business name.
- No offers, discounts, or review requests in text-back.
- Text-back sent once per missed call.
- Permission asked before appointment texts.
- Separate written consent collected before any promotional texts.
- Opt-out requests honored in any wording and recorded.
- Vendor default sequences reviewed and disabled if promotional.
- Consent language reviewed by a qualified professional.
:::

:::summary
Text-back can help callers who cannot talk, but texts are covered by federal rules. Keep text-back informational, send it once, and keep marketing out of it. The FCC requires consent for autodialed texts, with written consent for telemarketing texts, and its 2024 rules require honoring opt-outs made in any reasonable manner within 10 business days. The Jiffy Lube settlement shows how costly a single promotional text without clear consent can be.
:::

# Chapter 6: Do Not Call and Calling Hours

:::objectives
- Distinguish a callback from a telemarketing call.
- Explain the National Do Not Call Registry and company-specific do-not-call lists.
- Follow calling-hour limits.
- Keep a do-not-call log and honor requests.
:::

Returning a call to someone who just called you is not telemarketing. Calling a past customer to sell a maintenance plan, or calling a list of people who once asked for a quote, can be. Once follow-up shifts from answering a request to selling, federal telemarketing rules may apply. This chapter explains the basics so that your follow-up stays on the right side of the line.

## Key definitions

Telemarketing is a plan, program, or campaign to sell goods or services through telephone calls. The National Do Not Call Registry is a federal list of numbers whose owners have asked not to receive telemarketing calls. A company-specific or entity-specific do-not-call request is a person's request not to be called again by a particular business. An established business relationship is a prior relationship, such as a recent purchase, that permits some live telemarketing calls to numbers on the national registry. Calling hours are the times of day when telemarketing calls are allowed.

## The basic rules

The FTC's guide to the Telemarketing Sales Rule (TSR) explains that it is a violation to call any consumer who has asked not to be called again by or on behalf of a particular seller, and that sellers and telemarketers are responsible for maintaining their own do-not-call lists [14]. The guide explains that sellers may place live telemarketing calls, but not automated calls or robocalls, to a consumer with whom they have an established business relationship, provided the consumer has not asked to be on the seller's do-not-call list [14]. It also states that, unless a telemarketer has a person's prior consent, it is a violation to make outbound telemarketing calls to a person's home outside the hours of 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. local time at the location called [14]. The FCC's rules similarly allow telephone solicitations to home phones only between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. [9].

The TSR also prohibits interfering with a person's right to be placed on a do-not-call list: a business cannot refuse a request, require the person to listen to a sales pitch first, or make them call a different number [14]. States may have additional rules, including stricter calling hours or registries.

| Situation | Telemarketing? | Key rule (summary) |
|---|---|---|
| Returning a missed call from a new caller | No | Answer their request; do not add unrelated sales |
| Calling a customer about their scheduled visit | No | Informational; respect stated preferences |
| Calling past customers to sell a maintenance plan | Likely yes | Check do-not-call list; observe calling hours; live calls only under established relationship |
| Calling old quote requests months later | Likely yes | Check national registry and company list; consider consent |
| Customer says "don't call me again" | n/a | Add to company do-not-call list and honor it |

## A framework for calling rules

1. Separate callbacks (answering a request) from sales calls in your process.
2. Keep a company do-not-call list and check it before any outbound sales call.
3. Accept do-not-call requests in any form, without argument or sales pitch.
4. Make sales calls only between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. in the person's time zone, and check state rules.
5. Use live calls only, not prerecorded or automated messages, for sales follow-up unless you have proper written consent.
6. Check the National Do Not Call Registry before calling anyone without an established relationship or written consent.
7. Make sure any vendor calling on your behalf follows the same rules.
8. Review the list and process quarterly.

:::case Dish Network and the Do Not Call Registry (real, cited)
In 2009, the U.S. Department of Justice, on behalf of the FTC, and the states of California, Illinois, North Carolina, and Ohio sued satellite television provider Dish Network over telemarketing violations [15]. In June 2017, after a bench trial, a federal court in Illinois ordered penalties totaling $280 million, found Dish liable for more than 66 million calls that violated the do-not-call, entity-specific, and abandoned-call provisions of the TSR, and held Dish responsible for calls made by its telemarketing vendors and retailers [15]. The court also imposed injunctive relief, including a requirement to hire a telemarketing compliance expert [15]. After the Seventh Circuit upheld liability but vacated the monetary award for recalculation, the parties agreed in December 2020 to a $210 million settlement of the monetary portion, according to the California Attorney General [16]. These facts come from government sources and concern Dish Network, not TTI. The case shows two lessons for small businesses: do-not-call requests and registries must be honored, and a business can be held responsible for calls that vendors make on its behalf.
:::

:::example Oakline's maintenance-plan calls (illustrative)
Denise wants to call 300 past customers to offer a $179 annual maintenance plan. Marcus checks the company do-not-call list and removes 12 numbers. He checks the dates of last service: 241 customers had service within the past 18 months, and 47 had service longer ago. For the 47 older customers, he checks the National Do Not Call Registry and removes 19 numbers. That leaves 241 + 28 = 269 customers to call, using live calls only, between 9 a.m. and 7 p.m. Over three weeks, Marcus reaches 160 and 38 sign up, for 38 × $179 = $6,802 in plan revenue. Nine people ask not to be called again; Marcus adds them to the do-not-call list the same day.

:::summary
Returning a missed call is not telemarketing, but calling past customers or old leads to sell may be. The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule requires honoring company-specific do-not-call requests, limits telemarketing calls to 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. local time without consent, and prohibits interfering with do-not-call requests. # Chapter 7: Recording and Call Notes

:::objectives
- Decide whether to record calls and how to give notice.
- Understand one-party and all-party consent at a basic level.
- Take useful call notes without collecting unnecessary data.
- Protect caller information.
:::

Recordings and notes can improve service. A recording lets the owner review how a call was handled; notes make sure the technician knows what the caller said. But recording is regulated, and every note is personal information that must be protected. This chapter explains how to capture what you need without creating legal or privacy risk.

## Key definitions

Call recording is capturing the audio of a phone conversation. One-party consent means that a conversation may be recorded if one party, which can be the person recording, consents. All-party consent means that every party must consent. Notice is telling the other party at the start that the call is being recorded. Call notes are written records of what was said. Data minimization is collecting only the information needed for a stated purpose.

## Recording laws

The Digital Media Law Project explains that federal law permits recording a phone call with the consent of at least one party, but that a number of states require the consent of all parties, and that when a call crosses state lines, the safest approach is to follow the stricter law or to get consent from everyone [17]. A short notice at the start of a call, such as "This call may be recorded for quality," combined with the caller's choice to continue, is a common approach, but the details vary, so check with a qualified attorney.

## Call notes

Notes should capture what the technician or the next person needs: name, number, address, the problem, urgency, access details, and the agreed next step. They should not include unnecessary sensitive information, such as payment card numbers written on paper, health details beyond what the job requires, or personal opinions about the caller. The FTC's guide to protecting personal information recommends that businesses take stock of what they collect, scale down to what they need, lock it, pitch what they no longer need, and plan ahead for security incidents [19].

| Data item | Needed? | Where to keep it | Retention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name, phone, address | Yes | Tracker, scheduling system | Duration of relationship |
| Problem description | Yes | Tracker, job record | With job record |
| Gate code or access notes | Yes, for the job | Job record; not on paper left in trucks | Delete after job |
| Payment card number | No, not in notes | Payment system only | Never in notes |
| Recording of call | Optional | Phone system with access controls | Set period, such as 90 days |
| Personal opinions of caller | No | Do not record | Not applicable |

## A framework for recording and notes

1. Decide whether you need recordings, and why.
2. If you record, add a notice at the start of calls and in your greeting where appropriate.
3. Follow the stricter law when callers may be in all-party consent states.
4. Limit access to recordings to named people.
5. Set a deletion period for recordings and follow it.
6. Use a standard note format with only the needed fields.
7. Keep payment details out of notes; take payments through a secure system.
8. Review notes and recordings quarterly for unnecessary data.

:::case Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney (real, cited)
In Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney, decided in 2006, the Supreme Court of California considered a complaint alleging that employees at the brokerage firm's Atlanta branch had repeatedly recorded telephone conversations with California clients without the clients' knowledge or consent [18]. California's privacy statute generally prohibits recording a telephone conversation without the consent of all parties, while Georgia's law permits recording with the consent of one party [18]. The court concluded that California law should apply to determine whether the secret recording was unlawful, allowing the clients to seek an injunction against future recording without notice, though it declined to impose damages for past conduct [18]. The court noted that California law prohibits only secret or undisclosed recording, so a business that discloses at the outset of a call that it is being recorded would not violate it [18]. These facts come from the court's opinion and concern Salomon Smith Barney, not TTI. For a small business, the case shows that the caller's location can determine which recording law applies, and that a simple notice at the start of a call addresses the core concern.
:::

:::example Oakline's notes audit (illustrative)
Marcus reviews 100 call notes from the past month. He finds 23 with gate codes written on paper trackers left in trucks, 6 with partial card numbers written down during phone payments, and 11 with comments about callers' moods. He changes the process: gate codes go only into the scheduling system and are deleted after the job, card payments go through a secure payment link, and mood comments are banned. The phone system's recordings had been kept indefinitely, totaling about 1,800 calls; Denise sets a 90-day deletion period and adds "This call may be recorded for quality" to the start of calls. Marcus estimates the audit took 4 hours and the process changes about 2 more.
:::

:::checklist Recording and notes
- Purpose for recording written down.
- Recording notice at the start of calls.
- Stricter law followed when callers may be in all-party consent states.
- Access to recordings limited to named people.
- Recording deletion period set and followed.
- Standard note format with only needed fields.
- No payment card numbers in notes.
- Quarterly review of notes and recordings.
:::

:::summary
Recordings and notes can improve service but create legal and privacy risk. Federal law permits recording with one party's consent, but some states require all parties' consent, and a notice at the start of a call is a common safeguard. Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney shows that the caller's location can determine which law applies. Keep notes to what the job requires, protect them, and delete what you no longer need.

# Chapter 8: Escalation and Handoff

:::objectives
- Define when a missed call must be escalated to the owner or a senior person.
- Give front-line staff authority to solve common problems without waiting.
- Hand off information so that the caller never has to repeat themselves.
- Close the loop with the caller after an escalation.
:::

Some missed calls cannot be resolved by whoever returns them. An angry customer whose repair failed, a caller asking for a refund, a property manager with ten units, or a caller describing a safety problem may need the owner or a senior technician. Escalation is the process of moving such calls to the right person quickly. Handoff is the transfer of information so that the next person can act without starting over. Done badly, escalation leaves the caller waiting and repeating their story; done well, it shows the caller that the business takes them seriously.

## Key definitions

Escalation is moving a call or issue to a person with more authority or expertise. Handoff is passing complete information to that person. Front-line authority is the power of the person returning the call to resolve certain issues without approval, up to a stated limit. A loop closure is a final contact confirming that the issue was resolved. An escalation trigger is a condition that requires escalation.

## Triggers and authority

Write down a short list of escalation triggers. Typical triggers include a safety concern, a complaint about completed work, a request for a refund or credit above a set amount, a commercial or multi-unit inquiry, a legal threat, and any caller who asks for the owner. Then write down what front-line staff may do without approval: for example, reschedule a visit, waive a diagnostic fee up to $89, or offer a priority appointment. Clear authority prevents two problems: staff who escalate everything, and staff who make promises they cannot keep.

## The handoff note

A handoff note should let the next person act without calling the caller to ask the same questions again. It should include the caller's name and number, address, what happened, what the caller wants, what has been tried, what was promised and by when, and the caller's preferred contact method. A promise in the handoff note, such as "Denise will call you before 5 p.m.," must be kept.

| Trigger | Escalate to | Time limit | Front-line authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safety concern | Owner or on-call technician, plus 911 or utility direction | Immediately | Direct caller to emergency services |
| Complaint about completed work | Owner | Same business day | Apologize; offer priority visit |
| Refund or credit request | Owner | Same business day | Credits up to $89 |
| Commercial or multi-unit inquiry | Owner | Within 4 business hours | Gather details |
| Legal threat | Owner | Same business day | Do not argue; take details |
| Caller asks for owner | Owner | Same business day | Take message; promise time |

## A framework for escalation

1. List escalation triggers and post them with the triage card.
2. Set front-line authority limits in dollars and actions.
3. Use a standard handoff note format.
4. Promise the caller a specific time for the next contact.
5. Escalate within the stated time limit.
6. Have the escalation owner contact the caller and resolve or set a next step.
7. Close the loop: confirm with the caller that the issue is resolved.
8. Review escalations monthly to adjust triggers and authority.

:::case The Ritz-Carlton's $2,000 rule (real, cited)
Horst Schulze, co-founder and former president of The Ritz-Carlton, described in a 2019 interview with Knowledge at Wharton how the company empowered every employee to spend up to $2,000 to correct a mistake for a guest [20]. Schulze explained that it was "an economic decision": he had estimated that a repeat customer was worth about $200,000 to the company over a lifetime, so the company taught every employee problem resolution and authorized them to spend up to that amount [20]. "Nobody ever spent $2,000," he said; instead, employees "bought breakfast or sent a food basket or some cookies," and guests were amazed [20]. These are Ritz-Carlton's documented practices as described by its co-founder, not TTI's results, and a small business's limits will be far smaller. The case illustrates the principle of front-line authority: when the person closest to the problem is trained and trusted to fix it within a clear limit, the customer is helped faster, and the limit is rarely reached.
:::

:::example Oakline's front-line authority (illustrative)
Before setting limits, Marcus escalated every complaint to Denise, who often could not respond until evening. Over one month, 14 complaints waited an average of 6 hours for a first response. Denise sets Marcus's authority: he may apologize, waive the $89 diagnostic fee, and book a priority return visit without asking her. Over the next month, Marcus resolves 9 of 13 complaints himself, waiving fees on 6, for a total cost of 6 × $89 = $534. The other 4 are escalated with handoff notes, and Denise calls each within 3 hours. Denise compares the $534 with the value of keeping customers whose average annual spend with Oakline is about $450, and decides the limit is worth keeping.
:::

:::summary
Escalation moves difficult calls to the right person quickly, and a good handoff note keeps the caller from repeating themselves. Write down triggers, time limits, and front-line authority. Close the loop with every escalated caller. # Chapter 9: Automating With Care

:::objectives
- Decide which parts of the missed-call process to automate.
- Understand how federal rules treat automated and AI-generated calls and texts.
- Evaluate vendor claims about automated answering and follow-up.
- Keep a human in the loop where it matters.
:::

Automation can help a small business respond faster: an automatic text after a missed call, a scheduling link, an after-hours answering service, or an AI voice agent. But automation also multiplies mistakes. A human who sends one wrong text makes one mistake; a system set up wrongly can send thousands. This chapter explains how to automate the routine parts of the process while keeping the risky parts under human control.

## Key definitions

Automation is using software to perform a task without a person acting each time. An autodialer, or automatic telephone dialing system, is equipment defined in the TCPA. An artificial or prerecorded voice is a voice message that is not a live person speaking. An AI voice agent is software that uses artificial intelligence to conduct spoken conversations. Human in the loop means a person reviews or approves an automated action before or after it happens.

## What the rules say

The definition of an autodialer has been narrowed by the Supreme Court. In Facebook v. Duguid (2021), the Court held that to qualify as an autodialer under the TCPA, a device must have the capacity either to store or to produce a telephone number using a random or sequential number generator [21]. That ruling narrowed one part of the law, but it did not remove the TCPA's other restrictions. The rules on artificial and prerecorded voices remain, and in 2024 the FCC confirmed that the TCPA's restrictions on artificial or prerecorded voice calls apply to AI technologies that generate human voices [22]. The FCC's guidance states that AI-generated voice calls require consumer consent unless the caller is exempt or the call is made for an emergency purpose [9]. State laws may also be broader than the federal definition.

## Evaluating vendor claims

Vendors of answering and follow-up tools often make bold claims about bookings or revenue. The FTC has warned businesses not to exaggerate what an AI product can do, not to promise that an AI product does something better than a non-AI product without adequate proof, and to understand the reasonably foreseeable risks of the product [23]. A small business evaluating a vendor should ask for evidence behind any performance claim, test the product on its own calls, and confirm how consent, opt-outs, recording notices, and data storage work.

| Task | Automate? | Human role | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text-back after missed call | Yes, with one informational message | Review replies; handle opt-outs | Repeated or promotional texts |
| Scheduling link | Yes | Confirm unusual bookings | Wrong job type or area |
| After-hours answering (live service) | Optional | Review messages next morning | Inaccurate messages |
| AI voice agent | Test carefully | Review transcripts; set limits | Wrong information; consent and disclosure |
| Outbound sales calls with prerecorded or AI voice | Avoid without written consent | Not applicable | TCPA liability |
| Review requests | Manual or email unless written consent | Choose who to ask | Marketing rules |

## A framework for automation

1. List each step of your missed-call process.
2. Mark which steps are routine, low risk, and repeated often.
3. Automate one step at a time, starting with the lowest risk.
4. Read every message the system will send, including default sequences.
5. Confirm how the system handles consent, opt-outs, and recording notices.
6. Ask vendors for evidence behind performance claims.
7. Review automated activity weekly for errors.
8. Keep a human responsible for exceptions, escalations, and complaints.

:::case Facebook v. Duguid (real, cited)
Facebook offered an optional security feature that sent users "login notification" text messages when someone attempted to access their account from an unknown device or browser [21]. In 2014, Noah Duguid received several such texts, although he had never created a Facebook account, and he tried without success to stop them before bringing a class action alleging that Facebook had violated the TCPA by using an autodialer [21]. In April 2021, the Supreme Court unanimously held that to qualify as an autodialer, a device must have the capacity to store or produce telephone numbers using a random or sequential number generator, and that Facebook's notification system did not [21]. These facts come from the Court's opinion and concern Facebook, not TTI. For a small business, the case teaches two lessons. First, automated texts can reach the wrong person, such as someone who inherited a number, and that person may be annoyed enough to sue. Second, the legal definition of an autodialer is narrower than it once was, but other rules, including those on artificial and AI-generated voices and on state law, still apply, so automation should be designed with consent and opt-outs in mind.
:::

:::example Oakline's automation review (illustrative)
A vendor offers Denise an AI voice agent for $299 per month, claiming it will "book 40 percent more jobs." Denise asks for the evidence behind the claim; the vendor offers testimonials but no data. She runs a free 14-day test on after-hours calls only. The agent handles 38 calls: it books 11 appointments correctly, takes 19 accurate messages, gives a wrong price on 3 calls, and fails to state that it is an automated system on 5 calls because of a setting. Denise fixes the disclosure setting, adds the correct prices, and calculates that 11 bookings in 14 days at $380 is $4,180 in potential revenue, against a cost of about $140 for 14 days. She extends the test for 30 days with Marcus reviewing every transcript each morning, and she will not use the agent for outbound calls.
:::

:::checklist Automation review
- Each process step listed and rated for risk.
- One step automated at a time.
- Every outgoing message read, including default sequences.
- Consent, opt-out, and recording notices confirmed.
- Disclosure that a system is automated where appropriate.
- Vendor evidence requested for performance claims.

# Chapter 10: Trackers and Weekly Review

:::objectives
- Use a daily callback tracker to make sure no caller is forgotten.
- Summarize the week with a short set of numbers.
- Run a 15-minute weekly review that leads to one improvement.
- Decide when to get help answering calls.
:::

A missed-call system that is not measured will drift. Callbacks get skipped on busy days, the greeting promises a reply time no one meets, and opt-outs are forgotten. A tracker and a short weekly review keep the system honest. They also give the owner the evidence needed to decide whether to hire help, change hours, or adjust the process.

## Key definitions

A daily callback tracker is a log of every real missed call, with the time, bucket, reply method, reply time, and outcome. A weekly count sheet summarizes the week's numbers. A response rate is the share of real missed callers who received a reply. A median reply time is the middle value of reply times, which is less affected by a few very slow replies than the average. A booking rate is the share of new callers who booked.

## Why checklists and trackers work

Simple checklists can change outcomes even in complex fields. In a study published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2009, researchers introduced the 19-item World Health Organization Surgical Safety Checklist in eight hospitals in eight cities around the world; the rate of death fell from 1.5 percent before the checklist to 0.8 percent afterward, and inpatient complications fell from 11.0 percent to 7.0 percent [24]. The evidence deserves care: a later study of the introduction of surgical checklists across hospitals in Ontario, Canada, found that implementation was not associated with significant reductions in operative mortality or complications [25]. The lesson is not that any checklist guarantees results, but that a short, well-used tool can make important steps harder to skip, and that results depend on how seriously the tool is used.

## The weekly numbers

Five numbers are usually enough: real missed callers, response rate, median reply time, booking rate for new callers, and opt-outs or complaints. Track them on the weekly count sheet in Appendix E and compare them over four weeks before drawing conclusions.

| Weekly number | How to calculate | Example target |
|---|---|---|
| Real missed callers | Missed calls minus spam, wrong numbers, vendors | Track trend |
| Response rate | Callers replied to ÷ real missed callers | 100 percent |
| Median reply time | Middle value of reply times | Within promised time |
| New-caller booking rate | New callers booked ÷ new callers reached | Track trend |
| Opt-outs and complaints | Count | Low; each reviewed |

## When to get help

At some point, the owner's time may be better spent on paid work than on returning calls. Entrepreneur and author Dan Martell suggests a "buyback rate": divide your annual income by 2,000 hours to get an hourly value, then divide by four; tasks someone else could do for less than that rate are candidates to delegate, and Entrepreneur's example is that an owner earning $100,000 has a buyback rate of $12.50 [26]. The calculation is a rough guide. For phone coverage, compare the cost of help, such as a part-time person or an answering service, with the value of the owner's time and the revenue at stake in unreturned calls.

## A framework for weekly review

1. Record every real missed call on the daily tracker as it happens.
2. At the end of each day, confirm every caller has been replied to or scheduled for a reply.
3. On a fixed day each week, fill in the weekly count sheet.
4. Spend 15 minutes reviewing the five numbers with whoever handles calls.
5. Pick one improvement for the next week.
6. Compare four weeks before changing major parts of the process.
7. Review opt-outs and complaints individually.
8. Every quarter, decide whether you need more help answering calls.

:::case The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (real, cited)
Between October 2007 and September 2008, researchers led by Alex Haynes and Atul Gawande studied the introduction of a 19-item surgical safety checklist in eight hospitals in Toronto, New Delhi, Amman, Auckland, Manila, Ifakara, London, and Seattle, collecting data on 3,733 patients before and 3,955 after the checklist was introduced [24]. The rate of death fell from 1.5 percent to 0.8 percent, and inpatient complications fell from 11.0 percent to 7.0 percent [24]. A later study published in the same journal found that implementation of surgical safety checklists across Ontario, Canada, was not associated with significant reductions in operative mortality or complications [25]. These are findings about hospitals, not about TTI or small businesses, and the mixed evidence matters. The case is useful because it shows both the promise and the limits of simple tools: a checklist can help make critical steps routine, but it works only when people actually use it and when the steps it covers are the ones that matter.
:::

# Glossary

All-party consent: a rule requiring every participant's consent before a conversation is recorded.

Autodialer: under the TCPA as interpreted in Facebook v. Duguid, equipment with the capacity to store or produce telephone numbers using a random or sequential number generator.

Booking rate: the share of new callers reached who book an appointment.

Bucket: a priority group used in triage, such as urgent, new business, or routine.

Call labeling: a carrier's practice of marking calls as "spam likely" or similar.

Callback: a return call made by a business to a person who called and was missed.

Caller ID spoofing: falsifying the number displayed on caller ID.

Do-not-call request: a person's request not to be called again by a particular business.

Escalation: moving a call or issue to a person with more authority or expertise.

Front-line authority: the power of the person handling a call to resolve certain issues without approval, up to a stated limit.

Handoff: passing complete information about a caller to the next person who will act.

Informational text: a text related to a transaction or request the person initiated, with no advertising.

Marketing text: a text that promotes a product, service, or offer.

Median reply time: the middle value of reply times in a period.

One-party consent: a rule allowing a conversation to be recorded with the consent of one participant.

Opt-out: a person's request to stop receiving calls or texts; also called revocation of consent.

Prior express written consent: a signed agreement, which may be electronic, authorizing marketing calls or texts using automated technology.

Response rate: the share of real missed callers who received a reply.

Speed to lead: the time between a prospective customer's inquiry and the business's meaningful reply.

STIR/SHAKEN: a caller ID authentication framework that lets carriers verify that a call comes from the number displayed.

Telemarketing: a plan, program, or campaign to sell goods or services by telephone.

Text-back: a text message sent to a caller after a missed call.

Triage: sorting calls by urgency and type so that the most important are handled first.
