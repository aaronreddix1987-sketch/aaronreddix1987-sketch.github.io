# How to Use This Book

Most local service businesses do not have a lead problem as much as a lead handling problem. Phones ring while the owner is on a ladder. A web form arrives at 9 p.m. and is answered two days later. A quote goes out and is never followed up. A happy customer is never asked for a review. Each of these gaps is small, but together they decide whether marketing money turns into booked jobs.

This book is written for owners, office managers, and front-desk helpers at plumbing, roofing, HVAC, electrical, cleaning, moving, landscaping, auto repair, and salon businesses. It explains why fast, consistent follow-up matters, what research and platform documentation say about it, and how to build simple routines that work even without special software. Every script in the book can be sent by hand from a business phone or email account.

Each chapter follows the same structure. It begins with learning objectives, explains the concept and the evidence behind it, gives a step-by-step method, and walks through a worked example. Worked examples labeled "illustrative" are invented to show how a method works; they are not real customers or results. Case studies are drawn from public sources and are cited by number to the References section at the end of the book. Each chapter closes with a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions.

The running example throughout the book is an invented company, Delgado Heating and Air, a six-technician HVAC business owned by Rosa Delgado. Rosa's office manager, Kevin, answers phones when he can, but on busy days many calls go to voicemail. Her situation will be familiar to most owners in the trades.

The appendices contain the practical kit: missed-call text scripts for nine trades, voicemail greetings, callback scripts, quote follow-up messages, review requests and replies, seasonal check-ins, a lead tracker, a weekly numbers page, and a consent and privacy checklist.

:::note Disclaimer
This book is educational and is not legal, tax, or financial advice. Laws on calls, texts, email, reviews, and consumer contracts vary by state and change over time; consult a qualified professional about your situation. No specific number of leads, booking rate, or income is promised. Case studies describe what specific businesses and researchers reported; their results are their own and may not be typical.
:::

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Map where leads leak out of your business and measure the leaks.
- Respond to calls, texts, and web forms within minutes using a written routine.
- Qualify a job on the first callback and prepare clear estimates.
- Follow up on open quotes politely and consistently.
- Request honest reviews in ways that follow Google's policies and FTC rules.
- Track calls, quotes, and jobs on a single weekly page.
- Use customer phone numbers and records responsibly.

# Chapter 1: Why Leads Leak

:::objectives
- Define a lead and the stages it passes through.
- Identify the most common points where local service leads are lost.
- Understand the research on response time.
- Measure your own lead leaks with a simple audit.
:::

A lead is a person who has contacted your business, or given you permission to contact them, about a possible job. Leads arrive through phone calls, voicemails, texts, web forms, email, social media messages, referrals, and paid lead platforms. Each lead passes through stages: first contact, response, qualification, estimate, follow-up, booking, completion, and, ideally, a review and repeat business.

At each stage, some leads are lost. Some losses are healthy: the caller is outside your service area, wants a service you do not offer, or has a budget far below what the job requires. Other losses are avoidable: the call was missed and never returned, the estimate was confusing, or nobody followed up. The aim of this book is to reduce avoidable losses, not to win every job.

## The Common Leak Points

1. **Missed calls.** Calls that ring out while staff are busy, especially without a voicemail greeting that tells callers what to expect.
2. **Slow responses.** Web forms and messages answered hours or days later, after the customer has hired someone else.
3. **Weak first conversations.** Callbacks that fail to capture the information needed to quote or schedule.
4. **Unclear estimates.** Quotes that leave out scope, exclusions, or next steps.
5. **No follow-up.** Open quotes that are never checked on.
6. **No-shows and late cancellations.** Appointments missed because the customer forgot or was unsure of the time.
7. **No review or repeat request.** Satisfied customers who are never asked for feedback or reminded of seasonal service.

## What the Research Says About Speed

The most widely cited research on response time comes from James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington, who reported their findings in a 2011 Harvard Business Review article [1]. They audited 2,241 US companies by submitting web inquiries and timing the responses. As the article reported, only 37% responded within an hour, 23% never responded, and among companies that did respond within 30 days, the average response time was 42 hours [1]. A separate analysis in the same article of more than a million leads found that firms that tried to contact a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it as firms that waited even an hour longer, and more than 60 times as likely as firms that waited 24 hours or more [1].

These studies are more than a decade old and drew mostly on larger companies, so the exact multipliers should not be treated as a forecast for any particular business. The direction, however, is consistent with everyday experience in the trades: a homeowner with a broken air conditioner in July usually calls several companies and books the first one that answers clearly.

## The Five-Step Lead Leak Audit

1. **Count inbound contacts.** For two weeks, record every call, voicemail, text, form, and message, including missed calls from your phone's call log.
2. **Record response times.** For each, note when it arrived and when someone first responded.
3. **Track outcomes.** Mark whether each lead became an estimate, a booked job, a lost job, or was unqualified.
4. **Find the biggest gap.** Compare stages to see where the largest number of qualified leads drop out.
5. **Choose one fix.** Start with the single leak that loses the most qualified leads.

:::example Rosa's Two-Week Audit (illustrative)
Kevin records every contact at Delgado Heating and Air for two weeks in late spring. There are 146 inbound contacts: 98 calls answered live, 31 missed calls, 11 web forms, and 6 texts. Of the 31 missed calls, 12 left a voicemail, and Kevin returned 9 of those the same day. The other 19 missed callers never left a message, and nobody called them back. Web forms took an average of 19 hours to answer because they went to an inbox Rosa checks at night. Of 52 estimates sent, only 14 received any follow-up. Rosa concludes that missed calls without a callback and unanswered estimates are her two biggest leaks, and she decides to fix missed calls first.
:::

# Chapter 2: Being Easy to Find and Contact

:::objectives
- Understand how Google ranks local businesses.
- Set up a complete, accurate Google Business Profile.
- Answer common customer questions online.
- Make every contact route clear and monitored.
:::

Lead handling begins before the phone rings. Customers must be able to find you, understand what you do, and contact you through a route someone actually monitors. For most local service businesses, the Google Business Profile, which appears on Google Search and Maps, is the most important single listing.

## How Google Ranks Local Results

Google states that local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity [2]. Relevance is how well a profile matches a search; distance is how far the business is from the searcher or the searched location; and popularity reflects how well known a business is, including signals such as reviews [2]. Google also states that there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking [2]. Its guidance recommends verifying the business, keeping information complete and accurate, responding to reviews, and adding photos [2].

## Contact Routes Change

Platforms change their features, so owners should not rely on any single channel. Google discontinued the chat feature in Business Profiles as of July 31, 2024, and recommended that businesses make sure a phone number appears on the profile [9]. A business that had relied on profile chat for inquiries had to move those conversations elsewhere. Keep your phone number, website, and hours accurate on every listing, and review them quarterly.

## Answering Questions Online

Customers want answers before they call: what you do, where you work, typical price ranges or price factors, how scheduling works, and whether you handle emergencies. Clear answers on your website and profile reduce unqualified calls and help qualified customers choose you.

## Steps to Get Found and Contacted

1. **Claim and verify** your Google Business Profile.
2. **Complete every field**: categories, service area, hours, phone, website, services, and photos of real work.
3. **List contact routes** you actually monitor, and remove those you do not.
4. **Publish answers** to the ten questions customers ask most.
5. **Check listings quarterly** for accuracy, including holiday hours.

:::example Cleaning Up Rosa's Listings (illustrative)
Rosa finds that her Google Business Profile still shows an old phone number that forwards to a line nobody checks, and her website lists an email address that goes to a former employee. She updates both, adds her service area by city, posts photos of recent installations, and publishes a "Common Questions" page covering service area, emergency availability, diagnostic fees, and what happens during a tune-up. Kevin notices fewer calls asking whether the company serves outlying towns.
:::

:::case River Pools and Spas: Answering Customers' Questions
The New York Times reported in 2013 on River Pools and Spas, a Virginia pool installer co-owned by Marcus Sheridan [6]. During the 2008–2009 downturn, the company's sales collapsed, and it could no longer afford its previous advertising budget of about $250,000 a year [6]. Sheridan began writing blog posts that answered the questions customers asked most, including questions about price, and the Times reported that the company cut its advertising spending to roughly a tenth of its previous level while its sales eventually recovered beyond their pre-downturn peak [6]. The approach later became known as "They Ask, You Answer." River Pools' results are its own and reflect years of consistent writing; the general lesson is that answering real customer questions publicly can bring in better-informed inquiries.
:::

# Chapter 3: Speed to Lead: The Five-Minute Routine

:::objectives
- Set a written response-time standard.
- Use a five-minute routine for every new inquiry.
- Assign responsibility for each contact route.
- Handle after-hours inquiries honestly.
:::

Speed matters, but speed without a routine produces rushed, inconsistent replies. The five-minute routine is a short, repeatable habit that ensures every new inquiry receives a calm, useful first response quickly, even when the person responding is busy.

## Set a Response Standard

Write down how quickly you will respond to each type of inquiry during business hours. A reasonable starting standard for many small service businesses is a first response within 5 to 15 minutes during business hours and by the start of the next business day for after-hours inquiries. Choose a standard you can actually meet; a promise broken is worse than a modest promise kept.

## The Five-Minute Routine

1. **Notice.** Check missed calls, voicemails, texts, and forms at set times, such as every hour and at every break between jobs.
2. **Acknowledge.** Send a short, personal reply that confirms you received the inquiry and states when you will call.
3. **Capture.** Log the lead with name, phone, address or area, service needed, urgency, and source.
4. **Call.** Return the call within your stated time.
5. **Set the next step.** End every contact with a specific next step: an appointment, an estimate date, or a polite close if the job is not a fit.

## Assign Responsibility

Every contact route needs an owner and a backup. If the office manager is responsible for web forms, someone else covers when they are out. Unassigned inboxes are where leads go to wait.

## After-Hours Honesty

If you do not offer emergency service at night, say so clearly in your voicemail and auto-replies, and tell callers when you will respond. If you do offer emergency service, state any after-hours fees up front.

:::example The Routine at Delgado Heating and Air (illustrative)
Rosa sets a standard: during business hours, every missed call or form gets a personal text within 10 minutes and a call within 30 minutes. Kevin owns the office phone and forms; the on-call technician is the backup during lunch. Technicians check the shared missed-call list between jobs. Within a month, the median first response falls from several hours to about 12 minutes on weekdays. Rosa also notes that the busiest summer weeks remain difficult and plans to add part-time phone help for July.
:::

:::case Harvard Business Review: The First Hour
The same 2011 Harvard Business Review article reported that firms that attempted to contact a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that waited longer, and that most firms did not respond that quickly [1]. The authors' data concerned web-generated leads at larger companies, not local trades specifically. Still, the finding supports a simple management principle: a written response standard, with a named person responsible for meeting it, turns speed from a matter of luck into a routine.
:::

# Chapter 4: Missed Calls, Voicemail, and Text-Back

:::objectives
- Write a voicemail greeting that helps callers and you.
- Send hand-written missed-call texts appropriately.
- Understand basic consent principles for business texting.
- Recognize how missed calls affect paid lead platforms.
:::

No small business can answer every call live. The goal is not perfection but recovery: every missed caller should hear a clear greeting, and every missed call during business hours should receive a prompt follow-up.

## The Voicemail Greeting

A good greeting identifies the business, tells callers you are with a customer or on a job, asks them to leave specific information (name, address or town, service needed, and the best time to call back), and states when they can expect a return call. Keep it under 30 seconds. Appendix B contains five greetings.

## Missed-Call Text-Back

Many customers prefer a quick text to waiting for a callback. When someone calls your business from a mobile phone and you miss the call, a short, personal text sent by hand from your business phone, such as "Hi, this is Kevin at Delgado Heating and Air. Sorry we missed your call; we're with a customer. Is this about a repair, a tune-up, or a new system? I can call you back within 30 minutes," often restarts the conversation. Appendix A contains scripts for nine trades.

## Consent Basics for Texting

Texting rules are complex, and this section is general information only. The FCC's rules under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act require consent for informational autodialed or prerecorded calls and texts to wireless numbers, and require prior written consent for autodialed telemarketing texts [10]. The wireless industry association CTIA publishes voluntary messaging best practices that distinguish conversational messages (a back-and-forth started by the consumer), informational messages, and promotional messages, each with different consent expectations [11]. CTIA's guidance describes a consumer-initiated text followed by a single quick reply as likely conversational, and calls for honoring opt-out words such as STOP, end, unsubscribe, cancel, and quit [11]. Practical implications:

- Hand-sent replies to someone who just called you are different from automated marketing campaigns.
- Do not add callers to promotional text lists without clear permission.
- Honor any request to stop, immediately, and record it.
- If you adopt automated texting software, review consent requirements with a qualified advisor first.

## Missed Calls and Paid Leads

On Google Local Services Ads, Google says your responsiveness to customer inquiries is one of the factors in ad ranking and that missed calls may negatively affect it [8]. Google also counts some missed calls as chargeable leads: under its documentation, a missed call during business hours in which the customer stays on the line for more than 20 seconds can be a valid lead [7]. A business that pays for leads and then misses calls pays twice: once for the lead and again in lower visibility.

:::example A Text-Back That Saved a Job (illustrative)
At 2:10 p.m. on a hot afternoon, a homeowner calls Delgado Heating and Air while Kevin is on another line. At 2:14, Kevin sends a hand-typed text: "Hi, this is Kevin at Delgado Heating and Air. Sorry we missed you. Is your AC not cooling? I can call in 15 minutes, or reply here with your address and what's happening." The homeowner replies with the address and says the unit is blowing warm air. Kevin calls at 2:25, books a 4:30 diagnostic, and notes that the homeowner had already left voicemails with two other companies.
:::

:::case Google Local Services Ads: Responsiveness Counts
Google's help documentation for Local Services Ads states that ads are ranked in an auction that considers bid and profile quality, along with how likely an ad is to produce a lead, including the business's responsiveness to customer inquiries; Google notes that missed calls may negatively affect responsiveness [8]. Profile quality factors listed by Google include rating, number of reviews, average response time, high-quality images, and completed verification checks [8]. The documentation illustrates how response habits, not only advertising budgets, influence visibility on a major lead platform.
:::

# Chapter 5: The First Callback

:::objectives
- Prepare before returning a call.
- Qualify a job with four core questions.
- Listen for the customer's real problem and urgency.
- End each call with a clear next step.
:::

The first live conversation decides whether a lead becomes an appointment. A good callback is short, friendly, and structured: it confirms the problem, checks fit, and sets a next step. A poor one wanders, misses key information, and requires a second call.

## Prepare in 30 Seconds

Before calling, review the voicemail, text, or form. Note the customer's name, town, and what they said. Have your schedule open.

## The Four Core Questions

1. **What is happening?** Let the customer describe the problem in their own words.
2. **Where is the job?** Confirm address or town and whether it is in your service area.
3. **How urgent is it?** Emergency, this week, or planning ahead.
4. **Who decides?** Confirm whether the caller owns the property or needs someone else's approval.

## Listen for Implications

Sales research has long emphasized understanding the customer's problem before presenting a solution. Huthwaite International, whose SPIN Selling methodology is based on research involving more than 35,000 sales calls, teaches that questions about a buyer's situation, problems, and the implications of those problems build understanding of need [18]. In a service call, an implication might be that a broken water heater means a family cannot shower before work, or that a failed walk-in cooler means a restaurant may lose inventory. Understanding the stakes helps you prioritize and explain your recommendation.

## Set the Next Step

End with a clear commitment: an appointment window, an estimate visit, or a friendly explanation that the job is not a fit, ideally with a referral.

:::example A 90-Second Callback (illustrative)
Kevin returns a voicemail: "Hi, Ms. Patel, this is Kevin from Delgado Heating and Air, returning your call about the furnace. Can you tell me what's happening?" She explains that the furnace runs but blows cold air. Kevin confirms her address is in the service area, asks whether anyone in the home is at risk from the cold (her elderly father lives with her), and confirms she owns the home. He offers a slot the same evening and explains the diagnostic fee. She books. Kevin logs the call and notes the elderly resident so the technician arrives prepared.
:::

:::case Huthwaite: Questions Before Solutions
Huthwaite International states that its SPIN methodology was developed from research observing more than 35,000 sales calls [18]. The methodology groups effective questions into situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff questions [18]. Local service callbacks are far shorter than the complex sales Huthwaite studied, but the principle transfers: a few well-chosen questions about the problem and its consequences produce better appointments than an immediate price quote over the phone.
:::

| Question | Purpose | Example wording |
|---|---|---|
| What is happening? | Understand the problem | "Can you tell me what's going on?" |
| Where is the job? | Confirm service area | "What's the address or nearest cross street?" |
| How urgent is it? | Prioritize scheduling | "Is this an emergency or can it wait a few days?" |
| Who decides? | Avoid wasted visits | "Will anyone else need to be there or approve the work?" |
| What's the impact? | Understand stakes | "How is this affecting your home or business?" |

# Chapter 6: Estimates That Customers Understand

:::objectives
- Capture complete job information before quoting.
- Write estimates with clear scope, price, and exclusions.
- Offer options where appropriate.
- Understand the FTC Cooling-Off Rule for in-home sales.
:::

An estimate is both a price and a promise. Customers compare estimates, often from several companies, and an unclear one invites doubt. A clear estimate explains what will be done, what it costs, what is not included, and what happens next.

## Capture the Job Before You Quote

Use a job prep sheet (Appendix D) to record the customer's problem, site conditions, measurements, photos, access issues, and any special requests. Missing information leads to change orders and disputes.

## Elements of a Clear Estimate

1. **Customer and job details**: name, address, date, and estimate number.
2. **Scope of work**: what will be done, in plain language.
3. **Price**: itemized where helpful, with taxes and fees shown.
4. **Exclusions and assumptions**: what is not included and what could change the price.
5. **Options**: good, better, best choices when the job allows.
6. **Timeline**: when work can start and how long it will take.
7. **Validity**: how long the price is good for.
8. **Next step**: how to accept and what happens after.

## Options Without Pressure

Offering two or three options, such as repair now, repair with a maintenance plan, or replacement, lets customers choose based on their budget and priorities. Each option should be honest; do not include an option designed only to make another look reasonable.

## The FTC Cooling-Off Rule

Home service businesses should understand the FTC's Cooling-Off Rule. The rule gives buyers three days to cancel certain sales made at their home, workplace, or temporary locations, and it applies when a buyer invites a salesperson to make a presentation in the home [12]. According to the FTC, the rule does not cover sales under $25 made at home, sales needed to meet an emergency, or sales made because the buyer asked the seller to visit the home to repair or perform maintenance on personal property; however, things the buyer purchases beyond that repair or maintenance request are covered [12]. For covered sales, the seller must tell the buyer about the right to cancel at the time of sale and provide two copies of a cancellation form and a dated contract or receipt [12]. The right to cancel lasts until midnight of the third business day after the sale, with Saturdays counting as business days [12]. Many states have additional home-solicitation or contractor rules; check with your state licensing board or an attorney.

:::example A Clear Replacement Estimate (illustrative)
A technician finds a 17-year-old air conditioner with a failed compressor. Rosa's estimate presents three options: (1) compressor replacement at $2,450 with a 1-year labor warranty, noting that other aging components may fail; (2) a mid-efficiency system replacement at $7,800; and (3) a high-efficiency system at $10,900, noting that any utility rebate eligibility should be confirmed by the customer with their utility. Exclusions state that ductwork modifications, if needed, are priced separately. Because the replacement options go beyond the homeowner's original repair request and are sold in the home, Rosa's estimate form includes the Cooling-Off Rule cancellation notice and forms.
:::

:::case FTC: The Cooling-Off Rule and In-Home Sales
The FTC's consumer guidance on the Cooling-Off Rule uses the example of a door-to-door seller persuading a homeowner to pay for a new roof the homeowner is not sure they need [12]. The rule's exceptions for emergencies and buyer-requested repairs, and its coverage of purchases beyond those requests, map closely onto everyday trade situations such as a repair visit that turns into a replacement proposal [12]. For an honest business, the rule is less a burden than a reminder: customers should never feel rushed into large in-home purchases, and clear written terms protect both sides.
:::

# Chapter 7: Quote Follow-Up Without Pressure

:::objectives
- Build a simple Day 1, Day 3, Day 7 follow-up sequence.
- Write follow-up messages that help rather than push.
- Respond calmly to price comparisons and delays.
- Close out quotes politely.
:::

Many customers who request estimates simply get busy, compare options, or need time to arrange money. A polite follow-up gives them a reason to reply and shows the professionalism they want in a contractor. Without follow-up, open quotes quietly expire.

## The Follow-Up Sequence

1. **Day 1: Confirm receipt.** "Did the estimate come through, and do you have any questions?"
2. **Day 3: Offer help.** Address a likely question: options, scheduling, financing information if you offer it, or permits.
3. **Day 7: Check timing.** Ask whether they are still considering the work and when they expect to decide.
4. **Close.** After the final follow-up, send a polite message closing the quote and inviting them to reach out later.

Adjust timing for urgency: an emergency repair quote may need a same-day follow-up, while a landscaping redesign may warrant a longer sequence.

## Calm Answers to Price and Timing

When a customer says another company is cheaper, ask what the other estimate includes, compare scope honestly, and explain what your price covers. If the other option is a better fit, say so. When a customer wants to wait, ask what would make the timing right and offer to follow up then.

## Email Rules

If your follow-up messages are commercial emails promoting your services, the CAN-SPAM Act applies. The FTC explains that CAN-SPAM covers commercial messages, requires accurate header information and non-deceptive subject lines, requires a valid physical postal address, requires a clear way to opt out of future marketing email, and requires honoring opt-out requests within 10 business days [14]. Individual follow-ups on a requested quote often fall in a gray area; including your address and honoring any request to stop is a sensible practice regardless.

:::example Rosa's Follow-Up Sequence (illustrative)
Rosa sends a replacement estimate on Monday. Tuesday, Kevin texts: "Hi Mr. Owens, this is Kevin at Delgado Heating and Air. Just checking the estimate came through. Any questions I can answer?" Thursday, Kevin emails a one-page comparison of the two system options. The following Monday, he asks: "Are you still considering the replacement? If the timing isn't right, I'm glad to check back later." Mr. Owens replies that he is waiting for a second estimate and asks how the warranties compare. Kevin answers, and Mr. Owens chooses the mid-efficiency option two days later. Over the next quarter, Rosa tracks follow-ups and finds that quotes receiving the full sequence are booked more often than those without, though she notes that her sample is small.
:::

:::case FTC: The CAN-SPAM Act
The FTC's CAN-SPAM compliance guide stresses that the law is not limited to bulk email: it covers any email whose primary purpose is commercial advertisement or promotion, makes no exception for business-to-business email, and allows penalties for each separate violating email [14]. The guide's main requirements, including honest headers and subject lines, a physical address, a working opt-out, and honoring opt-outs within 10 business days, are straightforward to build into any follow-up template [14]. For a small service business, following them is also simply good manners.
:::

# Chapter 8: Confirmations, Reminders, and No-Shows

:::objectives
- Reduce missed appointments with confirmations and reminders.
- Write booking confirmation and on-my-way messages.
- Understand the evidence on text reminders.
- Handle no-shows and reschedules gracefully.
:::

A booked job is not yet a completed job. Customers forget appointments, misunderstand arrival windows, or are not home to let the technician in. Each no-show wastes a technician's time and an open slot that another customer could have used.

## The Confirmation Sequence

1. **Booking confirmation.** Sent immediately: date, arrival window, service, technician name if known, and any preparation (clear access to the unit, secure pets).
2. **Reminder.** Sent the day before: confirm the window and ask the customer to reply if they need to reschedule.
3. **On-my-way message.** Sent when the technician leaves for the job, with an estimated arrival time.
4. **Post-visit message.** Thanks the customer and explains how to reach you with questions.

These are informational messages sent to customers who provided their number to book service. Keep them free of promotions unless you have appropriate consent [11].

## What the Evidence Shows

Research on reminders comes mostly from health care, where missed appointments are well studied. A Cochrane systematic review, updated in 2013, included eight randomized controlled trials with 6,615 participants and found low- to moderate-quality evidence that text message reminders increase attendance at health care appointments compared with no reminders or postal reminders, with a similar effect to phone call reminders [13]. Service appointments differ from medical visits, but the underlying mechanism, people forget and a timely reminder helps, is general.

## Handling No-Shows

When a customer is not home, call and text immediately, wait a reasonable time, and leave a polite note if appropriate. Follow up later to reschedule without blame. If you charge a trip fee for missed appointments, disclose it clearly at booking.

:::example Confirmations at Delgado Heating and Air (illustrative)
Before adding reminders, Rosa's technicians arrived to locked doors about six times a month. Kevin begins sending a confirmation at booking, a reminder at 4 p.m. the day before, and an on-my-way text. Customers occasionally reply to reschedule, which frees slots for others. After two months, no-shows fall to about two a month. Rosa recognizes that other changes, including a clearer arrival window, may have contributed.
:::

:::case Cochrane: Text Message Reminders
The Cochrane review "Mobile phone messaging reminders for attendance at healthcare appointments," by Gurol-Urganci and colleagues, pooled eight randomized trials involving 6,615 participants [13]. It concluded that text reminders improved attendance compared with no reminder and postal reminders and performed similarly to phone call reminders, while rating the evidence as low to moderate quality [13]. The review is useful for service businesses because it shows that a simple, low-cost reminder can perform as well as a more time-consuming phone call.
:::

| Message | Timing | Must include |
|---|---|---|
| Booking confirmation | At booking | Date, window, service, preparation |
| Reminder | Day before | Window; how to reschedule |
| On-my-way | Departure | Technician name; ETA |
| Post-visit | Same day | Thanks; contact for questions |
| No-show follow-up | Same day | Polite reschedule offer |

:::checklist Appointment Reliability
- Confirmation sent at every booking.
- Reminder sent the day before.
- On-my-way message sent with ETA.

# Chapter 9: Honest Reviews and Replies

:::objectives
- Ask customers for reviews in ways that follow Google's policies.
- Understand the FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials.
- Reply professionally to positive and negative reviews.
- Avoid practices that create legal and platform risk.
:::

Reviews influence both customer choice and local search visibility. Google lists reviews and replies among the ways a business can stand out, and on Local Services Ads, rating and number of reviews are profile quality factors [2][8]. Because reviews matter, there are strong temptations to manipulate them. Those temptations carry real risk.

## What Google Prohibits

Google's Maps content policy states that reviews should reflect a genuine experience [3]. It prohibits merchants from offering incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods or services in exchange for reviews, from discouraging or prohibiting negative reviews, and from selectively soliciting positive reviews from customers [3]. Reviews based on conflicts of interest, such as those from employees or family, are also prohibited [3].

## What the FTC Prohibits

The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, which took effect on October 21, 2024, prohibits practices including fake reviews, buying reviews conditioned on their sentiment, certain undisclosed insider reviews, and suppressing negative reviews through threats or intimidation [4]. The FTC can seek civil penalties for knowing violations [4].

## How to Ask

1. **Ask every customer**, not just the happy ones. Selective solicitation violates Google's policy [3].
2. **Ask soon after the job**, while the experience is fresh.
3. **Make it easy** with a direct link.
4. **Ask for honesty**: "We'd appreciate an honest review of your experience."
5. **Offer no incentive** for reviews.

## How to Reply

Reply to positive reviews with brief, specific thanks. Reply to negative reviews calmly: thank the reviewer, acknowledge the concern without arguing, protect privacy by not disclosing job details, and invite them to contact you directly. Future customers read replies as evidence of how you treat people.

:::example Replying to a Negative Review (illustrative)
A customer posts a two-star review saying the technician arrived two hours late and did not call. Rosa replies: "Thank you for letting us know, and I'm sorry we arrived late without calling. That's not the standard we set. I'd like to hear more and make it right; please call me directly at the office." She calls the customer, learns the on-my-way text failed to send, and fixes the process. The customer later updates the review on their own initiative; Rosa did not ask them to.
:::

:::case FTC: Fashion Nova and Suppressed Reviews
In January 2022, the FTC announced that online retailer Fashion Nova would pay $4.2 million to settle allegations that it blocked negative reviews of its products from being posted on its website [5]. The FTC alleged that the company misrepresented that its website reviews reflected the views of all purchasers who submitted reviews when, in fact, reviews with ratings lower than four stars were suppressed [5]. The FTC described the case as its first to challenge a company's failure to post negative reviews [5]. Fashion Nova is not a local service business, but the principle applies widely: presenting only positive feedback as if it were complete feedback can be deceptive.
:::

| Practice | Allowed? | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Asking all customers for honest reviews | Yes | Google policy [3] |
| Offering a discount for a review | No | Google policy [3]; FTC rule [4] |
| Asking only happy customers | No (on Google) | Google policy [3] |
| Employees reviewing the business | No | Google policy [3]; FTC rule [4] |
| Threatening reviewers to remove reviews | No | FTC rule [4] |
| Replying professionally to criticism | Yes | Google guidance [2] |

:::checklist Review Practices
- Every customer receives a review request.
- Requests ask for honest reviews.
- No incentives are offered for reviews.
- Staff, family, and friends do not post reviews.
- Negative reviews receive calm, private-information-free replies.
- Reviews are never suppressed or threatened.
- Process problems revealed by reviews are fixed.
:::

:::summary
Reviews affect customer choice and visibility. Google prohibits incentives, discouraging negative reviews, selective solicitation, and conflict-of-interest reviews. The FTC's 2024 rule prohibits fake and suppressed reviews, among other practices, and the Fashion Nova case shows the cost of hiding negative feedback. Ask every customer for an honest review and reply professionally.
:::

# Chapter 10: Paid Lead Sources

:::objectives
- Understand how Google Local Services Ads charges for leads.
- Compare pay-per-lead and pay-per-click advertising.
- Protect paid leads with fast response.
- Measure cost per booked job, not just cost per lead.
:::

Paid lead sources, including Google Local Services Ads, search ads, and lead marketplaces, can fill a schedule quickly. They can also drain money if leads are handled poorly. The lead handling habits in this book matter most when each lead has a price.

## How Local Services Ads Work

Google describes Local Services Ads as a pay-per-lead product: businesses pay when a potential customer contacts them through the ad by phone, message, or booking, rather than paying for clicks [19]. Advertisers set an average weekly budget; Google says spending can exceed the weekly average in a given week but will not exceed a monthly maximum calculated from the weekly budget [7]. Lead prices vary by location, job type, lead type, and bidding mode [7]. Google states that eligible businesses can earn badges such as Google Guaranteed, Google Screened, and License Verified through its verification process [19].

## Lead Credits and Quality

Google says it filters invalid activity such as spam and duplicate contacts, and that charged leads may be reassessed and credited automatically if determined to be low quality [7]. Businesses can also give feedback on lead quality [7]. Review your leads weekly and use the available feedback tools for leads outside your services or area.

## Pay-Per-Click Search Ads

Standard Google Ads search campaigns charge per click. Google explains that daily spending can reach up to twice the average daily budget on some days, while monthly charges will not exceed the average daily budget multiplied by 30.4 [17]. Set budgets with those rules in mind.

## Measure Cost Per Booked Job

Cost per lead is only part of the picture. Track: leads received, leads answered, estimates, jobs booked, and revenue per source. A cheaper lead that rarely books may cost more per job than an expensive lead that books often.

:::example Comparing Two Lead Sources (illustrative)
Over a quarter, Rosa compares Local Services Ads with a lead marketplace. Local Services Ads produce 60 leads at an average of $45 each ($2,700) and 24 booked jobs: $112.50 per booked job. The marketplace produces 80 leads at $25 each ($2,000), but because the same leads are sold to several competitors, only 12 book: $166.67 per booked job. Rosa shifts budget toward the source with the lower cost per booked job and keeps tracking, noting that seasonality and her own response speed affect both numbers.
:::

:::case Google: How Local Services Ads Leads Work
Google's Local Services Ads documentation explains which contacts count as valid leads, including calls the business answers or returns and certain missed calls during business hours in which the customer stays on the line more than 20 seconds [7]. It also explains that message leads are often priced lower than phone leads and that pricing depends on factors such as the service requested and whether the customer has contacted other advertisers [7]. Read closely, the documentation is a reminder that response habits affect both what a business pays and how visible it is.

# Chapter 11: Repeat and Seasonal Business

:::objectives
- Understand why existing customers are valuable.
- Build a permission-based seasonal reminder system.
- Write repeat and check-in messages.
- Avoid over-messaging.
:::

New leads are only one source of work. Past customers already know your quality, and many trades have natural repeat cycles: HVAC tune-ups, gutter cleaning, lawn care, pest control, oil changes, and haircuts. A simple reminder system brings customers back at the right time.

## The Value of Retention

Harvard Business Review editor Amy Gallo wrote in 2014 that, depending on the study and industry, acquiring a new customer is anywhere from five to 25 times more expensive than retaining an existing one, and cited research by Frederick Reichheld of Bain & Company associating a 5% increase in retention with a 25% to 95% increase in profits [16]. These figures are widely repeated and come from varied contexts, so treat them as directional rather than precise. For a local service business, the practical point is simpler: a past customer who already trusts you is often the easiest job to book.

## A Seasonal Reminder System

1. **Record service dates** and what was done for every customer.
2. **Ask permission** to send reminders, and record the answer.
3. **Set reminder dates** based on the service cycle.
4. **Send helpful reminders**, not just promotions.
5. **Honor opt-outs** immediately.

Promotional messages by text generally require stronger consent than informational messages [10][11], and commercial emails must follow CAN-SPAM [14].

## Over-Messaging

More messages are not always better. A customer who receives weekly promotions may unsubscribe or stop trusting you. Match frequency to the service cycle.

:::example Rosa's Tune-Up Reminders (illustrative)
At the end of every job, Rosa's technicians ask: "Would you like a reminder when your next tune-up is due?" Customers who agree are logged with a reminder date. In early March and early September, Kevin sends: "Hi, this is Kevin at Delgado Heating and Air. Your spring AC tune-up is due. Reply to schedule or STOP to opt out of reminders." About a third of reminded customers book, filling slow weeks before the summer rush.
:::

:::case Harvard Business Review: Keeping the Right Customers
In "The Value of Keeping the Right Customers," Amy Gallo summarized research suggesting that retention is far cheaper than acquisition and that small improvements in retention can have large effects on profit [16]. The article also emphasized that not all customers are equally valuable to keep [16]. For service businesses, this suggests focusing reminder programs on customers who fit your services well and who have agreed to hear from you, rather than messaging everyone as often as possible.
:::

## Calm Answers to Common Questions

- **"Another company is cheaper."** "That's helpful to know. Would you be willing to share what their estimate includes? I want to make sure you're comparing the same scope. If theirs is a better fit, that's fine too."
- **"We want to wait."** "That makes sense. What would make the timing right? I can check back then."
- **"Can you do better on price?"** "I can look at scope options that reduce the price, such as [option]. The price for the same work reflects our costs and warranty."
- **"We need to think about it."** "Of course. What are the main things you're weighing? I'm glad to send anything that helps."

# Appendix H: Consent, Privacy, and Stop-Rule Checklist

This checklist is general information, not legal advice. Rules vary by state; consult a qualified advisor.

- Hand-sent replies go only to people who contacted us or gave us their number for service.
- Promotional texts are sent only with appropriate written consent [10][11].
- Every request to stop (STOP, end, cancel, unsubscribe, quit, or similar) is honored immediately and logged [11].
- Automated or AI-voice calls and texts are reviewed for FCC consent requirements before use [10].
- Commercial emails include accurate headers, a physical address, and an opt-out honored within 10 business days [14].

- Paper records are secured and shredded when no longer needed [15].
- Customer information is never sold or shared without permission.

# Glossary

**Answer rate.** The share of incoming leads that receive a live answer or prompt response.

**Booking rate.** Jobs booked divided by leads or estimates.

**CAN-SPAM Act.** A US law setting rules for commercial email, enforced by the FTC.

**Conversational message.** In CTIA's guidance, a back-and-forth text exchange initiated by the consumer.

**Cooling-Off Rule.** An FTC rule giving buyers three days to cancel certain sales made at home or at temporary locations.

**Cost per booked job.** Marketing spend divided by jobs booked from that source.

**CTIA.** The trade association for the US wireless industry, which publishes voluntary messaging best practices.

**Google Business Profile.** A free listing that shows business information on Google Search and Maps.

**Lead.** A person who has contacted a business, or given permission to be contacted, about a possible job.

**Lead credit.** A refund of a charge for a lead deemed invalid or low quality on a paid platform.

**Local Services Ads.** Google's pay-per-lead advertising product for local service businesses.

**Median response time.** The middle value of response times, less distorted by extremes than the average.

**No-show.** A customer who is not present for a scheduled appointment.

**Opt-out.** A customer's request to stop receiving messages.

**Pay-per-click.** An advertising model in which the advertiser pays for each click on an ad.

**Pay-per-lead.** An advertising model in which the advertiser pays for each qualifying contact.

**Selective solicitation.** Asking only customers likely to leave positive reviews, prohibited by Google's policy.

**Speed to lead.** The time between an inquiry and the first meaningful response.

**TCPA.** The Telephone Consumer Protection Act, which the FCC implements with rules on calls and texts.

**Text-back.** A text message sent in reply to a missed call.
