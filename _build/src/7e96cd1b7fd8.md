# How to Use This Book

A remote service business is one of the most accessible ventures available to a working adult. It requires no storefront, little inventory, and often no employees. That accessibility is also its main hazard. Because the barriers to entry are low, the market is crowded, prices are easy to compare, and many new owners confuse being busy with being in business. The owners who last tend to share a small set of habits: they sell a skill they can already deliver well, to a defined client, at a price grounded in arithmetic; they register and insure the business properly; they communicate in writing with discipline; and they keep records that make tax time uneventful.

This book is written for people who want to sell a professional skill (writing, design, bookkeeping support, administrative assistance, customer support, project coordination, marketing operations, technical setup, research, or a similar service) to clients they serve primarily online. It is also written for freelancers who already have a few clients and want to turn an informal side income into a structured business. It does not assume a business degree. It does assume that you are willing to do the arithmetic and paperwork that a durable business requires.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Explain the economics of a small remote service business and set realistic expectations for its first year.
- Choose a service you can deliver remotely, check it against licensing rules, and state it in one clear sentence.
- Define an ideal client and design a productized offer around a specific outcome.
- Register the business, obtain an Employer Identification Number where appropriate, separate your finances, and evaluate insurance.
- Assemble a secure, economical home office and software stack, and understand the home office tax rules.
- Price your service from target income, capacity, and costs rather than from guesswork.
- Find first clients through warm networks, referrals, platforms, and content, while avoiding common work-from-home scams.
- Write a simple service agreement, invoice correctly, and get paid on time.
- Deliver work from a distance using asynchronous communication practices documented by well-known remote companies.
- Track money, taxes, and a small set of operating metrics, and decide when and how to delegate.
- Execute a structured 90-day launch plan.

# Chapter 1: The Remote Service Business Model

:::objectives
- Define a remote service business and distinguish it from remote employment and from product businesses.
- Describe the size of the self-employed and freelance economy using public data.
- Explain the basic economics of selling time, projects, and retainers.
- Identify the most common reasons small service businesses stall in their first year.
:::

A remote service business sells expertise or labor that is delivered primarily through digital channels: documents, video calls, shared software, and messages. The owner is not an employee of the client. The owner sets the method of work, supplies the tools, takes on the business risk, and is responsible for taxes and compliance. This independence is the attraction, and it is also the burden. An employee who has a slow month still receives a paycheck; a business owner who has a slow month does not.

It is useful to separate three things that are often blurred together. Remote employment means you work for one employer from home; the employer handles payroll taxes, equipment, and benefits. Freelancing means you sell your services to clients as an independent contractor, often project by project, without necessarily building systems or a brand. A remote service business is a deliberate structure: a defined offer, defined clients, written processes, separate finances, and an intention to keep operating even as individual clients come and go. Many people begin as freelancers and become business owners when they add those structures. This book is about making that transition on purpose.

## The Scale of the Self-Employed Economy

The self-employed sector is large. According to the U.S. Census Bureau's 2022 Nonemployer Statistics, the United States had 29.8 million nonemployer businesses, meaning businesses with no paid employees that are subject to federal income tax, and together they accounted for about $1.7 trillion in receipts, roughly 6.8 percent of the economy [1]. The largest sector by number of establishments was Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services, with about 4.0 million nonemployer establishments [1]. That sector includes many of the services this book discusses: design, writing, consulting, bookkeeping support, and technical services.

Survey data on freelancing tell a similar story. Upwork's Freelance Forward 2023 study estimated that 64 million Americans performed freelance work in the prior year, about 38 percent of the U.S. workforce [2]. Survey definitions are broad and include occasional side work, so the figure should be read as an indication of how common independent work has become rather than as a count of full-time businesses.

Two conclusions follow. First, the model is proven: millions of people earn some or all of their income this way. Second, competition is real. A remote service business is rarely competing only with local providers; it is competing with capable people everywhere who have the same software you do. The response is not to compete on price but to compete on specificity, reliability, and trust, which later chapters address.

## Three Ways to Sell a Service

Service businesses generally sell in one of three units.

**Time.** You bill by the hour or day. Hourly billing is simple to explain and fair when scope is uncertain. Its weakness is that income is capped by hours and that efficiency is penalized: the faster you become, the less you earn for the same work.

**Projects.** You quote a fixed price for a defined deliverable, such as a website, a set of documents, or a system setup. Projects reward efficiency and are easy for clients to budget. Their weakness is scope risk: if you underestimate the work, you absorb the overrun.

**Retainers.** The client pays a recurring monthly fee for ongoing access to a defined bundle of work or availability. Retainers smooth income and reduce the time you spend selling. Their weakness is that they require careful scope definition so that the client does not treat a retainer as unlimited availability.

Most durable remote service businesses move over time from hourly work toward projects and retainers, because those units decouple income from hours and make revenue more predictable. Chapter 6 shows how to price each.

## The Economics in One Equation

At its simplest, the annual revenue of a one-person service business equals billable hours multiplied by the effective hourly rate. Billable hours are always far lower than total working hours, because the owner must also sell, invoice, answer email, learn, and rest. A common planning assumption for solo service providers is that 50 to 65 percent of working time is billable once the business is established, and considerably less in the first year, when marketing consumes much of the week.

:::example Kendra's Capacity Arithmetic (illustrative)
Kendra plans to work 40 hours a week for 46 weeks a year, leaving six weeks for holidays, illness, and slow periods. That is 1,840 working hours. She estimates that in her first year only 45 percent of those hours will be billable, because she will spend substantial time on outreach and setup: 1,840 x 0.45 = 828 billable hours. If her effective rate is $55 per hour, her first-year revenue would be 828 x $55 = $45,540 before expenses and taxes. If, in her second year, billable utilization rises to 60 percent (1,104 hours) and she raises her effective rate to $65, revenue would be $71,760. These figures are planning assumptions, not forecasts; her actual results could be higher or lower.
:::

The example shows why the first year is usually the hardest. Revenue depends on utilization, and utilization depends on having enough clients, which depends on marketing that takes time to work.

## Why First-Year Service Businesses Stall

Patterns of failure in small service businesses are well known. The most common include:

1. **A vague offer.** "I do marketing for anyone" is not an offer; it is a job description. Prospects cannot tell whether you solve their problem.
2. **Underpricing.** New owners often set rates near what they earned as employees, forgetting that they now pay both halves of self-employment tax, buy their own equipment and benefits, and cannot bill every hour.
3. **Concentration risk.** One client supplies most of the revenue. When that client leaves, the business collapses.
4. **No pipeline.** Owners stop marketing when they are busy, then face an empty calendar when projects end.
5. **Poor records.** Mixed personal and business finances make taxes stressful and obscure whether the business is profitable.
6. **Scope creep.** Work expands beyond what was agreed, without a corresponding increase in price.

Each of these is addressed directly in later chapters. None requires special talent to avoid; each requires a habit.

:::case Pieter Levels: Publishing the Economics of a Solo Remote Business
Pieter Levels, a Dutch developer, built and operated internet businesses largely by himself while traveling, including Nomad List (now Nomads.com) and the job board Remote OK. In August 2017 he publicly reported that Nomad List, Remote OK, and his book MAKE together generated $34,733 in revenue over the prior 30 days, against monthly expenses of $3,950, which included hosting, email software, domains, and payments to several contractors [3]. He later wrote that it took about four years to reach $1 million in annual revenue with Nomad List and Remote OK [3]. The relevance for a service business is not the product model, which differs from client services, but the discipline: Levels tracked revenue and every recurring cost, kept overhead low relative to revenue, and used contractors for specific tasks rather than hiring early. His results are his own, reflect years of building, and are not typical.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choosing a Service You Can Deliver Remotely

:::objectives
- Inventory your marketable skills systematically.
- Test whether a skill can be delivered fully online and whether buyers already pay for it.
- Screen services for occupational licensing requirements.
- State your service in a single, testable positioning sentence.
:::

The best first remote business sells something you already know how to do well. Learning a new craft and learning to run a business at the same time doubles the difficulty and delays revenue. Your existing experience is also your most credible marketing asset: "I managed scheduling and billing for a six-location dental group for eight years" is more persuasive than any certificate earned last month.

## Step 1: Build a Skills Inventory

Begin with a broad list. Write down every task you have performed competently at work, in school, in volunteer roles, or in a household business. Aim for at least 20 items, and describe them as tasks rather than traits. "Organized" is a trait; "built and maintained a shared calendar for 14 providers" is a task. Tasks are what clients buy.

Group the tasks into clusters. An office manager's list might cluster into scheduling, billing and collections, vendor management, staff onboarding, and software administration. A teacher's list might cluster into curriculum design, written communication, assessment, and parent communication. Clusters often suggest service offers.

## Step 2: Test for Remote Deliverability

For each cluster, ask whether it can be done entirely online. Some services are naturally remote: writing, editing, design, bookkeeping support, research, software configuration, and most administrative work. Others require occasional physical presence, such as photography, equipment installation, or in-person training. Services that need a physical presence can still be part of a business, but they limit your geographic market and complicate the remote model.

## Step 3: Test for Existing Demand

A service with no existing market is a research project, not a business. Look for evidence that people already pay for the service:

- Search freelance marketplaces for the service and note how many providers and listings exist and what they charge.
- Search job boards for part-time or contract roles that describe the same work.
- Ask three people in your target industry whether they currently pay anyone for this task, and how much.
- Look for software tools aimed at the task; vendors build tools where budgets exist.

Competition is a good sign. It means buyers exist. Your task is not to find an empty market but to find a specific segment of a proven market that you can serve better than generalists.

## Step 4: Screen for Licensing

Some services require a state or federal license. Accounting services represented as certified public accounting, legal advice, mental-health counseling, financial and investment advice, insurance sales, real-estate brokerage, and many health-related services are commonly regulated. Licensing rules vary by state and sometimes by city. The U.S. Small Business Administration advises that most businesses need some combination of federal, state, and local licenses and permits, and that requirements depend on the activity and location [4]. Before offering any service that touches a regulated profession, check the licensing board in your state and, if in doubt, consult an attorney.

Many unlicensed people legitimately provide support services adjacent to regulated professions. A bookkeeper who records transactions is distinct from a CPA who issues audited statements; a virtual assistant who schedules appointments for a law office is distinct from a paralegal or attorney. The key is to describe the service precisely and stay within it.

:::example Scoring Three Candidate Services (illustrative)
Kendra scores three candidate services on a 1-to-5 scale across five criteria: her skill level, remote deliverability, evidence of demand, freedom from licensing barriers, and her own interest.

Practice scheduling and front-desk support: skill 5, remote 4, demand 4, licensing 5, interest 4 = 22.
Medical billing and claims follow-up: skill 4, remote 5, demand 5, licensing 3 (she must check certification and privacy obligations), interest 3 = 20.
General social media management: skill 2, remote 5, demand 4, licensing 5, interest 3 = 19.

Kendra chooses operations support, combining scheduling, vendor coordination, and software administration, because it scores highest and draws on her strongest experience. She notes that any work involving patient information will require her to understand privacy obligations under federal health privacy law before she accepts it.
:::

## Step 5: Write the Positioning Sentence

Compress your choice into one sentence: "I help [specific client] with [specific task] so they can [outcome they value]." For Kendra: "I help independent dental and medical practices run a smooth front office by managing scheduling systems, vendor coordination, and practice software, so owners can spend their time on patients rather than administration."

Test the sentence on three people who fit the client description. If they ask clarifying questions about what you do, the sentence is too vague. If they immediately describe a problem they have, it is working.

| Service category | Typical remote fit | Common licensing or compliance issues to check |
|---|---|---|
| Writing and editing | High | Generally none; copyright and contract terms |
| Graphic and web design | High | Generally none; accessibility and copyright |
| Bookkeeping support | High | CPA titles are regulated; tax preparer rules |
| Administrative support | High | Confidentiality; health or legal data rules |
| Marketing operations | High | Advertising and endorsement rules |
| Technical setup and IT | Medium to high | Data security; some states license certain IT work |
| Coaching and consulting | High | Must avoid regulated advice (legal, medical, financial) |
| Tutoring | High | Background checks for minors on some platforms |

# Chapter 3: Defining the Ideal Client and the Offer

:::objectives
- Distinguish a market segment from an ideal client profile.
- Build an ideal client profile using observable criteria.
- Design a productized offer with a defined scope, deliverables, and outcome.
- Apply a value framework to strengthen an offer before pricing it.
:::

A service sold to everyone is effectively sold to no one. Prospects choose providers who appear to understand their specific situation. That perception of fit comes from specificity: a named type of client, a named problem, and a named outcome. This chapter translates your chosen service into an offer that a defined buyer can understand and evaluate quickly.

## Segments and Profiles

A market segment is a broad group, such as "small healthcare practices." An ideal client profile is a narrower description of the clients you most want to serve and are best positioned to help. It uses observable criteria so that you can tell, from public information, whether a prospect fits:

- **Industry and sub-industry**: for example, independent dental practices rather than all healthcare.
- **Size**: number of employees, locations, or approximate revenue.
- **Situation or trigger**: an event that creates urgency, such as opening a second location, switching software, or losing an office manager.
- **Decision-maker**: the person who feels the problem and controls the budget.
- **Ability to pay**: whether the client can afford the service at a sustainable price.

The trigger is often the most useful criterion. A practice that has just lost its office manager has an acute, time-sensitive problem. A practice that is functioning smoothly has no reason to change.

## Understanding the Client's Problem

Before designing an offer, interview prospective clients. The goal is not to sell but to learn. Useful questions include: What takes more of your time than it should? What have you tried? What would it be worth to you to have this solved? Who else is involved in the decision? Record the exact words people use; they become the language of your marketing.

## Productizing the Service

A productized service has a fixed name, scope, deliverables, timeline, and price. Instead of "I can help with whatever you need," the client sees "Front Office Reset: a four-week engagement that audits your scheduling system, documents your front-desk procedures, and trains your staff, for a fixed fee." Productizing has several advantages. It makes buying easier, because the client knows what they are getting. It makes delivery more efficient, because you repeat the same process. It makes pricing easier to defend, because the price is attached to an outcome rather than to hours.

A practical offer ladder for a remote service business contains three levels:

1. **Entry offer**: a small, low-risk engagement, such as an audit or diagnostic, that lets the client experience your work.
2. **Core offer**: the main productized project that solves the central problem.
3. **Continuity offer**: a monthly retainer that maintains the result.

## Strengthening the Offer with a Value Framework

Alex Hormozi, an entrepreneur and author, describes four drivers of perceived value: the dream outcome the client wants, the perceived likelihood that they will achieve it, the time delay before results appear, and the effort and sacrifice required of the client [5]. Value increases when the dream outcome and perceived likelihood rise and when time delay and effort fall. The framework is useful as a design checklist rather than a formula.

| Value driver | Question to ask | Example improvement for Kendra |
|---|---|---|
| Dream outcome | What does the client ultimately want? | Owner leaves the office on time; no double-booked days |
| Perceived likelihood | Why should they believe it will work? | Eight years running a six-location front office; documented process |
| Time delay | How soon will they see progress? | Written audit delivered in week one |
| Effort and sacrifice | How much work does it require of them? | Two 45-minute calls; Kendra drafts all documents |

:::example Designing Kendra's Offer Ladder (illustrative)
Entry offer: "Front Office Diagnostic," a 90-minute recorded review of the practice's scheduling software and procedures, followed by a written report with ten prioritized fixes, for $450.

Core offer: "Front Office Reset," a four-week engagement covering scheduling-template redesign, a written procedures manual, vendor contact consolidation, and two staff training sessions, for $3,200.

Continuity offer: "Front Office Partner," a monthly retainer covering up to 15 hours of software administration, vendor coordination, and procedure updates, with a two-business-day response standard, for $1,100 per month.

If Kendra sold six diagnostics, three resets, and secured two retainers in a quarter, quarterly revenue would be (6 x $450) + (3 x $3,200) + (2 x $1,100 x 3) = $2,700 + $9,600 + $6,600 = $18,900. The figures illustrate how the ladder combines; they are not a projection.
:::

## Writing the Offer Statement

An offer statement is a short paragraph that answers five questions: Who is this for? What problem does it solve? What exactly is delivered? How long does it take? What does it cost, and what happens next? Avoid superlatives and promises of specific financial results. State what you will do and what the client can reasonably expect.

Interviews reveal problems and the client's own language. Productized services package scope, deliverables, timeline, and price into named offers, typically arranged as an entry offer, a core offer, and a continuity offer. # Chapter 4: Setting Up the Business Correctly

:::objectives
- Compare common business structures for a one-person service business.
- Identify the registrations, tax identifiers, and licenses a new business typically needs.
- Separate business and personal finances from the first transaction.
- Evaluate the main types of insurance relevant to remote service providers.
:::

Setup decisions are not glamorous, but they determine how the business is taxed, how much personal risk the owner carries, and how professional the business appears to clients. Most of these steps cost little and can be completed in a few weeks. Skipping them, by contrast, can produce penalties, tangled records, and personal liability. This chapter outlines the typical sequence for a U.S. business; requirements vary by state and locality, so confirm each step with the relevant agency.

## Choosing a Business Structure

The U.S. Small Business Administration describes the main structures available to small businesses, including sole proprietorships, partnerships, limited liability companies (LLCs), and corporations, and notes that the choice affects day-to-day operations, taxes, and how much of your personal assets are at risk [6].

**Sole proprietorship.** The simplest form. If you do business under your own name and do nothing else, you are usually a sole proprietor by default. Profit is reported on your personal tax return. The drawback is that there is no legal separation between you and the business; you are personally responsible for its debts and obligations [6].

**Limited liability company.** An LLC is formed by filing with your state and paying a fee. It can offer protection of personal assets from many business liabilities, provided you keep finances separate and follow formalities [6]. By default, a single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship, though it may elect other tax treatment. State fees vary considerably, and some states charge an annual franchise tax or minimum fee regardless of income, so the cost of an LLC should be weighed against its benefits.

**Corporation and S corporation election.** Corporations involve more formality. Some LLC owners later elect S corporation tax treatment to manage self-employment tax once profits are substantial; that decision depends on income level, payroll costs, and state rules and should be made with a tax professional.

| Structure | Formation | Liability protection | Default federal tax treatment | Typical fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | None required beyond local licenses | None | Owner's personal return (Schedule C) | Testing a service; very low risk work |
| Single-member LLC | State filing and fee | Generally yes, if formalities kept | Same as sole proprietorship unless elected otherwise | Most solo service businesses |
| Multi-member LLC | State filing and operating agreement | Generally yes | Partnership return | Two or more owners |
| Corporation | State filing, bylaws, board | Yes | Corporate return (C or S election) | Higher profit or outside investors |

## Naming and Registering

If you operate under any name other than your own legal name, most states and counties require you to register an assumed name, also called a "doing business as" (DBA) name. An LLC's legal name is registered when it is formed. Before choosing a name, search your state's business-entity database, check that a matching domain name is available, and run a basic trademark search to avoid conflicts.

## Tax Identification

The Internal Revenue Service issues Employer Identification Numbers (EINs) free of charge through its website [7]. Sole proprietors without employees are not always required to obtain one, but many do so in order to avoid giving clients their Social Security number on tax forms, and banks commonly ask for one when opening a business account. The IRS notes that applying is free; be wary of websites that charge to obtain an EIN on your behalf [7].

## Licenses and Permits

The SBA notes that almost every business needs some type of license or permit, and that requirements depend on the business activity and location [4]. A home-based service business may need a city or county business license, and some localities require a home-occupation permit. Professional licenses apply to regulated services discussed in Chapter 2. Check with your city or county clerk and your state's business portal.

## Separating Finances

Open a dedicated business checking account before you accept your first payment. The SBA explains that a business bank account provides legal protection, makes taxes simpler, and makes the business appear more professional [8]. Route all business income into this account and pay all business expenses from it. If you need to move money to yourself, record it as an owner's draw. Mixing personal and business transactions is one of the main reasons LLC owners can lose liability protection and one of the main reasons tax preparation becomes expensive.

## Insurance

The SBA describes several common types of business insurance, including general liability, professional liability, and business owner's policies, and notes that some coverage may be required by law or by contract [9]. For a remote service provider, the most relevant are:

- **Professional liability (errors and omissions)**: covers claims that your advice or work caused a client financial loss. Many corporate clients require it in contracts.
- **General liability**: covers bodily injury and property damage claims; less central for purely remote work but sometimes required by clients.
- **Cyber liability**: covers costs arising from data breaches, which matter if you handle client records or credentials.
- **Home-based business coverage**: standard homeowners or renters policies often limit coverage for business equipment; a rider or separate policy may be needed.

:::example Kendra's Setup Budget (illustrative)
Kendra lives in a state where LLC formation costs $100 and an annual report costs $50. Her city requires a $75 business license. She obtains a free EIN from the IRS. She opens a business checking account with no monthly fee if she maintains a $500 balance. She obtains a quote of $45 per month for a professional liability policy with $1 million in coverage, and adds a $15 per month renters rider for business equipment. Her first-year setup and insurance cost: $100 + $50 + $75 + ($45 x 12) + ($15 x 12) = $945. These figures are hypothetical; fees vary widely by state, city, and insurer.
:::

- Decide on a business structure after reading the SBA guidance and, if needed, consulting a professional.
- Search the state entity database, domain availability, and trademark records for the chosen name.
- Form the LLC or register the DBA name, as applicable.
- Obtain a free EIN directly from the IRS website.
- Apply for city or county business licenses and any home-occupation permit.
- Confirm whether any professional license applies to your service.
- Open a dedicated business bank account and set up a separate business card if needed.
- Obtain insurance quotes for professional liability and home-based equipment.
- Create a folder (digital and paper) for formation documents, licenses, and policies.
- Put annual renewal dates on your calendar.
:::

Evaluate professional liability, cyber, and home-based equipment insurance. Calendar every renewal.

# Chapter 5: The Home Office, Tool Stack, and Security

:::objectives
- Set up a home workspace that supports focused, professional work.
- Understand the IRS rules for the home office deduction, including the simplified method.
- Select a lean software stack for communication, delivery, and administration.
- Apply basic cybersecurity practices recommended for small businesses.
:::

A remote business runs on three physical and digital assets: a workspace, a set of tools, and the data those tools hold. Each should be chosen deliberately, at the lowest cost that supports professional work, and each should be protected.

## The Workspace

A productive workspace needs a door or other boundary, a reliable internet connection with a backup option, adequate lighting, a camera and microphone that make you look and sound professional on calls, and ergonomic seating. A dedicated space also has a tax consequence, discussed below. If a dedicated room is not possible, designate a specific desk area used only for work, and use headphones and a neutral background for calls.

Connectivity deserves special attention. A missed client call because of an outage is costly. Many remote workers keep a mobile hotspot plan as a backup and know the nearest library or coworking space with reliable Wi-Fi.

## The Home Office Deduction

The IRS allows self-employed people to deduct expenses for business use of the home if part of the home is used exclusively and on a regular basis as the principal place of business, among other qualifying tests [10]. "Exclusively" means that the space is not also used for personal purposes; a dining table used for family meals usually does not qualify.

There are two methods. Under the simplified method, the deduction equals $5 per square foot of the home used for business, up to a maximum of 300 square feet, so the maximum deduction under this method is $1,500 [10]. The simplified method requires less recordkeeping, but it allows no depreciation deduction and does not permit the carryover of amounts that exceed the gross-income limit [10]. Under the regular method, you calculate the actual expenses of the home (such as rent or mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, and repairs) and deduct the business-use percentage, which requires more records but may produce a larger deduction. IRS Publication 587 explains both methods in detail [11]. Employees working from home for an employer generally cannot claim the deduction; it applies to self-employment [10].

:::example Comparing Home Office Methods (illustrative)
Kendra rents a 1,000-square-foot apartment and uses a 120-square-foot room exclusively for her business. Under the simplified method, her deduction is 120 x $5 = $600. Under the regular method, her business-use percentage is 120 / 1,000 = 12 percent. If her annual rent is $18,000, utilities $2,400, and renters insurance $300, total home expenses are $20,700, and 12 percent is $2,484. Assuming she meets the qualifying tests and her business income exceeds the deduction, the regular method yields a larger deduction but requires her to keep records of all those expenses. A tax professional can confirm which method fits her situation.
:::

## The Lean Tool Stack

New owners often overspend on software. Start with the minimum set that covers each function, and add tools only when a specific problem justifies them.

| Function | What it must do | Typical options |
|---|---|---|
| Email and calendar | Professional address on your own domain; shared scheduling | Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 |
| Video calls | Reliable calls, screen sharing, recording with consent | Zoom, Google Meet, Microsoft Teams |
| File storage | Shared folders, version history, access control | Google Drive, OneDrive, Dropbox |
| Project tracking | Tasks, due dates, client visibility | Trello, Asana, Basecamp, Notion |
| Scheduling | Clients book calls without email back-and-forth | Calendly, Google appointment schedules |
| Contracts and signatures | Send and sign agreements electronically | DocuSign, Dropbox Sign, built-in tools in invoicing software |
| Invoicing and payments | Send invoices, accept card and bank payments | QuickBooks, FreshBooks, Wave, Stripe |
| Password management | Store and share credentials securely | 1Password, Bitwarden |
| Bookkeeping | Categorize income and expenses | QuickBooks, Xero, Wave |

## Cybersecurity Basics

Clients increasingly entrust remote providers with passwords, customer records, and financial data. A breach can damage the client and end the relationship. The Federal Trade Commission provides cybersecurity guidance specifically for small businesses, covering basics such as keeping software updated, backing up files, requiring passwords on devices, using multi-factor authentication, securing routers, and training anyone who works with you to recognize phishing [12]. The FTC's "Start with Security" guide also distills lessons from enforcement cases, including collecting only the data you need, limiting access to sensitive data, and requiring secure passwords and authentication [13].

For a solo provider, a practical baseline is:

1. Use a password manager and unique passwords for every account.
2. Turn on multi-factor authentication for email, banking, cloud storage, and client systems.
3. Enable automatic updates for operating systems and browsers.
4. Encrypt laptop drives and require a screen lock.
5. Back up work files automatically, with at least one copy separate from your main device.
6. Never ask clients to email passwords; use a password manager's sharing feature or the client's own access controls.
7. Remove your access to client systems when an engagement ends, and document that you have done so.

:::case Basecamp and the Fully Distributed Company
37signals, the company behind the project-management software Basecamp, states in its public employee handbook that it is a fully distributed company with staff across five continents, and that it has been remote since it started [14]. Its founders, Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, wrote "Remote: Office Not Required" (2013), which argues that much knowledge work can be done well without a shared office when teams rely on clear written communication and trust [15]. For a solo remote business, the relevance is twofold. First, a client who hires a remote provider is effectively joining a small distributed team, so the provider's written communication and tools are part of the service. Second, the company's emphasis on calm, written, asynchronous work shows that remote delivery can be a deliberate operating choice rather than a compromise. The company's experience is its own and reflects its particular products and culture.
:::

:::checklist Workspace and Security Setup

- Record the square footage of the workspace and of the home.
- Test internet speed and arrange a backup connection.
- Set up a professional email address on your own domain.

:::

Build a lean tool stack that covers each business function and protect client data with the FTC's basic cybersecurity practices, including multi-factor authentication, updates, backups, and careful access management.

# Chapter 6: Pricing Your Service

:::objectives
- Calculate a minimum viable hourly rate from target income, costs, taxes, and capacity.
- Convert an hourly floor into project and retainer prices.
- Apply value-based reasoning to price above the floor where justified.
- Plan and communicate price increases.
:::

Pricing is where many new service businesses fail quietly. A rate that feels generous compared with a former salary can be insufficient once self-employment tax, unbillable time, equipment, software, insurance, and benefits are accounted for. Price should begin with arithmetic, then be adjusted upward for value and market position. It should never begin with a guess about what competitors charge, because you do not know their costs, utilization, or results.

## Step 1: Set the Target Owner Income

Decide what annual personal income the business must provide. Include the cost of benefits you previously received from an employer, such as health insurance and retirement contributions, because the business must now fund them.

## Step 2: Estimate Business Expenses

List annual business costs: software, insurance, equipment replacement, professional fees, licenses, marketing, training, and payment-processing fees (often around 3 percent of card payments).

## Step 3: Account for Taxes

Self-employed people pay self-employment tax for Social Security and Medicare in addition to income tax. The Taxpayer Advocate Service explains that self-employment tax generally applies if net self-employment earnings are $400 or more, and that half of the self-employment tax is deductible in computing adjusted gross income [16]. Federal and state income taxes are additional. For planning purposes, many owners set aside 25 to 30 percent of net profit for taxes; the right figure depends on income, location, and deductions and should be confirmed with a tax professional.

## Step 4: Estimate Billable Hours

Multiply working weeks by weekly hours, then by expected utilization (Chapter 1). Be conservative in the first year.

## Step 5: Calculate the Floor Rate

Floor rate = (target owner income + business expenses) / (1 - tax reserve percentage) / billable hours.

The floor rate is the minimum you must earn per billable hour for the business to meet its goals. It is not your price. It is the line below which you should not go except deliberately, for example in a short-term pilot.

:::example Kendra's Floor Rate (illustrative)
Kendra's target owner income is $52,000, plus $7,200 for health insurance and $3,000 for retirement contributions, for a total of $62,200. Her business expenses are $6,800. Combined, she needs $69,000 before taxes. Using a 28 percent tax reserve: $69,000 / 0.72 = $95,833 in required revenue. With 1,840 working hours and 50 percent planned utilization, she has 920 billable hours. Her floor rate is $95,833 / 920 = about $104 per hour.

This is much higher than the $55 she had assumed in Chapter 1. The gap shows why so many new owners struggle: at $55 per hour and 920 billable hours, revenue would be $50,600, and after expenses and taxes she would keep far less than her $62,200 target. She has three options: raise her prices, increase billable hours through better utilization, or accept a lower income during the first year while building toward the target. The arithmetic makes the trade-off visible.
:::

## Converting to Project and Retainer Prices

**Project pricing.** Estimate the hours a project will take, add a contingency of 15 to 25 percent for uncertainty, and multiply by your rate. Then consider the value of the outcome to the client and adjust upward if the value justifies it. Define scope precisely, and state how changes will be priced.

**Retainer pricing.** Define what the retainer includes (hours, deliverables, response times) and price it at your rate multiplied by expected hours, often with a modest discount in exchange for the predictability of recurring revenue. Specify whether unused hours roll over (usually they do not, or only for one month) and how excess work is billed.

| Pricing unit | Formula starting point | Key protection |
|---|---|---|
| Hourly | Floor rate or higher | Minimum billing increment; written estimate |
| Project | (Estimated hours x rate) x (1 + contingency) | Detailed scope; change-order process |
| Retainer | Expected monthly hours x rate, less a small discount | Defined inclusions; rollover and overage rules |
| Value-based | A share of measurable value to the client | Agreed baseline and measurement method |

## Value-Based Reasoning

When your work produces a measurable result, such as hours saved, errors avoided, or revenue protected, you can price relative to that value rather than to your hours. A front-office redesign that saves a practice owner five hours a week has a value that can be estimated. Value-based pricing requires a credible estimate agreed with the client and should never involve guaranteeing financial outcomes you do not control. Use it to justify pricing above your floor, not to make promises.

## Discounts and Price Increases

Avoid discounting your core offer to win business; it trains clients to expect lower prices and attracts price-sensitive buyers. If you need to reduce the price, reduce the scope. Review prices at least annually. Give existing clients written notice, typically 30 to 60 days, explain what is changing, and apply new rates to new clients first.

# Chapter 7: Finding Your First Clients

:::objectives
- Build a prospect list from warm network relationships.
- Write outreach messages that are specific, respectful, and easy to answer.
- Evaluate freelance platforms, referrals, partnerships, and content as client channels.
- Recognize and avoid work-from-home and business-opportunity scams.
:::

For most new service businesses, first clients come from people who already know and trust the owner, or from people those contacts refer. Trust is the scarcest resource in buying a service from a stranger over the internet; a warm introduction transfers some of it. Paid advertising and elaborate funnels rarely make sense before you have an offer that has been proven with real clients.

## Step 1: Build the Warm List

List every professional contact you can reach: former colleagues and managers, vendors and clients from previous jobs, classmates, members of associations, and friends who work in or near your target industry. A list of 100 names is a reasonable goal. Mark each contact as a potential client, a potential referrer, or both.

## Step 2: Write Specific, Respectful Outreach

A good outreach message is short, personal, and asks for something easy. It explains what you now do, for whom, and asks either for a conversation or for an introduction. It does not attach a long brochure or pressure the reader.

:::example Outreach Message and Conversion Arithmetic (illustrative)
Kendra writes: "Hi Marcus, it was great working with you at the dental group. I've started a small business helping independent practices tidy up their front office: scheduling templates, procedures, and software setup. Do you know one or two practice owners who have mentioned that kind of headache? I'd be grateful for an introduction, and happy to return the favor."

She sends 80 personalized messages over four weeks. Suppose 30 people reply (37.5 percent), 12 make introductions or agree to a conversation, 8 conversations happen, 3 result in a paid diagnostic, and 1 diagnostic converts to a Front Office Reset. Revenue from the campaign would be (3 x $450) + $3,200 = $4,550. These rates are illustrative; real response rates vary widely by relationship strength and industry.
:::

## Step 3: Use Additional Channels Deliberately

**Referrals and partnerships.** Identify businesses that serve your ideal client but do not compete with you: for Kendra, dental-software consultants, accountants who specialize in practices, and equipment vendors. Offer to share referrals in both directions.

**Freelance platforms.** Marketplaces such as Upwork and Fiverr can supply early work and testimonials. They also involve platform fees, price competition, and less control over the client relationship. Treat them as one channel, not the whole business.

**Professional communities and associations.** Industry associations, online groups, and local business networks allow you to demonstrate expertise by answering questions helpfully.

**Content.** Short, useful writing on LinkedIn or a simple website (checklists, explanations of common problems, before-and-after process descriptions) builds credibility over time. Content compounds slowly; it rarely produces clients in the first month.

| Channel | Speed to first client | Cost | Control of relationship | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Warm network | Fast | Time only | High | First 1-5 clients |
| Referral partners | Medium | Time; reciprocal referrals | High | Steady pipeline |
| Freelance platforms | Fast to medium | Platform fees | Low to medium | Early work and reviews |
| Associations and communities | Medium | Membership fees | High | Credibility in a niche |
| Content and website | Slow | Time | High | Long-term inbound demand |
| Paid advertising | Variable | Money | High | After the offer is proven |

## The Discovery Conversation

When a prospect agrees to talk, your aim is to understand their situation and decide together whether you can help. A simple structure: confirm the time available; ask about the current situation and what prompted the conversation; explore the impact of the problem; describe how you would approach it; state the relevant offer and price; agree on a next step. Follow up in writing the same day with a short summary and proposal.

## Avoiding Scams

People starting home-based businesses are frequent targets of fraud. The Federal Trade Commission warns that work-from-home job scams often promise high earnings for little work and may ask you to pay for training, starter kits, or certifications, or to receive and reship packages or deposit checks and send money onward [17]. The FTC's broader guidance on job and money-making scams notes that ads promising big money working from home or offering help starting an online business are common lures [18]. Legitimate clients pay you; they do not ask you to pay them to start. Be cautious of any "client" who sends a check for more than the invoice and asks you to refund the difference, a pattern associated with fake-check scams.

:::checklist First-Client Campaign
- Build a warm list of at least 100 names and tag each as client, referrer, or both.
- Write a two-to-four sentence outreach message and personalize every send.
- Send 15 to 20 messages per week and track replies in a simple spreadsheet.
- Identify five potential referral partners and contact each personally.
- Prepare a discovery-call outline and a one-page proposal template.
- Follow up once, politely, after five to seven business days.
- Ask every satisfied client for a referral and, with permission, a testimonial.
- Refuse any arrangement that requires you to pay upfront fees or forward funds.
:::

:::summary
First clients usually come from warm relationships and referrals because trust is scarce online. Build a list of about 100 contacts, send specific and respectful outreach that asks for a conversation or an introduction, and track results. Add referral partnerships, platforms, associations, and content as deliberate channels, recognizing their differences in speed, cost, and control. Run discovery conversations with a simple structure and follow up in writing. Avoid scams that require upfront payments or the forwarding of funds.
:::

# Chapter 8: Agreements, Invoicing, and Getting Paid

:::objectives
- Identify the essential clauses of a service agreement.
- Manage scope with a written change-order process.
- Design invoices and payment terms that encourage on-time payment.
- Understand basic information-reporting rules for payments to independent contractors.
:::

A handshake is not a business system. Written agreements protect both parties by setting expectations before work begins, when goodwill is highest. They also make collection easier, because there is no dispute about what was promised. This chapter outlines what a simple service agreement should contain and how to get paid reliably. It is not a substitute for a contract drafted or reviewed by an attorney licensed in your state.

## Essential Clauses

A service agreement for a small remote business typically includes:

1. **Parties and effective date.** The legal names of you (or your LLC) and the client.
2. **Scope of work.** The specific services and deliverables, with enough detail that a third party could tell whether they were delivered. Attach the proposal as an exhibit if helpful.
3. **Exclusions.** What is not included. This is as important as what is.
4. **Timeline and dependencies.** Key dates, and what the client must provide (access, information, approvals) for you to meet them.
5. **Fees and payment terms.** Amounts, deposit, due dates, accepted payment methods, and late-payment terms.
6. **Changes.** How changes to scope will be requested, priced, and approved.
7. **Confidentiality.** Both parties' obligations regarding confidential information, including how client data will be handled and returned or destroyed.
8. **Intellectual property.** Who owns the deliverables and when ownership transfers (often upon full payment), and any rights you retain to your pre-existing tools and templates.
9. **Independent contractor relationship.** A statement that you are not an employee and control the means of performing the work.
10. **Limitation of liability.** A cap on damages, often limited to fees paid, where enforceable.
11. **Termination.** How either party may end the agreement, notice periods, and payment for work completed.
12. **Governing law and dispute resolution.** Which state's law applies and how disputes will be handled.

## Scope and Change Orders

Scope creep is the gradual expansion of work without a corresponding change in price. It usually begins with small, reasonable requests. The remedy is a simple written process: when a request falls outside the agreed scope, acknowledge it, describe the additional work, estimate its price and effect on timeline, and ask for written approval before starting. Many providers keep a short change-order template ready.

:::example A Change Order in Practice (illustrative)
During a Front Office Reset priced at $3,200, the client asks Kendra to also redesign the practice's new-patient intake forms. That work was not in scope. Kendra replies the same day: "Happy to help with the intake forms. That's outside the Reset scope, so here's a change order: redesign of four intake forms, one revision round, estimated 6 hours at $110 per hour, $660, adding three business days to the timeline. Reply 'approved' and I'll begin after the training session." The client approves. Total engagement revenue becomes $3,860, and both parties have a clear record.
:::

## Invoicing and Payment Terms

Late payment is a cash-flow risk for small businesses. Several practices reduce it:

- **Deposits.** Require a deposit (commonly 30 to 50 percent) before starting project work.
- **Milestones.** Tie later payments to specific deliverables rather than to completion of the entire project.
- **Retainers in advance.** Bill monthly retainers at the start of the month, not the end.
- **Short terms.** Use net-7 or net-15 terms rather than net-30 where the market allows.
- **Easy payment.** Accept bank transfer and card payments; automatic billing for retainers reduces friction.
- **Clear invoices.** Include your business name, the client's name, invoice number, date, due date, line items that match the agreement, total, and payment instructions.
- **Consistent follow-up.** Send a friendly reminder on the due date, a firmer reminder at seven days late, and a call at 14 days. Pause work on accounts that are significantly overdue if the agreement allows.

| Payment structure | When to use | Cash-flow effect |
|---|---|---|
| 50% deposit, 50% on delivery | Short projects under 4 weeks | Half of cash before work begins |
| 40/30/30 milestones | Projects of 4 to 12 weeks | Cash tracks progress |
| Monthly in advance | Retainers | Predictable, front-loaded |
| Hourly, billed every two weeks | Uncertain scope | Limits exposure to unpaid hours |

## Information Reporting

Business clients may report payments to you on Form 1099-NEC. According to IRS instructions, for tax years beginning after 2025, the minimum threshold for reporting certain payments, including nonemployee compensation on Form 1099-NEC, increased to $2,000, and may be adjusted for inflation beginning in 2027 [19]. Regardless of whether you receive a form, all business income is taxable and must be reported. Clients will usually ask you to complete Form W-9 to provide your taxpayer identification number, which is one reason to obtain an EIN (Chapter 4). If you later pay subcontractors, you may have reporting obligations of your own [20].

:::checklist Agreement and Payment Readiness
- Draft a service agreement template and have it reviewed by an attorney.
- Prepare a proposal template that can be attached as a scope exhibit.
- Write a change-order template.
- Set up invoicing software with numbered invoices and online payment.
- Decide on deposit, milestone, and retainer billing rules.
- Prepare a completed Form W-9 using your EIN.
- Write three payment-reminder templates (due date, 7 days late, 14 days late).
:::

:::summary
A written service agreement sets expectations on scope, exclusions, timeline, fees, changes, confidentiality, intellectual property, independent status, liability, termination, and governing law. A change-order process prevents scope creep. Deposits, milestones, retainers billed in advance, short terms, easy payment methods, clear invoices, and consistent follow-up reduce late payment. For payments after 2025, the 1099-NEC reporting threshold is $2,000, but all income is taxable whether or not a form is issued.
:::

# Chapter 9: Delivering Excellent Work from a Distance

:::objectives
- Explain the difference between synchronous and asynchronous communication and when to use each.
- Design a client onboarding process that sets expectations from day one.
- Build a delivery rhythm using written updates, documented procedures, and defined response times.
- Collect feedback and measure client outcomes.
:::

Clients who hire a remote provider cannot see them working. Their confidence depends almost entirely on communication: whether updates arrive when promised, whether messages are clear, and whether problems are raised early. Delivery excellence in a remote business is therefore as much about communication design as about the underlying skill.

## Synchronous and Asynchronous Communication

Synchronous communication happens in real time: calls, video meetings, live chat. Asynchronous communication does not require both parties to be present at once: email, shared documents, recorded video, project-management comments. GitLab, one of the largest all-remote companies, with team members in more than 65 countries, describes asynchronous communication as a starting point and emphasizes communicating through written, transparent channels [21][22]. Its handbook advises defaulting to asynchronous communication for status updates, decisions with clear context, and anything that benefits from being searchable later, and writing as if the reader has none of your context [23].

Amir Salihefendic, founder of the remote software company Doist, has written that asynchronous communication drives both productivity and well-being by protecting time for focused work, and that many routine meetings can be replaced by written threads [24].

For a solo provider, a sensible default is: use asynchronous channels for updates, questions that are not urgent, and deliverables; use synchronous calls for kickoff, complex discussion, conflict, and relationship building.

:::case GitLab: Writing Things Down
GitLab has operated as an all-remote company since its inception and publishes its operating handbook openly [21]. A central practice is documentation: decisions, processes, and expectations are written in a shared handbook so that people can work without waiting for a meeting. The company's guidance on sync versus async communication recommends defaulting to asynchronous work for status, information, and proposals, and reserving real-time conversation for situations that genuinely need it [23]. A one-person business can apply the same principle at small scale: write a short client handbook covering how to reach you, response times, where files live, and how approvals work. GitLab's practices reflect a large organization and are adapted here, not copied; its results are its own.
:::

## Client Onboarding

The first two weeks of an engagement shape the client's perception of the entire relationship. A good onboarding process includes:

1. **Welcome message** within one business day of signing, confirming next steps.
2. **Intake questionnaire** gathering information you need, so the kickoff call is efficient.
3. **Access checklist** listing the accounts and files you need, with secure methods for sharing them.
4. **Kickoff call** to confirm goals, success measures, timeline, and communication norms.
5. **Client handbook** of one or two pages: how to contact you, expected response times, working hours, where documents live, and how to request changes.
6. **First visible deliverable** within the first week, even if small, to demonstrate momentum.

## Delivery Rhythm

Predictability builds trust. Choose a rhythm and keep it:

- A weekly written update on the same day each week, covering what was done, what is next, and what you need from the client.
- A defined response-time standard, such as one business day for email and two business days for non-urgent requests.
- A shared task board where the client can see status without asking.
- Documented procedures for recurring work, so quality is consistent and the work could be delegated later.

:::example A Weekly Update and Time Budget (illustrative)
Kendra's Friday update to a retainer client reads: "Done this week: rebuilt the hygiene schedule template; consolidated vendor contacts into one sheet (link); resolved the billing-software login issue. Next week: draft the new-patient procedure; review supply reorder points. Needed from you: approval of the hygiene template by Wednesday. Hours used this month: 9 of 15."

Writing and sending the update takes her 15 minutes per client per week. With four retainer clients, that is one hour per week, or about 4.3 hours per month. At her $110 rate, that time is worth about $473 per month across clients, which she treats as part of the service rather than unbilled overhead, because the updates are the main reason clients renew.
:::

| Situation | Preferred mode | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly status | Written update | Searchable record; respects client time |
| Kickoff | Video call | Builds relationship; resolves ambiguity |
| Simple question | Message or comment | Does not interrupt either party |
| Complex trade-off | Short call, then written summary | Discussion plus record |
| Bad news or conflict | Call, then written summary | Tone matters; record protects both |
| Deliverable handoff | Recorded walkthrough plus document | Client can review at their pace |

## Feedback and Outcomes

Ask for feedback at defined points: after the first deliverable, at the end of a project, and quarterly for retainers. Two questions are enough: what is working well, and what one change would make the service more valuable? Where possible, measure outcomes the client cares about, such as hours saved, error rates, or turnaround times, using a baseline taken at the start. Record results honestly; they will inform future offers and, with permission, testimonials. Do not present one client's results as typical.

A structured onboarding process, a client handbook, a weekly update rhythm, response-time standards, and documented procedures make delivery predictable. Collect feedback at defined points and measure outcomes against a baseline.

# Chapter 10: Money, Taxes, and Operating Metrics

:::objectives
- Set up a simple bookkeeping routine and keep records the IRS expects.
- Plan for self-employment tax and quarterly estimated payments.
- Build a basic cash-flow forecast and reserve policy.
- Track a small set of operating metrics that indicate business health.
:::

Profit is not the money in the bank account; it is what remains after every cost and tax is accounted for. Many owners discover at tax time that they spent money they owed the government. A weekly bookkeeping habit, a tax reserve, and a handful of metrics prevent that surprise.

## Recordkeeping

The IRS explains that good records help you monitor the progress of your business, prepare financial statements, identify sources of income, keep track of deductible expenses, and support items reported on your tax return [25]. Keep invoices, receipts, bank and card statements, contracts, and mileage or home-office records. Bookkeeping software connected to your business bank account can categorize most transactions automatically; review and correct the categories weekly.

## Self-Employment and Estimated Taxes

Self-employed people generally pay tax through quarterly estimated payments rather than paycheck withholding. The IRS Self-Employed Individuals Tax Center describes the obligation to pay self-employment tax and estimated tax and provides the relevant forms and due dates [26]. As noted in Chapter 6, self-employment tax applies when net earnings reach $400 or more [16]. Missing estimated payments can lead to penalties. A practical habit is to transfer a fixed percentage of every payment received, such as 25 to 30 percent, into a separate tax savings account, and to pay estimates from that account each quarter. Confirm the appropriate percentage and payment amounts with a tax professional.

## The Profit-First Habit of Allocation

Some owners allocate each deposit into separate accounts: operating expenses, taxes, owner pay, and reserves. The specific percentages matter less than the habit of deciding in advance where each dollar goes.

:::example Monthly Allocation and Reserve (illustrative)
In a given month, Kendra collects $7,400. Her allocation policy is: 28 percent to the tax account ($2,072), 10 percent to an operating reserve ($740), 12 percent to operating expenses ($888), and 50 percent to owner pay ($3,700). Her reserve goal is three months of business expenses plus three months of minimum owner pay: (3 x $570) + (3 x $3,000) = $10,710. At $740 per month, the reserve would take about 14.5 months to fill. She decides to direct any month's collections above $8,000 entirely to the reserve until it is full.
:::

## Cash-Flow Forecasting

A simple 13-week cash-flow forecast lists expected receipts and payments by week. It reveals gaps before they become crises. Update it weekly with actual figures. Include known tax payment dates and annual costs such as insurance renewals and state fees.

## Operating Metrics

Track a small number of metrics consistently. Too many metrics produce noise.

| Metric | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly revenue | Total invoiced or collected per month | Basic scale |
| Effective hourly rate | Revenue divided by total hours worked (billable and not) | Reveals underpricing and scope creep |
| Billable utilization | Billable hours divided by total working hours | Capacity and efficiency |
| Client concentration | Largest client's share of revenue | Risk if one client leaves; many owners aim to keep it under 30-40% |
| Pipeline value | Sum of open proposals weighted by likelihood | Predicts future revenue |
| Days sales outstanding | Average days from invoice to payment | Cash-flow health |
| Retention | Share of retainer clients renewing each quarter | Delivery quality and stability |

:::worksheet Monthly Financial Review
Revenue collected this month: ___

Effective hourly rate: ___
Largest client's share of revenue: ___
Open proposals and weighted pipeline value: ___
Invoices overdue and total amount: ___

Reserve balance and months of coverage: ___

:::

:::summary
Keep records that support every item on your tax return, review bookkeeping weekly, and separate a tax reserve from every payment received. Self-employed people generally owe self-employment tax and make quarterly estimated payments. Allocate deposits by policy, maintain a cash reserve, and keep a 13-week cash-flow forecast. Track a small set of metrics, including effective hourly rate, utilization, client concentration, pipeline value, days sales outstanding, and retention.
:::

# Chapter 11: Systems, Delegation, and Growth

:::objectives
- Document recurring work as standard operating procedures.
- Use a delegation framework to decide which tasks to hand off.
- Compare growth paths for a remote service business.
- Recognize the operational and legal considerations of working with contractors.
:::

A one-person business has a natural ceiling: the owner's hours. Growth beyond that ceiling requires raising prices, productizing delivery, or adding people. Each path depends on systems, meaning written procedures that let work be performed consistently by someone other than the original expert, or by the expert with less effort.

## Documenting Procedures

A standard operating procedure (SOP) describes a recurring task: its purpose, trigger, steps, tools, quality standard, and owner. Short screen recordings with narration are an efficient way to create first drafts. A good test is whether a capable person unfamiliar with the task could complete it from the SOP alone.

## Delegation Using the Buyback Framework

Dan Martell, an entrepreneur who founded SaaS Academy, a coaching program for software founders, popularized a framework for deciding what to delegate in his book "Buy Back Your Time." In an interview with Joe Polish of Genius Network, Martell describes a loop of auditing how time is spent, transferring tasks to others, and filling the freed time with higher-value work, and describes recording a task while performing it so that the recording becomes a training resource, which he calls the Camcorder Method [27]. Martell's "buyback rate" is a guide to how much to pay others: roughly one quarter of what your own hour is worth, so that delegation produces a clear return [28].

:::case Dan Martell's Buyback Loop
Martell launched SaaS Academy in 2015 [29], and it later became one of the largest coaching programs for business-to-business software founders. In 2026, Precision announced that it had acquired SaaS Academy [30]. Martell's core argument is that founders stall when they personally perform low-value tasks, and that the remedy is a deliberate cycle: audit a typical two weeks of work, label tasks by the energy and value they produce, transfer the lowest-value tasks using recorded demonstrations, and fill the freed time with work that only the founder can do [27]. His buyback rate (annual income divided by 2,000 hours, then divided by four) provides a quick test of whether paying someone for a task makes economic sense [28]. Martell's experience and income are his own and are not typical; the framework is presented as a decision aid.
:::

:::example Applying the Buyback Rate (illustrative)
Kendra's business generates $96,000 in annual revenue. Using Martell's formula: $96,000 / 2,000 = $48 per hour, divided by four = $12 per hour as a guide. In practice, she cannot find reliable help at $12 per hour for bookkeeping, so she uses the formula differently: she identifies tasks consuming her time that a contractor could perform at $25 per hour, such as invoice follow-up and data entry, which take her five hours per week. Delegating them costs $125 per week. If she uses the freed five hours to deliver billable work at $110 per hour, the gross return is $550 per week, a net gain of $425, assuming she has enough demand to fill the time. Without that demand, delegation would only add cost.
:::

## Growth Paths

| Path | How it works | Advantages | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raise prices | Increase rates for new and then existing clients | Fastest margin improvement | Some client loss; requires strong reputation |
| Productize | Standardize offers and processes | Efficiency; easier to sell and delegate | Less flexibility; requires discipline |
| Subcontract | Use independent contractors for defined tasks | Flexible capacity | Quality control; classification rules |
| Hire employees | Add staff on payroll | Control; loyalty; scale | Payroll obligations; fixed cost |
| Add digital products | Templates, courses, or tools | Revenue not tied to hours | Requires audience; different skills |

## Contractors and Classification

Working with independent contractors is common in remote businesses, but workers must be classified correctly under federal and state law. Misclassifying an employee as a contractor can lead to back taxes and penalties. Generally, the more control a business has over how, when, and where work is done, the more likely the worker is an employee. Rules differ by state, and some states apply stricter tests. Consult a professional before engaging regular help, and use written contractor agreements that define deliverables rather than hours and methods where appropriate.

:::case Zapier: Remote by Circumstance, Then by Design
Zapier, the workflow-automation company founded in 2011 by Wade Foster, Bryan Helmig, and Mike Knoop, began as a nights-and-weekends side project [31]. In a 2018 interview, Foster explained that after one co-founder moved back to Missouri, the team returned to the remote way of working they had used as a side project, and that early hires were former colleagues in other states, which pushed the company further toward a distributed model [31]. At that time Zapier had grown to about 180 employees across 17 countries and 25 states, with no headquarters, and its "delocation package" for employees willing to relocate reportedly increased job applications by more than 50 percent [31]. The lesson for a small remote business is that hiring people you have worked with before reduces the risk of remote collaboration, and that remote work widens the available talent pool. Zapier's growth reflects venture-scale software economics and is not a model for a solo service provider's results.
:::

:::checklist Systems and Delegation Readiness
- List every recurring task you performed in the last two weeks and the time each took.
- Mark each task as high, medium, or low value, and as energizing or draining.
- Write or record SOPs for the three most frequent low-value tasks.
- Calculate your buyback rate and compare it with the cost of help.
- Confirm there is demand to fill time freed by delegation.
- Review worker-classification rules before engaging regular help.
- Prepare a contractor agreement with deliverables, confidentiality, and access rules.
:::

:::summary
Growth beyond the owner's hours requires systems. Document recurring tasks as SOPs, often by recording them. Martell's Buyback Loop (audit, transfer, fill) and buyback rate offer a structured way to decide what to delegate, but delegation only pays when the freed time produces value. Growth paths include raising prices, productizing, subcontracting, hiring, and adding digital products, each with trade-offs. Classify workers correctly and use written agreements. Zapier's history illustrates how remote work widens the talent pool and how prior working relationships reduce hiring risk.
:::

# Chapter 12: The 90-Day Launch Plan

:::objectives
- Sequence launch activities across three 30-day phases.
- Set weekly activity targets that are within your control.
- Define decision points for continuing, adjusting, or pausing the business.
- Conduct a structured 90-day review.
:::

A plan converts the preceding chapters into a calendar. The 90-day horizon is long enough to complete setup and test the market, and short enough to maintain urgency. The plan emphasizes activities you control (messages sent, conversations held, procedures written) rather than outcomes you do not (clients signed, revenue earned). Outcomes follow activities, but on an uncertain timeline.

## Phase 1: Foundation (Days 1-30)

- Complete the service-selection and offer worksheets (Chapters 2 and 3).
- Test the positioning sentence with at least five people in the target market.
- Choose a structure, register the business, obtain an EIN, and apply for local licenses (Chapter 4).
- Open the business bank account and obtain insurance quotes.
- Set up email, file storage, invoicing, and password management (Chapter 5).
- Calculate the floor rate and price the offer ladder (Chapter 6).
- Draft the service agreement and have it reviewed (Chapter 8).
- Build the warm list of 100 contacts (Chapter 7).

## Phase 2: Market Test (Days 31-60)

- Send 15 to 20 personalized outreach messages per week.
- Contact five referral partners.
- Hold discovery conversations and send same-day written proposals.
- Deliver first diagnostics or small projects using the onboarding process (Chapter 9).
- Publish one useful piece of content per week on a professional network.
- Start the weekly bookkeeping routine and tax transfers (Chapter 10).

## Phase 3: Refine and Systematize (Days 61-90)

- Review what prospects said and refine the offer and language.
- Ask satisfied clients for referrals and permission-based testimonials.
- Convert at least one project client to a continuity offer proposal.
- Write SOPs for onboarding, weekly updates, and invoicing (Chapter 11).
- Build the 13-week cash-flow forecast.
- Conduct the 90-day review.

:::example Weekly Targets and Funnel Review (illustrative)
Kendra sets weekly activity targets for Phase 2: 18 outreach messages, 2 referral-partner conversations, 3 discovery calls, and 1 content post. Over four weeks she achieves 70 messages, 7 partner conversations, 10 discovery calls, and 4 posts. From the 10 calls she sells 4 diagnostics ($1,800) and 1 Front Office Reset ($3,200), for $5,000 in sales. Her discovery-to-sale rate is 50 percent and her average sale is $1,000. She concludes that her offer resonates but that she needs more conversations; for Phase 3 she raises the partner-conversation target to 3 per week. These numbers are illustrative and do not represent expected results.
:::

## Decision Points

At day 90, compare results with the targets you set and ask three questions. Is there evidence of demand (conversations, proposals, at least some paid work)? Are clients satisfied with the delivery? Is the price sustainable relative to the floor rate? If all three are positive, continue and scale activity. If demand exists but price is too low, adjust the offer and price. If demand is weak after sustained effort, revisit the client profile or the service. Pausing or changing direction after a fair test is a legitimate business decision, not a failure.

| Signal at day 90 | Interpretation | Suggested action |
|---|---|---|
| Many conversations, few sales | Offer or price mismatch | Revisit offer canvas; test a smaller entry offer |
| Few conversations | Insufficient outreach or weak positioning | Increase activity; sharpen positioning sentence |
| Sales but low effective rate | Underpricing or scope creep | Raise prices; enforce change orders |
| Satisfied clients, no referrals | Not asking | Add a referral request to project close |
| Strong demand, no time | Capacity limit | Document SOPs; raise prices; consider delegation |

:::summary
The 90-day plan has three phases: foundation (offer, registration, tools, pricing, agreements, warm list), market test (outreach, partner conversations, discovery calls, first deliveries, bookkeeping), and refinement (offer adjustments, referrals, continuity proposals, SOPs, forecasting). Focus targets on controllable activities, review the funnel regularly, and at day 90 decide whether to continue, adjust, or pause based on evidence of demand, client satisfaction, and price sustainability.

# Appendix G: Discovery Call Guide

A discovery call should feel like a professional consultation, not a sales pitch. The following sequence typically fits within 30 minutes.

1. **Open (2 minutes).** Thank them, confirm the time available, and state the purpose: "to understand your situation and see whether I can help; if I can't, I'll say so."
2. **Situation (6 minutes).** "Tell me how things work today." "What prompted you to look into this now?"
3. **Problem and impact (8 minutes).** "What's the hardest part?" "How much time does it take each week?" "What happens if nothing changes in the next six months?"
4. **Attempts (3 minutes).** "What have you tried so far? What happened?"
5. **Desired outcome (3 minutes).** "If this were solved, what would be different?" "How would you measure it?"
6. **Approach (4 minutes).** Summarize what you heard. Describe how you would approach it and which offer fits.
7. **Investment and next step (4 minutes).** State the price plainly. Ask: "Does that fit what you had in mind?" Agree on a next step and send a written summary the same day.

If the prospect is not a good fit, say so and, where possible, refer them to someone better suited. Referring away poor-fit work protects your reputation and often generates goodwill.

# Appendix K: Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need an LLC before my first client?** Not necessarily. Many people begin as sole proprietors and form an LLC once they confirm demand. The decision depends on liability exposure, state fees, and client expectations. Review SBA guidance and consult a professional if your work carries meaningful risk.

**Can I keep my job while starting?** Often, yes, and it can reduce financial pressure. Check your employment agreement for conflict-of-interest, non-solicitation, or moonlighting clauses, and do not use your employer's time or equipment for your business.

**How long until I find a client?** There is no reliable answer. Some owners sign a client in weeks through warm contacts; others take months. Plan your finances for a longer timeline.

**Should I work for free to build a portfolio?** Occasionally, a small pro bono project for a nonprofit or a deeply discounted pilot can make sense, if it is time-limited and clearly scoped. Avoid open-ended free work for for-profit businesses.

**What if a client doesn't pay?** Follow your reminder sequence, pause work if the agreement allows, and consider a formal demand letter. For small amounts, small-claims court may be an option in your state. Deposits and milestone billing reduce this risk.

**Do I need to charge sales tax on services?** In many states, most professional services are not subject to sales tax, but some states tax certain services, including some digital or data services. Check with your state's tax agency.

**How do I handle clients in other countries?** Agree on currency, payment method, and governing law in the contract. Be aware of time zones and of data-protection rules that may apply to clients' customer data.

# Glossary

**Asynchronous communication.** Communication that does not require participants to be present at the same time, such as email, shared documents, or recorded video.

**Billable utilization.** The share of total working hours that is billed to clients.

**Change order.** A written agreement to modify the scope, price, or timeline of an engagement.

**Client concentration.** The share of revenue that comes from the largest client or clients.

**Continuity offer.** A recurring service, typically a monthly retainer, that maintains or extends results.

**Days sales outstanding (DSO).** The average number of days between issuing an invoice and receiving payment.

**Doing business as (DBA).** An assumed business name registered with a state or county.

**Effective hourly rate.** Revenue divided by all hours worked, billable and non-billable.

**Employer Identification Number (EIN).** A federal tax identification number issued free by the IRS.

**Estimated tax.** Tax paid in quarterly installments on income not subject to withholding.

**Floor rate.** The minimum hourly rate needed to meet target owner income, cover expenses, and reserve for taxes, given expected billable hours.

**Form 1099-NEC.** An IRS information return used by businesses to report nonemployee compensation above the applicable threshold.

**Form W-9.** A form used to provide a taxpayer identification number to a business that pays you.

**Home office deduction.** A tax deduction for qualifying business use of part of a home.

**Independent contractor.** A person who performs services for a client but controls the means and methods of work and is not an employee.

**Limited liability company (LLC).** A state-registered business entity that can separate personal assets from many business liabilities.

**Nonemployer business.** A business with no paid employees that is subject to federal income tax, as defined by the U.S. Census Bureau.

**Productized service.** A service with a fixed name, scope, deliverables, timeline, and price.

**Professional liability insurance.** Insurance covering claims that professional work or advice caused financial loss; also called errors and omissions insurance.

**Retainer.** A recurring fee paid in advance for a defined bundle of services or availability.

**Scope creep.** Gradual expansion of work beyond the agreed scope without a corresponding price change.

**Self-employment tax.** Social Security and Medicare tax paid by self-employed individuals on net earnings.

**Standard operating procedure (SOP).** A written description of how to perform a recurring task.
