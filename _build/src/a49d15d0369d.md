# How to Use This Book

Most small business owners do not lack ideas for marketing. They lack time, a steady routine, and confidence that what they post is accurate and worth the effort. Generative AI tools promise to help with all three. They can draft a caption in seconds, propose a month of post ideas, rewrite a clumsy paragraph, and suggest headlines for an ad. Used carelessly, the same tools produce bland copy, invented facts, fake-sounding reviews, and claims that can get a business into trouble with customers, platforms, and regulators.

This book is a practical text on using AI as a marketing assistant, or what many vendors now call a marketing agent, in a small business. It is written for owners and small teams in local services, retail, studios, clinics, trades, and professional practices. The central idea is simple: the AI drafts, and a person decides. Each chapter takes one part of a small-business marketing routine, explains the reasoning behind it, and gives you tools to use the same week.

The book does not ask you to buy any particular software. The prompts work in most general-purpose AI chat tools, and the trackers work on paper or in a spreadsheet. Where the law or platform rules matter, as with reviews, endorsements, advertising claims, and claims about AI itself, the book summarizes the relevant public guidance and points you to the original source.

## How the chapters are built

Each chapter follows the same pattern. It opens with learning objectives and key definitions, explains the concept, and offers a numbered framework. It then works through an illustrative example with real arithmetic and presents one real, cited case study drawn from public sources such as regulatory actions, court and tribunal decisions, research papers, company statements, and reputable reporting. Each chapter closes with a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. The appendices collect the printable tools: a voice and offer sheet, a master brief, a prompt library, a 30-day calendar, a batch sheet, review reply templates, an ad pre-flight sheet, a post and inquiry tracker, and a monthly review.

## The running example

The worked examples follow one fictional business, Willow Creek Dog Grooming, a two-table grooming shop in Columbus, Ohio. The owner, Lena Ortiz, grooms full time; a second groomer, Sam, works four days a week; and a part-time bather helps on Saturdays. A full groom averages $85, a bath and tidy averages $45, and a walk-in nail trim is $20. The shop can handle about 14 dogs on a full day. Willow Creek, Lena, and Sam are illustrative only. They are not TTI customers, and their numbers are not benchmarks, forecasts, or promises. They exist so that each method can be shown with concrete arithmetic you can replace with your own figures.

:::callout Scope and limits
This book is educational. It is not legal, tax, or advertising-law advice, and it does not promise any number of views, followers, inquiries, bookings, or sales. Case studies describe the documented experience of the named organizations; their results are theirs, not TTI's, and they do not predict what any reader will achieve. Rules on reviews, endorsements, advertising, privacy, and AI change over time and vary by jurisdiction and platform. The summaries here reflect public sources as of 2026; confirm current requirements with official sources or a qualified professional before you rely on them. Always check every claim, price, photo, and testimonial before you publish.
:::

If you have one hour, read Chapter 2 and fill out the voice and offer sheet in Appendix A. If you have one week, read Chapters 1 through 5 and draft your first batch of posts. Before you publish reviews, testimonials, or paid ads, or before you describe your own use of AI to customers, read Chapters 6, 8, and 9. # Chapter 1: What an AI Marketing Agent Is

:::objectives
- Define an AI marketing agent in plain terms and describe what it can and cannot do.
- Distinguish drafting, deciding, and publishing as separate steps.
- Summarize what recent survey data show about small-business AI use.
- Estimate the time an AI assistant might save using your own numbers.
:::

The phrase "AI marketing agent" is used loosely. Some vendors mean a chat tool that writes posts on request. Others mean software that schedules posts, answers comments, adjusts ad budgets, and sends emails with little human involvement. For a small business, the useful definition is narrower and safer: an AI marketing agent is a tool, or a set of tools, that drafts marketing material and suggests actions according to instructions you write, while a person reviews and approves what is published or spent.

## Key definitions

Generative AI is software that produces new text, images, audio, or video in response to a prompt. A prompt is the instruction you give the tool. A brief is a standing set of instructions about your business, audience, offer, voice, and rules that you paste at the start of each session or store in the tool's settings. A draft is any output that has not yet been checked by a person. Publishing is the act of making content visible to the public, whether as a post, a listing update, an email, or an ad. Human review is a deliberate check of a draft for accuracy, tone, legality, and fit before publishing.

## Drafting, deciding, publishing

It helps to separate marketing work into three steps. Drafting is producing options: captions, headlines, post ideas, replies. AI tools are fast and inexpensive at drafting. Deciding is choosing which option is true, useful, on-brand, and lawful. Deciding requires knowledge the tool does not have, such as what your prices really are this month, which groomer is on vacation, and whether a customer agreed to have a photo of their dog posted. Publishing is the act that carries legal and reputational consequences. A sound routine lets the tool draft freely and keeps deciding and publishing with a person.

This separation is not only a matter of caution. It is how the tools work best. A model that is asked to "run our marketing" will produce generic material. A model that is asked to "write three caption options for a post about winter paw care, using the facts below, in our voice" will produce something you can use after a short edit.

## What the data show

The Federal Reserve Banks' Small Business Credit Survey, fielded from September to November 2025 among 6,525 small employer firms, found that 46 percent of firms said their business or its employees currently use AI, and another 15 percent planned to begin within 12 months [1]. Among firms using AI, writing or marketing was the most common use, reported by 83 percent, followed by individual productivity at 61 percent. Most AI users said they were experimenting or had only partially integrated AI, and the most common challenge they reported was accuracy, cited by 46 percent [1]. The survey is a convenience sample rather than a random sample, so its figures describe respondents rather than all small businesses, but the direction is clear: marketing is where small firms try AI first, and accuracy is what worries them most.

Public attitudes matter too. Pew Research Center's 2026 summary of its surveys reports that half of U.S. adults said in June 2025 that the increased use of AI in daily life makes them feel more concerned than excited, up from 37 percent in 2021, while just 10 percent said they were more excited than concerned; about half also said AI will worsen people's ability to think creatively [21]. For a local business, that means customers may react poorly to content that feels machine-made or misleading, even when the underlying service is good.

## A framework for adopting an AI marketing assistant

1. Write down the marketing jobs you do each week and how long each takes.
2. Mark which jobs are drafting, which are deciding, and which are publishing.
3. Assign drafting jobs to the AI assistant one at a time, starting with low-risk ones such as caption options.
4. Write a brief that includes your facts, voice, and rules (Chapter 2).
5. Keep a human review step for every item that is published or paid for.
6. Track time spent and real inquiries for four weeks before expanding (Chapter 10).

:::example Willow Creek's weekly marketing time (illustrative)
Lena logs her marketing work for two weeks. Each week she spends about 45 minutes thinking of post ideas, 60 minutes writing three captions, 30 minutes replying to reviews and comments, and 20 minutes updating her Google Business Profile hours and photos, for a total of 155 minutes. She tests an AI assistant on two drafting jobs only: post ideas and caption drafts. Idea time drops to 15 minutes and caption time to 30 minutes, including her edits, while the other jobs stay the same. Her new weekly total is 15 + 30 + 30 + 20 = 95 minutes, a saving of 60 minutes per week, or about 52 hours over a 52-week year. If she values her time at the shop's average revenue per grooming hour, roughly $85 for a 90-minute groom or about $57 per hour, the time is worth about 52 × $57 = $2,964 a year in capacity she could use for grooming, rest, or other work. That figure is a measure of capacity, not a forecast of extra revenue; whether the hour becomes income depends on demand.
:::

:::case Klarna's use of generative AI in marketing (real, cited)
In May 2024, the Swedish payments company Klarna said it was using generative AI for tasks such as running marketing campaigns and generating images, saving about $10 million a year in marketing costs [2]. Reuters reported that Klarna had cut its sales and marketing budget by 11 percent in the first quarter of 2024, with AI responsible for 37 percent of the savings, while increasing the number of campaigns. The company said tools such as Midjourney, DALL-E, and Firefly had reduced image production costs by $6 million, and that it had generated more than 1,000 images in the first three months of 2024, refreshing app and website images for occasions such as Valentine's Day and Mother's Day [2]. These are Klarna's results, as reported by Klarna, for a large company with an in-house marketing team; they are not TTI's results and do not predict savings for a small business. The useful lesson is narrower: the savings came from specific, repeatable production tasks, such as seasonal images, rather than from handing all marketing decisions to software.

# Chapter 2: Voice, Offer, and the Brief

:::objectives
- Define brand voice and tone and explain how they differ.
- Choose a single primary offer and a single trackable call to action.
- Write a reusable brief that keeps AI drafts accurate and on-voice.
- Build a list of words and claims your business will not use.
:::

AI tools produce vague copy when they receive vague inputs. Ask for "a post about our grooming shop" and you will get something like "Pamper your furry friend with our top-notch services." Give the tool your actual services, prices, neighborhood, and voice, and the result improves sharply. The brief is the single most important document in this book. It is the place where your facts and your judgment live, so the tool does not have to guess.

## Key definitions

Brand voice is the consistent personality of a business's writing: plain or formal, warm or reserved, playful or serious. Tone is how that voice adapts to a situation, such as a cheerful holiday post versus a careful reply to an upset customer. A primary offer is the one service or product you feature most often. A call to action is the single next step you ask a reader to take, such as calling, booking online, or visiting. A proof fact is a statement about your business that you can document, such as years in business, a license or certification, or a published policy. A banned list is a set of words, phrases, and claims your drafts must never include.

## Why one offer and one call to action

Small businesses often try to mention everything in every post. The result is cluttered and hard to act on. Choosing one primary offer for a month, and one call to action that you can track, makes each post clearer and makes measurement possible. If every post says "Text us at the shop number to book," you can count texts. If posts alternate among a website form, a phone number, a direct message, and a walk-in invitation, you will not know which one worked.

## Voice and tone

The distinction between voice and tone is well explained in Mailchimp's public content style guide, which describes voice as constant and tone as changing with the situation and the reader's emotional state: "You have the same voice all the time, but your tone changes" [3]. A small business can borrow that principle. Lena's voice is friendly, practical, and a little funny about dogs. Her tone is lighter on a post about a puppy's first groom and more careful when replying to a customer whose dog came home with a nick.

## A framework for writing the brief

1. State the business name, location, service area, and hours.
2. Name the primary offer, who buys it, and the real price or starting price.
3. State the single call to action exactly as it should appear.
4. List three to six proof facts you can document.
5. Describe your voice in three adjectives and give two sentences that sound like you.
6. List banned words, banned claims, and topics to avoid.
7. Add rules: reading level, length, no invented facts, and a reminder that a person will review every draft.

:::example Willow Creek's brief (illustrative)
Lena's brief reads, in part: "You are drafting marketing for Willow Creek Dog Grooming, a two-table grooming shop in Columbus, Ohio, open Tuesday through Saturday, 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. This month's primary offer is the full groom for dogs under 40 pounds, $85, including bath, haircut, nails, and ears. The call to action is: Text 'BOOK' to the shop number. Proof facts: open since 2019; Lena holds a professional grooming certification; we use one appointment per table at a time, so dogs are not kept in cages for hours. Voice: friendly, practical, lightly funny. Never use the words 'pamper,' 'fur baby,' 'unlock,' 'elevate,' or 'best in Columbus.' Never invent reviews, discounts, or availability. Write at about a seventh-grade level. A person will check every draft before posting." The brief is about 140 words. Lena estimates that pasting it at the start of each session takes 10 seconds and saves at least two rounds of edits per caption. At three captions a week and about four minutes per edit round, that is 3 × 2 × 4 = 24 minutes a week saved, or about 21 hours a year.
:::

:::case Mailchimp's public voice and tone guide (real, cited)
Mailchimp, the email marketing company, publishes its content style guide openly. The voice and tone section explains that the company aims to sound like "the experienced and compassionate business partner we wish we'd had," to educate people "without patronizing or confusing them," and to prefer "the subtle over the noisy" [3]. It separates a constant voice from a tone that shifts with the reader's emotional state, noting that you would not use the same tone with someone who is upset as with someone who is laughing [3]. Mailchimp's guide describes its own practice and makes no claim about results for other businesses; it is not a TTI resource or result. For a small business, the case shows that a voice can be written down in a page or two and then applied consistently by different writers, which is exactly what an AI brief asks you to do.

# Chapter 3: Prompts That Produce Drafts

:::objectives
- Write prompts that specify task, inputs, format, and limits.
- Use a de-robot pass to remove generic or hyped language.
- Recognize the most common errors in AI drafts.
- Apply a fact-check routine before anything is published.
:::

A prompt is a work order. Like any work order, it is better when it states what is needed, what materials to use, what the finished product looks like, and what not to do. Most disappointing AI output comes from prompts that leave those things out. This chapter gives a simple structure for prompts and a routine for checking what comes back.

## Key definitions

A task prompt asks for one specific piece of work, such as three caption options. Inputs are the facts and materials the tool should use, such as the brief and a photo description. Format is the required shape of the output: length, number of options, structure. Constraints are the limits: words to avoid, claims not to make. A hallucination is a confident statement by an AI tool that is false or unsupported. A de-robot pass is a second prompt or edit that strips generic phrases, hype, and filler from a draft.

## The four parts of a good prompt

A useful prompt has four parts. The task says what to produce. The inputs supply the facts, often by pasting the brief and adding details for this post. The format says how long and how many. The constraints say what to avoid. For example: "Task: write three caption options for an Instagram post. Inputs: the brief above; the photo shows a golden retriever after a summer trim; this is the dog's first visit. Format: each caption under 60 words, ending with our call to action. Constraints: no hashtags in the body, no claims about health benefits, do not name the dog or owner."

## Common errors in AI drafts

AI drafts fail in predictable ways. They invent details, such as a discount you never offered or a review you never received. They inflate claims, turning "careful" into "the best in the city." They drift into generic language that could describe any business. They get dates, prices, and hours wrong when those facts were not supplied. And they may suggest content that raises privacy issues, such as naming a customer. A fact-check routine catches these before they reach the public.

## A framework for prompting and checking

1. Paste the brief at the start of the session.
2. Write the task, inputs, format, and constraints for this piece.
3. Ask for two or three options, not one.
4. Run a de-robot pass on the best option.
5. Check every fact against your own records: price, hours, services, names.
6. Check every claim: can you prove it today?
7. Check privacy: do you have permission for any customer name, photo, or story?
8. Edit in your own words and publish only after the checks.

:::example Willow Creek's caption check (illustrative)
Lena asks for three caption options about a first puppy groom. Option two is the best, but her check finds four problems in its 52 words: it says "only $65," while the real puppy introduction price is $55; it says "open seven days," while the shop is open five; it calls Willow Creek "Columbus's favorite groomer," which she cannot prove; and it names the puppy's owner, who has not given permission. She fixes all four, which takes about three minutes. If she publishes three posts a week and each draft averages two errors, she will catch about 3 × 2 × 52 = 312 errors a year that would otherwise have gone out under her name. The arithmetic is a reminder that the check is not optional; it is the step that makes the time savings safe.
:::

:::case CNET's AI-written explainers (real, cited)
In January 2023, the technology news site CNET said it had issued corrections on a number of articles, some described as substantial, after using an internally designed AI tool to help write 77 published stories since November 2022 [4]. CNN reported that the stories were basic financial explainers, with headlines such as "How to Close A Bank Account," and that CNET's editor-in-chief wrote that editors generated outlines, then expanded, added to, and edited the AI drafts before publishing. CNET paused use of the tool after the errors came to light [4]. These events concern CNET's own editorial process; they are not TTI's experience. The lesson for a small business is direct: even a professional editorial team that reviewed AI drafts published factual errors, which is why the fact-check step in this chapter checks every number and claim against your own records rather than relying on a quick read.
:::

| Format | Length and number | "Under 60 words each" |

# Chapter 4: Pillars and the 30-Day Calendar

:::objectives
- Define content pillars and explain why a small set of them keeps posting steady.
- Build a 30-day calendar that balances helpful, proof, and offer posts.
- Use one longer piece of content as a source for several shorter posts.
- Set a weekly batching routine that fits a small business.
:::

The hardest part of small-business marketing is rarely writing a single good post. It is posting steadily for months while running the business. A calendar solves that problem by making most decisions once, in advance. Content pillars make the calendar easier to fill, and AI tools make each slot faster to draft.

## Key definitions

A content pillar is a recurring theme that your posts return to, such as care tips, behind-the-scenes work, customer questions, or offers. A content calendar is a schedule that assigns a pillar, topic, and format to each posting day. Batching is the practice of drafting several posts in one sitting. Repurposing is turning one piece of content into several smaller pieces for different formats or platforms. Posting cadence is how often you publish.

## Why pillars help

Without pillars, every post starts from a blank page. With four or five pillars, the question becomes "which care tip this week?" rather than "what should I post?" Pillars also balance the mix. A feed that is all offers reads like a flyer; a feed that is all tips never asks for the booking. A common working balance for a local service business is to make most posts helpful or behind-the-scenes and a smaller share direct offers, though no fixed ratio is proven to work for every business. Treat any ratio as a starting point to test.

## One piece, many posts

A practical way to fill a calendar is to start with one longer piece, such as a five-minute phone video answering three customer questions, and cut it into shorter pieces: a clip for each question, a quote card, a carousel of the three answers, and a caption that links them. AI tools are well suited to the cutting and rewriting, provided the source material is real and accurate.

## A framework for the 30-day calendar

1. Choose four or five pillars that match your services and your customers' real questions.
2. Decide on a cadence you can keep, such as three posts a week.
3. Assign a pillar to each posting day so the mix stays balanced.
4. Use the AI assistant to propose topics for each slot, then cut the list to the ones that fit.
5. Record one longer piece each month and plan the shorter posts that come from it.
6. Batch drafts once a week and schedule them.
7. Review the calendar at the end of the month against real inquiries (Chapter 10).

:::example Willow Creek's monthly calendar (illustrative)
Lena chooses five pillars: care tips, behind the table, customer questions, meet the dogs (with owner permission), and the month's offer. She commits to three posts a week, Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday, for about 13 posts in a 30-day month. She assigns care tips to 4 slots, behind the table to 3, customer questions to 3, meet the dogs to 2, and the offer to 1 slot plus a short mention at the end of 2 other posts. In percentage terms, 4 of 13 is about 31 percent care tips and 1 of 13, about 8 percent, is a dedicated offer post. Once a month she records a six-minute video answering "How often should my dog be groomed?", "Why do you need to see matted dogs sooner?", and "What happens during a first visit?" That one recording supplies 3 short clips and 1 carousel, covering 4 of the 13 slots. Her weekly batch session takes about 40 minutes for three posts, compared with roughly 20 minutes per post when she wrote them one at a time on the day, a saving of 3 × 20 - 40 = 20 minutes a week.
:::

:::case The GaryVee content model (real, cited)
Entrepreneur and media executive Gary Vaynerchuk published a description of the content process his team uses, which he calls a "reverse pyramid." He describes starting with one piece of long-form "pillar content," such as a vlog, a question-and-answer show, an interview, or a keynote, and having his team "analyze and repurpose it into 30 other pieces of content" designed for the platforms where they are distributed [5]. The document lists short-form pieces such as articles, quotes, images, and stories, all derived from the long-form source [5]. This describes the practice of a well-known personality with a full media team; it is not a TTI result and does not predict reach for a small business. Scaled down, the same idea fits a grooming shop or a plumbing company: one honest, useful recording a month can feed several weeks of posts.

# Chapter 5: Hooks, Captions, and Short Video

:::objectives
- Define a hook and explain its role in short-form content.
- Use fill-in-the-blank hook patterns with an AI assistant.
- Write captions that end with one clear call to action.
- Plan a short phone video that a small business can record in one take.
:::

Most people scroll past most posts. The opening line of a caption or the first two seconds of a video decide whether anyone reads or watches the rest. That opening is called the hook. AI tools can suggest dozens of hooks quickly, but the best hooks for a local business are usually specific and true rather than clever.

## Key definitions

A hook is the opening line or first moments of a post designed to earn attention. A caption is the text that accompanies an image or video. A carousel is a post with several images or slides that the viewer swipes through. A short video is a vertical clip, usually under a minute, made for platforms such as Instagram Reels, TikTok, or YouTube Shorts. A script is the planned words and shots for a video.

## Hook patterns that fit local businesses

Several hook patterns work well for service businesses because they promise useful, specific information. The question hook asks what customers really ask: "How often should a doodle be groomed?" The mistake hook names a common error: "The brushing mistake that leads to matting." The number hook gives a small, concrete list: "Three signs your dog's nails are too long." The behind-the-scenes hook shows a process: "What happens in the first ten minutes of a groom." The myth hook corrects a belief: "Shaving a double-coated dog does not keep it cooler." Each must be true and supportable; a hook that overpromises damages trust.

## Captions and calls to action

A caption should deliver what the hook promised, in plain language, and end with the single call to action from your brief. Short paragraphs and concrete details work better than adjectives. If the post is helpful rather than promotional, the call to action can be soft, such as "Text BOOK if your dog is due," but it should still be the same call to action, so that you can count responses.

## A framework for hooks, captions, and video

1. Pick the topic from your calendar.
2. Ask the AI assistant for ten hooks using two or three patterns.

# Chapter 6: Reviews and Testimonials

:::objectives
- Explain the main federal rules that apply to reviews and testimonials.
- Identify review practices that are prohibited or risky, including AI-generated reviews.
- Ask for reviews in a way that is fair and lawful.
- Draft review replies with AI help without revealing private information.
:::

Reviews are among the most persuasive marketing a small business has, because they come from customers rather than the business. That persuasive power is also why regulators police them. AI tools add a new risk: they can produce realistic-sounding reviews in seconds. This chapter explains the rules, the practices to avoid, and a safe routine for requesting and replying to reviews.

## Key definitions

A consumer review is a customer's evaluation of a business, product, or service, often with a star rating. A testimonial is a statement by a customer or other person endorsing a business, used in the business's own marketing. An endorsement, in the FTC's usage, is an advertising message that consumers are likely to believe reflects the opinions or experience of someone other than the advertiser. A material connection is a relationship, such as employment, family, payment, or free products, that could affect how people weigh an endorsement. Review gating is asking only customers you expect to be happy to leave reviews. Review suppression is preventing or hiding negative reviews.

## The rules in plain terms

The FTC's Endorsement Guides rest on a basic principle: endorsements must be honest and not misleading, and must reflect the honest opinion of the endorser. If there is a connection between an endorser and the business that consumers would not expect, such as an employee or relative writing a review, that connection should be disclosed clearly [9]. The FTC's guidance also says that asking for reviews only from customers you think are likely to be happy would be misleading if it substantially skews the favorability of the reviews, and that if you delay posting reviews to respond to them, you should delay all reviews, positive and negative, for the same reasonable period [9].

In August 2024, the FTC announced a final rule on consumer reviews and testimonials that allows it to seek civil penalties against knowing violators [8]. The rule prohibits fake or false reviews and testimonials, explicitly including AI-generated fake reviews and reviews by people who did not have actual experience with the business; it prohibits buying or selling such reviews. It also prohibits offering compensation or incentives conditioned on a review expressing a particular sentiment, positive or negative; certain undisclosed insider reviews; company-controlled "independent" review sites; review suppression through groundless legal threats, intimidation, or certain false accusations; misrepresenting that displayed reviews represent all or most reviews when negative ones have been suppressed; and buying or selling fake social media indicators such as bot followers or views [8].

## Where AI fits

AI tools should never write reviews or testimonials. They can help in three safe ways: drafting a polite request message that goes to all recent customers, drafting replies to reviews that you then edit, and summarizing themes across many real reviews so you can improve the business. Even reply drafting needs care. A reply must never confirm private details about a customer, such as their dog's medical issue or their address, and must not argue in a way that looks like intimidation.

## A framework for reviews

1. Ask every recent customer, not only the happy ones, using the same message.
2. Do not offer anything in exchange for a positive review; if you offer an incentive for any review, make it unconditional on sentiment and disclose it where required.
3. Never write, buy, or generate reviews, and never post reviews from staff or relatives without clear disclosure.
4. Display reviews honestly; do not hide negative ones.
5. Draft replies with AI help, then remove private details and any defensive language.
6. Respond to negative reviews with facts, an apology where appropriate, and an offer to talk offline.
7. Use testimonials in marketing only with the customer's permission and in their actual words.

:::example Willow Creek's review request (illustrative)
Lena completes about 55 grooms a week. She used to hand a review card only to customers who seemed delighted, which the FTC's guidance treats as risky if it skews favorability [9]. She switches to a text sent to every customer who agreed to receive texts, two days after the visit: "Thanks for visiting Willow Creek. If you have a minute, we'd value an honest review of your visit," followed by the link. If 55 customers receive the request each week and 8 percent leave a review, she can expect about 55 × 0.08 = 4.4, or roughly 4 reviews a week, about 18 to 19 a month. Some will be less than five stars. With a fair request process, her average rating may dip slightly, but it will reflect real experience, and she can reply to each review in about 3 minutes with an AI-drafted, human-edited response, about 4 × 3 = 12 minutes a week.
:::

:::case Fashion Nova and the FTC (real, cited)
In January 2022, the FTC announced that online fashion retailer Fashion Nova would pay $4.2 million to settle allegations that it blocked negative reviews of its products from being posted on its website [7]. According to the FTC, the company misrepresented that the reviews on its site reflected the views of all purchasers who submitted reviews, when it in fact suppressed reviews with ratings lower than four stars out of five, using a third-party review management system. The FTC described the case as its first involving a company's efforts to conceal negative customer reviews, and the order prohibits Fashion Nova from suppressing customer reviews [7]. These are the FTC's allegations and the settlement terms in that matter; they are not TTI's experience, and the case is offered only as a warning. Any small business that filters, delays, or hides low ratings, including through software settings it did not read carefully, should review those settings now.
:::

| Practice | Status under FTC rule and guidance | Safer alternative |
|---|---|---|
| AI-generated reviews | Prohibited as fake reviews [8] | Ask real customers |
| Paying for five-star reviews | Prohibited conditional incentive [8] | Same request to all customers, no sentiment condition |
| Staff or family reviews without disclosure | Prohibited or misleading [8][9] | Disclose the connection or do not post |
| Asking only happy customers | Misleading if it substantially skews results [9] | Ask everyone the same way |
| Hiding low ratings on your site | Prohibited misrepresentation [8] | Display all reviews |
| Threatening reviewers | Prohibited suppression if groundless [8] | Reply with facts and an offer to talk |

:::checklist Review practices
- One request message, sent to every recent customer.
- No reward tied to a positive rating.
- No reviews written, bought, or generated by AI.
- Staff and family connections disclosed or not posted.
- All reviews displayed on our website, positive and negative.
- Review software settings checked for filtering or delays.
- Every reply edited by a person and free of private details.
- Written permission on file for every testimonial used in ads.
:::

:::summary
Reviews persuade because they come from real customers, which is why the FTC prohibits fake, AI-generated, bought, and suppressed reviews and expects material connections to be disclosed. Ask every customer the same way, never tie rewards to sentiment, display reviews honestly, and use AI only to draft requests and replies that a person edits.
:::

# Chapter 7: Local Listings and Search

:::objectives
- Explain why a business profile on maps and search often matters more than social posts for local businesses.
- Apply Google's basic guidelines for representing a business accurately.
- Use AI to draft profile descriptions, posts, and answers without breaking listing rules.
- Recognize fake listings and review schemes and know how to respond.
:::

When someone searches "dog groomer near me" or "furnace repair Raleigh," the first thing they often see is a map with a few business profiles. For many local businesses, that profile produces more calls than any social media post. It shows hours, location or service area, photos, reviews, and a button to call. Keeping it accurate and active is one of the highest-value marketing jobs a small business can do, and one of the easiest to delegate partly to an AI assistant.

## Key definitions

A business profile is the listing that appears on a map or search results page for a business, such as a Google Business Profile. A category is the label that describes the core business, such as "pet groomer." A service-area business is one that serves customers at their locations rather than at a storefront. Keyword stuffing is adding search terms to a business name or description that are not part of the real name or relevant content. A listing post is a short update published on a business profile.

## The rules that matter most

Google's guidelines for representing a business ask owners to represent the business as it is consistently known in the real world, across signage, stationery, and branding; to keep the address or service area accurate and precise; to choose the fewest categories that describe the core business; and to keep only one profile per business [10]. The business name must reflect the real-world name; Google lists marketing taglines, phone numbers, location details, and service descriptions as examples of information that must not be added to the name, and warns that including unnecessary information can lead to suspension [10]. The business description should be honest and relevant and must not focus on special promotions, prices, or sales, and it may not contain links [10]. Service-area businesses should have one profile for a central location with a designated service area and cannot list a virtual office unless it is staffed during business hours [10].

## Where AI helps

An AI assistant can draft a business description within Google's limits, propose listing posts from your calendar, suggest answers to common questions, and write photo captions. It must be told the rules. Without them, a model will often suggest exactly the things the guidelines prohibit, such as adding "Best Dog Grooming Columbus" to the business name or putting a discount in the description.

## A framework for your listing

1. Confirm the business name exactly as it appears on your sign and invoices.
2. Confirm address or service area, hours, holiday hours, phone, and website.
3. Choose the fewest accurate categories.
4. Draft a description with AI help, using your brief plus Google's rules: no promotions, prices, or links.
5. Add real, recent photos of your work, space, and team, with permission.
6. Publish a short listing post weekly, drawn from your calendar.
7. Reply to every review using the routine in Chapter 6.
8. Check the profile monthly for unauthorized edits and duplicate listings.

:::example Willow Creek's profile clean-up (illustrative)
Lena's profile name had been set years ago as "Willow Creek Dog Grooming - Best Groomer Columbus OH." Under Google's guidelines, the extra words are a marketing tagline and location detail that do not belong in the name [10], so she changes it to "Willow Creek Dog Grooming." Her description had said "20 percent off first groom," which the guidelines treat as promotional content for the description field [10]; she moves the offer to a listing post and rewrites the description with AI help, describing services, the one-dog-per-table approach, and the shop's history. She had three categories; she keeps one primary category and one secondary that fit her core service. Her hours were missing Saturday. The audit takes about 45 minutes. She then sets a 10-minute monthly check, or 12 × 10 = 120 minutes a year, to keep the profile accurate.
:::

:::case Google's lawsuit against a fake-listing scheme (real, cited)
In June 2023, Google announced that it had filed a consumer protection lawsuit against a party it said had run a coordinated campaign to manipulate its services for small businesses [11]. According to Google, the defendant posted more than 350 fraudulent Business Profiles and tried to bolster them with more than 14,000 fake reviews, then attempted to sell information about consumers lured in by the false claims. Google said it had detected and removed the deceptive content and that, in 2022, it had protected more than 185,000 businesses from further abuse after detecting suspicious activity [11]. These are Google's statements about its own enforcement; they are not TTI's results. For a small business, the case is a reminder that platforms actively look for fake listings and reviews, so any vendor offering to "boost" a profile with reviews or extra listings is offering something that can get the business removed.
:::

| Address or area | Accurate and precise [10] | Virtual office | Use real location or service area |

# Chapter 8: Small-Budget Paid Ads

:::objectives
- Explain the truth-in-advertising rules that apply to small-business ads.
- Distinguish express and implied claims and the need for substantiation.
- Set a small test budget and calculate cost per inquiry.
- Use AI to draft ad copy and audience ideas without overstating results.
:::

Paid ads on social platforms and search engines let a small business reach people who would not otherwise see its posts. They also spend real money quickly. AI tools can write dozens of ad variations in minutes, which makes it tempting to run many ads with little thought. This chapter covers the rules that apply to ad claims, a simple way to test with a small budget, and research showing why "the ad got clicks" does not always mean "the ad caused sales."

## Key definitions

An express claim is a statement made directly in an ad. An implied claim is one that a reasonable consumer would infer from the ad's words or images. Substantiation is the evidence a business must have to support a claim before the ad runs. A test budget is a fixed amount spent to learn what works. Cost per inquiry is ad spend divided by the number of real inquiries the ad produced. Incrementality is the extra result an ad causes beyond what would have happened without it.

## The rules in plain terms

The FTC's guide for small business advertisers summarizes the core rules: advertising must be truthful and not deceptive, advertisers must have evidence to back up their claims, and ads cannot be unfair [14]. The FTC looks at both express and implied claims, considers what an ad leaves out, and requires a "reasonable basis," meaning objective evidence, before the ad runs; letters from satisfied customers usually are not enough to support claims that require objective evaluation [14]. Platforms add their own rules. Meta, for example, reviews ads against its Advertising Standards, which describe the types of ad content it allows and prohibits and the advertiser behavior that can lead to restrictions on an account [13].

For an AI-drafted ad, this means every number, comparison, and promise must be checked. "Columbus's top-rated groomer" is a claim that needs proof. "Stress-free grooming" may imply something you cannot guarantee for every dog. "Book in 60 seconds" must be true on a phone.

## What clicks do and do not show

Ad platforms report clicks, reach, and sometimes conversions. Those numbers are useful but can mislead, because some people who clicked would have become customers anyway. Experiments are the best way to measure what an ad adds, and they sometimes show that ads add less than the dashboard suggests.

## A framework for a small ad test

1. Choose one offer and one call to action from your brief.
2. Use AI to draft five headline and text variations, then fact-check each against the claim rules.
3. Remove any claim you cannot substantiate today.
4. Set a fixed test budget and duration, such as $150 over two weeks.
5. Target a realistic area and audience; do not rely on broad defaults.
6. Track real inquiries that mention the ad or use an ad-only call to action.
7. Calculate cost per inquiry and cost per booking.
8. Stop, keep, or change the ad based on those numbers, not on clicks alone.

:::example Willow Creek's two-week ad test (illustrative)
Lena runs a $150 test over 14 days, about $10.71 a day, promoting the under-40-pound full groom at $85 within five miles of the shop. The ad asks people to text "GROOM" rather than "BOOK," so she can tell ad inquiries apart. The platform reports 210 clicks, or about $0.71 per click. She receives 12 texts with "GROOM," so her cost per inquiry is $150 ÷ 12 = $12.50. Seven of the 12 book, so her cost per booking is $150 ÷ 7 = about $21.43. At $85 per groom, the seven bookings bring in $595 in revenue; if her direct cost per groom, including shampoo, supplies, and the bather's time, is about $25, her gross margin on those grooms is 7 × ($85 - $25) = $420, which exceeds the $150 spend. She also notes that 2 of the 7 were existing customers who might have booked anyway, so she treats only 5 as possibly new, a cost of $30 per possibly new booking. All figures are invented for illustration.
:::

:::case eBay's paid search experiments (real, cited)
Researchers Tom Blake, Chris Nosko, and Steven Tadelis, with Nosko and Tadelis then at eBay Research Labs, reported results from a series of large field experiments at eBay designed to measure the causal effect of paid search ads [12]. In their 2014 NBER working paper, they found that returns from paid search were a fraction of conventional non-experimental estimates. As an extreme case, ads on brand keywords, such as searches that already included "eBay," had no measurable short-term benefit. For non-brand keywords, new and infrequent users were positively influenced by ads, but frequent users, whose purchases were not influenced by ads, accounted for most of the ad spending, so average returns were negative [12]. These are eBay's experimental findings for a very large online marketplace; they are not TTI's results and do not predict outcomes for any small business. The lesson is to ask what an ad adds: paying to reach people who were already going to call you can look successful on a dashboard while adding little.
:::

|---|---|---|
| Headline | "Columbus's #1 Groomer" | "Full groom for small dogs, $85" |
| Promise | "Stress-free for every dog" | "One dog per table, no long crate waits" |

# Chapter 9: Honest AI Claims and Governance

:::objectives
- Explain why claims about your own use of AI are subject to truth-in-advertising rules.
- Describe recent enforcement and legal decisions involving AI claims and AI-generated content.
- Accept responsibility for what any AI tool says on your behalf.
- Set simple governance rules for AI use in your marketing.
:::

As AI becomes common, some businesses advertise that they use it, and some use it in ways customers cannot see, such as chatbots on their website or AI-written content under a staff member's name. Both raise questions of honesty. This chapter covers claims about AI, responsibility for AI outputs, and a short governance policy that a small business can adopt in an afternoon.

## Key definitions

An AI claim is any statement in marketing about what AI does in a product or service, such as "AI-powered scheduling." AI washing is exaggerating or inventing the role of AI in a product. A chatbot is software that converses with users in text or voice. Disclosure is telling customers clearly when they are dealing with AI or AI-generated content where that would matter to them. Governance is the set of rules, roles, and checks an organization uses to manage a risk.

## AI claims are advertising claims

The FTC has said plainly that claims about AI are subject to the same truth-in-advertising rules as other claims. In a 2023 business blog post, FTC attorney Michael Atleson asked advertisers whether they were exaggerating what their AI product can do, promising that it does something better than a non-AI product without proof, and whether the product actually uses AI at all [22]. In September 2024, the FTC announced Operation AI Comply, a law enforcement sweep with five actions against operations that used AI hype or sold AI technology that could be used deceptively, including a case against the writing tool Rytr, whose service could generate customer reviews and testimonials [15]. In February 2025, the FTC finalized an order requiring DoNotPay, which had promoted its service as "the world's first robot lawyer," to stop making deceptive claims, pay $193,000 in monetary relief, and notify past subscribers; the FTC alleged the company had not tested whether its AI performed at the level of a human lawyer [16].

## You own what your AI says

A business cannot disown what its AI tools tell customers. That principle was tested in a Canadian small claims decision described in the case below. The same logic applies to AI-written posts, emails, and ads: if they go out under your name, they are your statements.

## Governance for a small business

Governance sounds like a large-company concern, but the core ideas scale down. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, published in January 2023, is a voluntary resource intended to help organizations of all sizes manage the risks of designing, developing, deploying, or using AI [19]. It organizes risk management into four functions: govern, map, measure, and manage. For a small business, that translates to: decide who is responsible, list where AI is used, check its outputs, and fix problems when they appear.

## A framework for honest AI use

1. List every place your business uses AI in marketing or customer contact.
2. Name one person responsible for each use.
3. Do not claim AI capabilities you have not tested; describe what the tool actually does.
4. Never publish AI-generated people, reviews, or testimonials as if they were real.
5. Disclose AI use where customers would care, such as a website chatbot.
6. Give any customer-facing AI tool accurate, current policies and prices, and test it monthly.
7. Honor what your AI tells customers, or correct it promptly and fairly.
8. Review this list every quarter.

:::example Willow Creek's AI inventory (illustrative)
Lena lists four AI uses: caption drafting, review reply drafting, listing post drafting, and a website chat widget that answers questions about hours and prices. She assigns herself as the person responsible for all four and asks Sam to test the chat widget on the first Monday of each month with 10 standard questions. In January, the widget answers 9 of 10 correctly, a 90 percent accuracy rate; it quotes the old puppy price of $50 instead of $55. Lena updates the widget's information and decides that any customer who was quoted $50 in the past month will be charged $50. She estimates that perhaps 6 puppy bookings came through the widget that month, so honoring the old price costs 6 × $5 = $30, a small amount compared with the trust it protects.
:::

:::case Moffatt v. Air Canada (real, cited)
In February 2024, the British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal ordered Air Canada to pay compensation to a customer, Jake Moffatt, who had relied on the airline's website chatbot after a grandmother's death in November 2022 [18]. The chatbot told Moffatt they could apply for a reduced bereavement fare retroactively within 90 days; when Moffatt applied, the airline said bereavement rates did not apply to completed travel, a policy described on another page of its website. CBC reported that Air Canada argued the chatbot was "a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions," which tribunal member Christopher Rivers called "a remarkable submission," writing that "it should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website" and that the airline "did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate." The tribunal ordered Air Canada to pay about 812 Canadian dollars [18]. This is a decision about Air Canada; it is not TTI's experience and is not legal advice for any jurisdiction. Separately, in November 2023, Futurism reported that Sports Illustrated had published product reviews under author names and headshots that appeared to be AI-generated, with no evidence the writers existed [17]. Together, the two cases show the same point: customers and tribunals treat what appears on your website as yours.
:::

|---|---|---|
| Caption drafting | Invented facts | Pre-publish check (Chapter 3) |
| Review replies | Private details revealed | Human edit before posting |
| Website chatbot | Wrong prices or policies | Monthly test; honor or correct promptly |
| AI images | Misleading depiction of real work | Label or use real photos for results |
| "AI-powered" claims | Overstated capability | Describe only tested functions |

# Chapter 10: Measuring What Matters

:::objectives
- Distinguish vanity metrics from business metrics.
- Track posts and inquiries with a simple log.
- Run a monthly review that leads to one change at a time.
- Interpret results with appropriate caution.
:::

Marketing that cannot be measured tends to be judged by how it feels, and feelings are poor guides. Likes and views are easy to see and pleasant to watch, but a grooming shop is paid for grooms, not for likes. This final chapter sets up a simple measurement routine that connects marketing work to inquiries and bookings, using tools no more complicated than a spreadsheet.

## Key definitions

A vanity metric is a number that is easy to increase but weakly connected to revenue, such as followers or views. A business metric is a number tied directly to revenue or customers, such as inquiries, bookings, or revenue from new customers. Attribution is the process of linking a result to its cause. A source question is a short question asked of every new customer, such as "How did you hear about us?" A monthly review is a scheduled look at the numbers that ends with a decision.

## Why vanity metrics mislead

Vanity metrics can rise while the business stands still. A post can collect hundreds of likes from people outside the service area. Even large advertisers have found that reported delivery overstated actual attention. In 2018, Reuters reported that Procter & Gamble, the world's biggest advertiser, said it had cut digital advertising spending by $200 million in 2017 after viewership data showed its ads were not reaching its target audience effectively; the company's chief brand officer said the average view time for an ad on a mobile newsfeed was 1.7 seconds [20]. These are P&G's statements about its own advertising; they are not TTI's results. For a small business, the lesson is to look past reach to what people actually do.

## The two numbers that matter most

For most small service businesses, two numbers matter most: real inquiries, meaning calls, texts, messages, and form submissions from potential customers, and bookings from those inquiries. Track both by source. The simplest attribution tools are a unique call to action for each channel, as in Chapter 8, and a source question asked of every new customer.

## A framework for monthly measurement

1. Log every post or ad with date, platform, topic, and call to action.
2. Log every inquiry with date, source, and whether it booked.
3. Ask every new customer the source question and record the answer.
4. At month end, total inquiries and bookings by source.
5. Compare with time and money spent on each channel.
6. Choose one change for next month: keep, stop, or adjust one thing.
7. Review three months before drawing firm conclusions.

:::example Willow Creek's monthly review (illustrative)
In March, Lena logs 13 posts, 4 listing posts, and one $150 ad test. She records 61 inquiries: 22 from her Google Business Profile, 12 from the ad (texted "GROOM"), 9 from Instagram posts (texted "BOOK" and mentioned a post), 11 from referrals, and 7 unknown. Bookings were 15, 7, 4, 9, and 3, for 38 in total, an overall booking rate of 38 ÷ 61 = about 62 percent. Her Instagram posts took about 6 hours of work for 4 bookings, or 1.5 hours per booking; her listing upkeep took about 1.5 hours for 15 bookings, or 6 minutes per booking. The comparison does not prove the listing caused all 15 bookings, but it suggests where an extra hour is most likely to help. Her one change for April is to add two more listing posts and a weekly photo upload while keeping her Instagram routine the same, so that she can see the effect of one change.
:::

:::case Procter & Gamble's digital spending cuts (real, cited)
Reuters reported in 2018 that Procter & Gamble said it had cut digital advertising spending by $200 million in 2017, based on viewership data from technology and media companies showing its ads were not reaching its target audience effectively [20]. P&G's chief brand officer, Marc Pritchard, said fresh data led the company to reduce digital spending with several big media companies by 20 to 50 percent, and that the average view time for an ad on a mobile newsfeed was 1.7 seconds, "little more than a glance" [20]. These figures describe P&G's experience as the world's largest advertiser; they are not TTI's results and do not predict outcomes for a small business. The case shows that measurement can justify spending less as well as more, and that the right question is always whether the intended people saw and acted on the message.
:::

# Glossary

Attribution: the process of linking a marketing result, such as a booking, to the activity that caused it.

Batching: drafting several posts in one sitting.

Brand voice: the consistent personality of a business's writing.

Brief: a standing set of instructions about a business, its offer, voice, and rules, given to an AI tool before each task.

Business profile: a listing for a business on a map or search results page.

Call to action: the single next step a reader is asked to take.

Chatbot: software that converses with users in text or voice.

Content pillar: a recurring theme that posts return to.

Cost per inquiry: ad spend divided by the number of real inquiries produced.

De-robot pass: an edit that removes generic phrases, hype, and filler from a draft.

Endorsement: an advertising message that consumers are likely to believe reflects the opinion or experience of someone other than the advertiser.

Express claim: a statement made directly in an ad.

Generative AI: software that produces new text, images, audio, or video in response to a prompt.

Hallucination: a confident statement by an AI tool that is false or unsupported.

Hook: the opening line or first moments of a post designed to earn attention.

Implied claim: a claim a reasonable consumer would infer from an ad's words or images.

Incrementality: the extra result an ad causes beyond what would have happened without it.

Keyword stuffing: adding search terms to a business name or description that are not part of the real name or relevant content.

Material connection: a relationship between an endorser and a business that could affect how people weigh the endorsement.

Prompt: an instruction given to an AI tool.

Repurposing: turning one piece of content into several smaller pieces.

Review gating: asking only customers expected to be happy to leave reviews.

Review suppression: preventing or hiding negative reviews.

Substantiation: evidence that supports an advertising claim, held before the ad runs.

Tone: how a brand voice adapts to a situation or audience.

Vanity metric: a number that is easy to increase but weakly connected to revenue.
