# How to Use This Book

This book is for people who want to write grant proposals well: small business owners exploring the limited grants open to for-profit firms, founders and staff of small nonprofits, community groups, and anyone preparing a first application to a government agency, foundation, or corporate program. It assumes no prior experience. It does assume that you are willing to read funders' rules carefully, gather evidence, and write plainly.

The central argument is that grant writing is less about persuasive prose than about fit, evidence, and compliance. Reviewers fund applicants who are eligible, who propose work the funder already wants to support, who show credible evidence of need, who present a coherent plan and an honest budget, and who follow instructions exactly. Good writing matters because it makes these qualities easy to see. It cannot substitute for them.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of this book you should be able to:

- Distinguish the main types of funders and identify which are realistically open to you.
- Determine your eligibility and complete the registrations federal applications require.
- Find and screen opportunities using public databases and funders' tax filings.
- Read a notice of funding opportunity the way a reviewer reads it and build a compliance matrix.
- Write a need statement, goals and objectives, a logic model, and a work plan.
- Build a budget and budget justification consistent with federal cost principles.
- Design an evaluation plan that distinguishes outputs from outcomes.
- Submit on time, avoid grant scams, manage an award, and learn from a rejection.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, defines its key terms, and presents a step-by-step framework. Every chapter includes a worked example with real numbers, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Many chapters include case studies drawn from public sources, such as federal regulations, agency guidance, the Small Business Innovation Research program, and large public grant competitions.

The worked examples follow a fictional applicant, Kiara Wells, who owns a two-year-old for-profit LLC that teaches computer and online-safety skills to older adults, and who partners with a fictional nonprofit, the Eastside Library Foundation. Kiara and the foundation are illustrative only; they are not TTI customers, and their numbers are not predictions. The organizations described in case studies are real, and their results are their own, not TTI's.

:::callout Scope and limits
This book is educational. It is not legal, tax, or accounting advice, and it does not promise any grant, award, or amount of funding. Total Transformation Inc. is a for-profit company, not a grant agency, and does not award grants. Rules for federal awards, state programs, and private funders change; figures and requirements cited here were checked against official sources in November 2026. Always read each funder's current instructions, and consult the funder, an attorney, or an accountant when questions arise. Never pay anyone who guarantees a grant.
:::

# Chapter 1: The Grant Landscape

:::objectives
- Define a grant and distinguish it from loans, contracts, and prizes.
- Describe the major categories of grant makers and what each typically funds.
- Explain why most grants are unavailable to start or expand ordinary for-profit businesses.
- Identify the grant categories that are realistically open to small for-profit firms.
:::

A grant is a transfer of money or property from a funder to a recipient to carry out a public or charitable purpose, which the recipient generally does not repay as long as it uses the funds as agreed. That last clause matters. A grant is not a gift with no strings; it is an agreement to perform work, follow rules, and report results. Failing to meet the terms can require repayment.

Grants differ from three related instruments. A loan must be repaid with interest. A contract is a purchase of goods or services for the buyer's own use, such as a government agency hiring a firm to build software. A prize or competition rewards a past or proposed achievement, often with fewer reporting requirements. Some programs that call themselves grants are actually prizes or contests, and some government "grants" to businesses are, legally, cooperative agreements with substantial agency involvement.

## Who gives grants

| Funder type | Typical recipients | Typical purposes | Where to look |
|---|---|---|---|
| Federal agencies | States, local governments, nonprofits, universities, tribes; some small businesses (research) | Public programs, research, infrastructure, services | Grants.gov; SAM.gov Assistance Listings |
| State and local governments | Nonprofits, local businesses, individuals in some programs | Economic development, arts, workforce, public health | State agency and city websites |
| Private foundations | Mostly 501(c)(3) public charities | Mission-specific causes | Candid; funders' Form 990 filings |
| Community foundations | Local nonprofits, some individuals (scholarships) | Local needs | Local community foundation sites |
| Corporate giving programs | Nonprofits in operating communities; some small-business programs | Community relations, employee interests | Company foundation pages |
| Private competitions | Startups, small businesses, individuals | Entrepreneurship, innovation | Organizer rules pages |

Private philanthropy is large. Giving USA estimates that U.S. charitable giving reached $617.2 billion in 2025, of which foundations gave about $117 billion [1]. Nearly all of that money goes to charitable organizations, not to businesses.

## The myth of "free money" for businesses

Many first-time applicants arrive believing that government grants are widely available to start or grow a business. They are not. The U.S. Small Business Administration states directly that it does not provide grants for starting and expanding a business; its grants go mainly to nonprofits, resource partners, and educational organizations that provide counseling and training, plus a few targeted programs for research and exporting [2]. The Federal Trade Commission warns that unsolicited offers of "free government grant money" for personal or business expenses are scams [3].

What remains for small for-profit firms is narrower but real:

1. Research and development awards, primarily the Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) programs.
2. State and local economic development grants, such as façade improvement, workforce training, or disaster recovery programs.
3. Private competitions and corporate small-business grant programs.
4. Partnerships in which a nonprofit or public agency is the applicant and contracts with a business to deliver part of the work.

:::case Qualcomm and the SBIR program
The SBIR program, sometimes called "America's Seed Fund," lets participating federal agencies fund research and development by small U.S. businesses. Phase I awards, typically $50,000 to $323,000 over 6 to 12 months, test feasibility; Phase II awards, typically $650,000 to $2.2 million over about 24 months, continue development for Phase I awardees [4].

Qualcomm is one of the program's best-known alumni. According to the SBIR program's own account, Qualcomm in 1985 was a small company doing contract research for the government. SBIR awards from agencies including the National Science Foundation and the Department of Defense, totaling about $1.5 million, were among its first funding and helped it hire engineers and begin developing chips; the program credits that work with helping the company pivot from contract research toward the products that later defined it [5]. The lesson for applicants is not that a grant creates a large company—the case is exceptional—but that research grants fund a specific, risky technical question that private investors may not yet fund. Qualcomm's results are its own and not TTI's.
:::

:::example Kiara sorts her options (illustrative)
Kiara's LLC has two years of operations, no employees, and $48,000 in annual revenue. She lists every "grant" she has seen in social media ads and classifies each:

| Opportunity seen | Type | Open to her LLC? | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| "$25,000 government grant, no repayment" (text message) | Likely scam | No | Report and delete |
| Federal library services grant | Federal, nonprofit/government applicants | Not directly | Explore partnership with library |
| State digital-equity subgrant | State program, nonprofits and some businesses | Check | Read rules |
| National women-owned business competition | Private competition | Yes, if eligible | Apply if fit is strong |
| Community foundation senior services grant | Foundation, 501(c)(3) only | Not directly | Partnership |

Of five opportunities, one is fraudulent, three require a nonprofit or public applicant, and one may be directly open to her. Kiara's strategy becomes two-track: apply directly to a small number of well-fitted competitions, and help the Eastside Library Foundation apply for funds that would pay her company as a contractor for workshops.
:::

:::checklist Landscape check
- I can explain the difference between a grant, a loan, a contract, and a prize.
- I know which funder types are open to my legal status.
- I have discarded any "guaranteed grant" offers.
- I have identified at least one partnership route if direct grants are closed to me.
- I have a realistic picture of competition levels.
:::

:::summary
A grant is an agreement to perform work for a public or charitable purpose, not unconditional money. Federal agencies, state and local governments, foundations, corporations, and private competitions each fund different recipients and purposes. Foundations gave about $117 billion in 2025, almost entirely to charities. SBA does not give grants to start or expand businesses; realistic routes for small firms include SBIR/STTR research awards, state and local programs, private competitions, and partnerships with eligible applicants.
:::

# Chapter 2: Applicant Status and Readiness

:::objectives
- Document your legal status and the facts that determine eligibility.
- Complete or plan the registrations required for federal applications.
- Evaluate fiscal sponsorship and partnerships as routes to eligibility.
- Assess organizational readiness to manage an award.
:::

Every grant begins with a question that cannot be argued around: are you eligible? Funders define eligible applicants by legal form, location, size, age, purpose, and sometimes the characteristics of the people served. An ineligible application is not reviewed, no matter how strong. Grants.gov notes that every federal notice of funding opportunity contains a section defining eligibility, and that applying when you are not eligible wastes time and money [6].

## The applicant profile

Your applicant profile is a one-page record of the facts funders ask about. It should include legal name exactly as registered; legal structure (sole proprietorship, LLC, corporation, nonprofit corporation, government unit); tax status (for example, 501(c)(3) public charity); Employer Identification Number; physical location; year founded; annual revenue and number of employees; and any special designations that are true and documentable, such as location in a designated rural area or certification as a small disadvantaged business. Never claim a status you cannot prove; misrepresentation on a federal application can carry civil and criminal penalties.

## Registrations for federal awards

Federal applications require several registrations, which are free but can take weeks. As one agency summarizes, applicant organizations typically need an Employer Identification Number, a Unique Entity Identifier, a System for Award Management (SAM.gov) registration, a Login.gov account, and a Grants.gov registration [7]. SAM.gov explains that applying for federal awards as a prime recipient requires a full registration, during which a Unique Entity ID is assigned [8].

| Registration | Purpose | Time to allow | Renewal |
|---|---|---|---|
| EIN (IRS) | Federal tax identification | Same day online for most | None |
| SAM.gov registration and UEI | Eligibility for federal awards | Several weeks; longer if validation issues | Annually |
| Login.gov | Secure sign-in to federal systems | Minutes | As needed |
| Grants.gov | Submitting applications | Days, after SAM is active | Keep roles current |
| Agency-specific systems | Some agencies require their own (e.g., research portals) | Days to weeks | Varies |

Start registrations as soon as you consider any federal application. Lapsed SAM registration is a common, avoidable cause of rejected submissions. Beware of third-party firms that charge to "register" you; registration with SAM.gov is free.

## Fiscal sponsorship and partnerships

If you are not eligible directly, two routes may help.

Fiscal sponsorship is an arrangement in which an established 501(c)(3) organization accepts grants on behalf of a project that lacks its own tax-exempt status, in exchange for oversight and usually a fee. The sponsor is legally responsible to the funder, so it will exercise real control over how funds are spent.

A partnership places an eligible organization in the role of applicant, with your organization as a subrecipient or contractor. The distinction matters under federal rules: a subrecipient carries out part of the program and must follow award terms; a contractor sells goods or services to the recipient. Your role should be described accurately in the proposal and in a written agreement.

## Readiness to manage an award

Winning a grant creates obligations. Before applying, confirm that you can track grant expenses separately, document staff time, retain records, and submit reports on schedule. Funders increasingly ask about financial management systems, and federal recipients must follow detailed administrative requirements (Chapter 12).

:::example Kiara's profile and readiness (illustrative)
Kiara completes her profile: Wells Digital Skills LLC, single-member LLC in Ohio, EIN on file, founded 2024, revenue $48,000, no employees, not a nonprofit. She also records that the Eastside Library Foundation is a 501(c)(3) with a seven-member board, $410,000 in annual revenue, an audited financial statement, and an active SAM.gov registration.

Readiness scoring (1 = weak, 3 = strong):

| Capacity | Wells LLC | Eastside Library Foundation |
|---|---|---|
| Separate grant accounting | 2 (bookkeeping software, no class tracking yet) | 3 |
| Time documentation | 1 | 3 |
| Reporting experience | 1 | 3 |
| Federal registrations | 1 (not started) | 3 |
| Program delivery expertise | 3 | 2 |
| Total | 8 | 14 |

The foundation is better positioned to be the applicant; Kiara's firm is stronger at delivery. Kiara begins her own SAM.gov registration anyway, because some state programs require it, and sets up class tracking in her bookkeeping software so that grant-funded work can be reported separately.
:::

# Chapter 3: Finding and Qualifying Opportunities

:::objectives
- Search federal, state, foundation, and corporate sources systematically.
- Use funders' public tax filings to research private foundations.
- Apply a go/no-go scoring method to decide which opportunities to pursue.
- Maintain a funding pipeline.
:::

Searching for grants is research, not browsing. The goal is not to find many opportunities but to find a few where your work fits the funder's priorities, your organization is eligible, and the effort of applying is justified by the likely return.

## Sources by funder type

Federal opportunities are posted on Grants.gov, which allows searches by keyword, agency, eligibility, and Assistance Listing number. SAM.gov Assistance Listings describe federal programs in general terms—their purpose, eligibility, and typical award sizes—even when no opportunity is currently open [9]. Reading the Assistance Listing for a program lets you prepare months before a notice appears.

State and local opportunities are usually posted on agency websites (economic development, commerce, arts councils, workforce boards, health departments) and through state grant portals. Many federal dollars reach local organizations as subgrants from states.

Foundation opportunities can be researched through Candid's Foundation Directory, a subscription database also available free at many public libraries, which lets users search grantmakers, past grants, and recipients [10]. Foundations' annual returns—Form 990-PF for private foundations—are public records. ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer provides free access to Form 990 filings, including lists of grants paid, which show whom a foundation actually funds and how much [11].

Corporate and private programs are found on company foundation pages and organizers' rules pages. Read the official rules directly; secondary listings are often outdated.

## Reading a funder's history

A funder's stated priorities and its actual grants can differ. Before investing in an application, review two or three years of grants paid. Ask: What types of organizations received funds? What was the typical grant size? Were grants local or national? Did the funder support new grantees or mostly renew existing ones? Does it accept unsolicited proposals, or only invited ones?

:::case Funding programs can pause
Even established programs can stop. The SBIR and STTR programs' statutory authority expired on September 30, 2025, which halted new awards for about six months until Congress passed and the President signed a reauthorization on April 13, 2026, extending the programs through September 30, 2031 [12]. For applicants, the episode is a reminder to treat any single program as uncertain, to check a program's current status before investing in a proposal, and to keep a diversified pipeline rather than depending on one source.
:::

## Go/no-go scoring

Before writing, score each opportunity. A simple matrix scores seven factors from 0 to 3 and pursues opportunities that clear a threshold, such as 15 out of 21, with no zero on eligibility or mission fit.

| Factor | 0 | 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Eligibility | Not eligible | Clearly eligible |
| Mission fit | Funder's priorities differ | Funder's priorities match closely |
| Funding history | Funder never funds work like ours | Funds similar organizations and amounts |
| Award size vs. effort | Award too small for effort | Award justifies effort |
| Competitiveness | Very low success rate, no relationship | Reasonable odds or existing relationship |
| Capacity | Cannot manage reporting or match | Can manage fully |
| Timing | Deadline too close to do well | Ample time |

:::example Kiara and the foundation screen three opportunities (illustrative)
| Factor | State digital-equity subgrant | Regional bank foundation | National tech-company competition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eligibility | 3 (foundation applies) | 3 (foundation applies) | 2 (LLC eligible; rules unclear on age) |
| Mission fit | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| Funding history | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| Award size vs. effort | 3 ($75,000) | 2 ($15,000) | 1 ($10,000, long application) |
| Competitiveness | 2 | 2 | 0 |
| Capacity | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| Timing | 2 (9 weeks) | 3 (rolling) | 3 |
| Total | 17 | 18 | 11 |

Decision: pursue the bank foundation (rolling deadline, strong history of local senior-services grants) and the state subgrant; decline the national competition, whose announced award count and past winner lists suggested thousands of applicants for a handful of awards with weak mission fit. The team estimates the competition would take 20 hours for a very low probability of a $10,000 award—an expected value well below the alternatives.
:::

## The funding pipeline

A pipeline is a tracked list of opportunities by stage: identified, researched, qualified, in preparation, submitted, awarded or declined. Track deadlines, amounts requested, decision dates, and contacts. A healthy pipeline for a small organization might include eight to twelve qualified opportunities a year, with a few in active preparation at any time.

:::checklist Opportunity research
- I have searched Grants.gov and relevant Assistance Listings.
- I have checked state and local agency sources.
- I have reviewed at least two years of each target foundation's grants paid.
- I know whether each foundation accepts unsolicited proposals.
- I have scored every opportunity before writing.
- My pipeline tracks stage, deadline, amount, and contact.
:::

:::summary
Search systematically: Grants.gov and SAM.gov Assistance Listings for federal programs, agency sites for state and local funds, Candid and Form 990 filings for foundations, and official rules pages for private programs. A funder's grants paid reveal more than its stated priorities. Programs can pause, as SBIR did in 2025–2026, so diversify. Score each opportunity on eligibility, fit, history, size, competitiveness, capacity, and timing before investing effort, and track everything in a pipeline.
:::

:::review
1. What information does an Assistance Listing provide that a funding notice may not?
2. How can Form 990-PF filings help you evaluate a foundation?
3. What happened to the SBIR program between September 2025 and April 2026, and what does it teach applicants?
4. List the seven go/no-go factors.
5. Why might a smaller award be a better choice than a larger competition?
:::

# Chapter 4: Reading the Solicitation Like a Reviewer

:::objectives
- Identify the standard sections of a federal notice of funding opportunity.
- Explain how review criteria and scoring drive funding decisions.
- Build a compliance matrix that maps every requirement to a location in the proposal.
- Prepare questions for the funder's program officer.
:::

A solicitation—called a notice of funding opportunity (NOFO) in federal grants, and a request for proposals (RFP) or guidelines elsewhere—is the single most important document in the process. It tells you who may apply, what the funder wants, how proposals will be scored, and exactly what to submit. Experienced applicants read it several times before writing a word.

## Anatomy of a solicitation

Federal NOFOs vary by agency but generally include: a program description stating purpose and priorities; federal award information (amounts, number of awards, period of performance); eligibility information, including cost-sharing requirements; application and submission information (format, page limits, required forms, deadline); application review information (criteria, weights, review process); award administration information (reporting, terms); and agency contacts.

Foundations' guidelines are shorter but address similar points: priorities, eligibility, amount ranges, required content, and process.

## How reviewers score

Most competitive programs use structured review. Reviewers—agency staff, outside experts, or peers—score each proposal against published criteria, often with assigned points or weights. Your job is to make each criterion easy to score.

The National Institutes of Health provides a well-documented example. NIH reviewers assign an overall impact score on a 9-point scale, where 1 is exceptional and 9 is poor, using whole numbers only; NIH guidance notes that 5 is considered an average score and that there is no formula converting criterion scores into the overall score, which reflects reviewers' judgment [13].

:::case A peer-reviewed open call
MacKenzie Scott's Yield Giving, working with Lever for Change, ran an open call in 2023 for community-led nonprofits serving people and places of meager or modest means. The call received 6,353 applications and initially planned 250 awards of $1 million each. Top-rated organizations, scored first by their peers, advanced to an external evaluation panel and due diligence. The donors then expanded the pool: 279 organizations received $2 million each and 82 received $1 million, for 361 awards totaling $640 million, announced on March 19, 2024 [14]. Two lessons apply broadly. Competitive odds are often steep—here roughly 6 percent of applicants were funded. And review processes increasingly involve multiple stages and multiple kinds of readers, so a proposal must be clear to a peer practitioner and an outside expert alike. The awardees' results are their own and not TTI's.
:::

## The compliance matrix

A compliance matrix is a table listing every requirement in the solicitation—content, format, forms, attachments, limits—with the place in your proposal that satisfies it and the person responsible. It prevents the most avoidable failure in grant writing: rejection for noncompliance.

1. Read the solicitation and highlight every "must," "shall," "required," and "will be scored."
2. Enter each item in the matrix, quoting the language and noting the page of the solicitation.
3. Add review criteria as rows, with their points or weights.
4. Assign each row to a proposal section and an owner.
5. Before submission, check each row and record where it is satisfied.

:::example Compliance matrix excerpt (illustrative)
The state digital-equity subgrant's guidelines specify a 10-page narrative, 12-point font, and five scored criteria. The Eastside Library Foundation's matrix begins:

| Requirement (quoted) | Points | Proposal location | Owner | Done |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Narrative shall not exceed 10 pages" | — | Whole narrative | Grants lead | |
| "Describe the population and evidence of need" | 20 | Section 2 | Kiara | |
| "Measurable objectives and timeline" | 25 | Sections 3–4 | Kiara | |
| "Organizational capacity and partners" | 20 | Section 5 | Foundation director | |
| "Evaluation plan" | 20 | Section 6 | Kiara | |
| "Budget and justification consistent with program" | 15 | Attachment B | Foundation finance | |
| "Letters of commitment from partners" | — | Attachment C | Foundation director | |

Because objectives and timeline carry 25 of 100 points, the team allocates about a quarter of the narrative's ten pages—two and a half pages—to them. Space in the narrative should roughly follow the points.
:::

## Asking good questions

Most federal NOFOs and many foundations name a program officer. Contacting them is appropriate and often valuable, within the rules stated in the solicitation. Prepare a one-paragraph project summary and specific questions that the guidelines do not answer, such as whether a particular cost is allowable or whether a partnership structure is acceptable. Do not ask questions answered in the document. Record answers in writing, and check any posted frequently-asked-questions pages, which may be updated before the deadline.

:::summary
The solicitation defines eligibility, priorities, format, and scoring; read it several times. Federal NOFOs share a common structure. Reviewers score against published criteria—NIH, for example, uses a 1-to-9 overall impact scale where 5 is average. Competition can be steep, as Yield Giving's 6,353 applications for 361 awards show. A compliance matrix maps every requirement to a proposal location and owner; allocate space roughly in proportion to points. Ask program officers specific questions the document does not answer.
:::

# Chapter 5: The Need Statement

:::objectives
- Define a need statement and distinguish need from the applicant's own wants.
- Select credible, current, local evidence to document a problem.
- Structure a need statement that leads logically to your proposed solution.
- Avoid common errors, including circular reasoning and deficit-only framing.
:::

The need statement, sometimes called the problem statement or statement of need, explains the condition your project will address, for whom, and why it matters now. It is the foundation of the proposal: objectives, activities, and evaluation all follow from it. A reviewer who is not persuaded that a real, specific problem exists will not be persuaded by anything else.

## Need versus want

The most common error in need statements is describing the applicant's need rather than the community's or the field's. "Our organization needs a van" or "We lack funding for staff" describes an organizational want. A need statement describes a condition affecting people or a field: "Older adults in the service area lack transportation to medical appointments, and missed appointments are associated with poorer outcomes." The van may be a reasonable solution, but it is not the need.

A related error is circular reasoning: defining the problem as the absence of your solution. "There is no digital-skills class for seniors in our neighborhood" assumes that a class is the answer. A stronger statement describes the underlying condition—for example, that many older residents cannot safely use online services they increasingly need—and then argues that a class is an effective response.

## Evidence

Credible evidence is current, relevant to the population and place you will serve, from reliable sources, and cited. Combine three levels:

1. National or state context from authoritative sources, which shows the problem is real and recognized.
2. Local data for your service area—from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey, state agencies, health departments, school districts, or local institutions [15].
3. Direct evidence from the people you serve: surveys, intake records, waiting lists, interviews, and documented requests.

Local and direct evidence carry the most weight because they show the problem exists where you will work. National statistics alone suggest that you have not studied your own community.

| Evidence type | Example source | Strength | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| National statistics | Federal surveys, research centers | Establishes significance | May not reflect your area |
| Local public data | American Community Survey, state agencies | Shows local scale | Margins of error for small areas |
| Organizational data | Intake logs, waiting lists | Shows demand you observe | Possible selection bias |
| Participant voice | Surveys, interviews, focus groups | Shows lived experience | Small samples; report honestly |
| Research literature | Peer-reviewed studies | Shows the approach can work | Context may differ |

## Structure

A strong need statement typically moves through five steps:

1. State the problem in one or two sentences.
2. Show its scale with national, then local, data.
3. Describe who is affected and how, using direct evidence.
4. Explain the consequences of inaction.
5. Identify the gap your project addresses and why your approach fits.

## Framing

Funders and communities increasingly object to deficit-only framing—describing people solely by their problems. Pair evidence of need with evidence of assets: skills, relationships, institutions, and the community's own efforts. This framing is more respectful and often more accurate, and it makes your solution more credible because it builds on what exists.

:::example Kiara drafts the need statement (illustrative)
Kiara and the foundation's director draft the following for the state digital-equity subgrant, using real national figures and illustrative local ones:

"Older adults increasingly need to use the internet safely to manage health care, banking, and benefits, yet many lack the skills and confidence to do so, and they are frequent targets of online fraud. Nationally, 79 percent of adults 65 and older own a smartphone, compared with 97 percent of adults under 50, according to the Pew Research Center [16]. In 2025, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center received more than 201,000 complaints from people 60 and older, reporting losses of more than $7.7 billion [17].

In the library's service area, the American Community Survey estimates that about 6,200 residents are 65 or older [illustrative local figure]. Over the past twelve months, library staff logged 412 one-on-one requests from older patrons for help with tasks such as patient portals, video calls, and identifying suspicious messages—requests that exceed the staff's capacity. A survey of 118 older patrons found that 64 percent had received a message they believed was a scam in the prior three months, and 41 percent said they avoid online banking because they fear making a mistake.

These residents also bring strengths: most own a smartphone, many are active library users, and a group of volunteers has offered to serve as practice partners. The proposed program builds on these assets with structured, small-group instruction and supervised practice."

The statement moves from problem to national scale to local scale to direct evidence to assets, and it describes the condition—unsafe and limited internet use—before naming the solution.
:::

:::checklist Need statement review
- The need describes a condition affecting people, not my organization's wants.
- I have not defined the problem as the absence of my solution.
- National data are current and cited.
- I include local data for my service area.
- I include direct evidence from the people I serve.
- Consequences of inaction are stated.
- Community assets are acknowledged.
- Every statistic has a source.
:::

:::summary
A need statement documents a condition affecting people or a field, not the applicant's wants, and avoids defining the problem as the absence of the proposed solution. Combine national context, local data such as the American Community Survey, and direct evidence from participants. Structure the statement from problem to scale to experience to consequences to gap, and pair evidence of need with evidence of community assets.
:::

# Chapter 6: Goals, Objectives, and the Logic Model

:::objectives
- Distinguish goals, objectives, activities, outputs, and outcomes.
- Write SMART objectives.
- Build a logic model that links resources to intended results.
- Use a logic model to test the coherence of a proposal.
:::

Once the need is established, the proposal must show what the project will achieve and how. Reviewers look for a clear chain from resources to activities to results. Two tools make that chain visible: well-written objectives and a logic model.

## Definitions

A goal is a broad statement of the long-term change you seek, such as "Older adults in the service area use the internet safely and confidently." Goals are not usually measured directly.

An objective is a specific, measurable result that contributes to the goal within the project period.

Activities are what the project does: classes, coaching sessions, outreach.

Outputs are the direct, countable products of activities: number of classes held, number of participants served.

Outcomes are changes in participants or conditions: increased skills, changed behaviors, improved status. Short-term outcomes usually involve knowledge and skills; intermediate outcomes, behavior; long-term outcomes, conditions.

Confusing outputs with outcomes is the most frequent weakness reviewers note. "Serve 200 seniors" is an output. "Increase the share of participants who can identify a phishing message from 40 to 80 percent" is an outcome.

## SMART objectives

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's evaluation guidance describes SMART objectives as specific (who and what, with one action verb), measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound [18].

| Weak objective | Problem | SMART version |
|---|---|---|
| Improve seniors' tech skills | Not measurable or time-bound | By month 12, 75% of 160 participants will complete three core online tasks unassisted, as measured by a skills checklist |
| Teach online safety | Activity, not result | By month 12, the share of participants correctly identifying 4 of 5 sample scam messages will rise from a baseline of about 40% to at least 75% |
| Serve many people | Vague output | Deliver 20 six-session cohorts of 8 participants (160 total) by month 11 |

## The logic model

A logic model is a visual summary of how a program is expected to work. The W.K. Kellogg Foundation's widely used Logic Model Development Guide describes it as a systematic way to present the relationships among the resources you have, the activities you plan, and the changes or results you hope to achieve [19]. A basic logic model has five columns: inputs (resources), activities, outputs, outcomes, and impact. Many models also list assumptions and external factors.

Reading a logic model from left to right, each column should be an "if-then" consequence of the one before: if we have these resources, then we can carry out these activities; if we carry out these activities, then we will produce these outputs; if participants receive these outputs, then these outcomes will follow.

:::case The Kellogg Foundation's logic model guide
The W.K. Kellogg Foundation published its Logic Model Development Guide to help grantees connect planning, evaluation, and action; the guide is still used by public agencies and funders, which often reproduce or adapt it [19]. Its practical contribution is to insist that a program's theory be written down before work begins, so that planners can find gaps—an activity with no outcome, an outcome with no activity, an assumption no one has tested—while they are still cheap to fix. Many federal and foundation solicitations now request a logic model or something like it. The guide is the foundation's work, and its use here is educational.
:::

:::example Kiara's logic model (illustrative)
| Inputs | Activities | Outputs | Short-term outcomes | Intermediate outcomes | Long-term impact |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Instructor (Kiara), 0.4 FTE coordinator, 10 volunteers, library lab with 10 tablets, curriculum | 20 six-session cohorts; weekly drop-in practice; 4 community scam-awareness talks | 160 participants complete ≥4 of 6 sessions; 40 drop-in sessions; 4 talks reaching ~200 people | Participants can complete core tasks; identify scam messages | Participants use patient portals, video calls, and banking safely; report scams | Older adults in service area participate more safely and fully in online life |

Assumptions: participants own or can borrow a device; library staff can refer patrons; volunteers remain engaged.
External factors: new scam tactics; changes in health-system portals.

Testing the chain: one activity—community talks—had no corresponding outcome. The team added a short-term outcome ("talk attendees can name two signs of a scam and know where to report") and a measure (a two-question exit card), keeping the model internally consistent.
:::

## Using the model to test coherence

Before writing the narrative, check the logic model against the need statement and budget:

1. Does every element of the need map to at least one outcome?
2. Does every outcome have activities likely to produce it?
3. Does every activity require resources that appear in the budget?
4. Does every budget item support an activity?
5. Are the outcomes measurable within the project period, or should they be described as longer-term aims?

:::worksheet Objectives and logic model
Project goal: ___
Objective 1 (SMART): ___
Objective 2 (SMART): ___
Objective 3 (SMART): ___
Inputs: ___
Activities: ___
Outputs: ___
Short-term outcomes: ___
Intermediate outcomes: ___
Key assumptions: ___
:::

:::summary
Goals state long-term aims; objectives state specific, measurable results; activities produce outputs; outcomes are changes in people or conditions. Write objectives that are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound, and do not confuse outputs with outcomes. A logic model, as described in the Kellogg Foundation's guide, shows the "if-then" chain from inputs to impact and exposes gaps between need, activities, outcomes, and budget.
:::

# Chapter 7: The Project Plan and Work Plan

:::objectives
- Describe a project's approach, including why it is likely to work.
- Build a work plan with tasks, responsible parties, and timelines.
- Present staffing and management structure clearly.
- Identify project risks and mitigation strategies.
:::

The project plan—also called the project description, approach, or methods—explains what you will do, how, when, and by whom. Reviewers use it to judge whether your activities can plausibly produce your outcomes and whether your team can carry them out.

## Describing the approach

Explain the approach in plain language and justify it. Justification can draw on research evidence, recognized models, your own track record, or pilot results. Avoid jargon and unexplained acronyms. Reviewers often read many proposals in a short time; a clear description of who will do what, for whom, how often, and where earns more credit than elaborate language.

Include recruitment and retention. Many projects fail not because the service is poor but because the intended participants never arrive or do not stay. Describe how you will reach participants, what barriers they face, and how you will reduce them.

## The work plan

A work plan is a table or chart listing tasks, responsible parties, start and end dates, and milestones. It demonstrates that you have thought through sequence and workload.

1. List major phases: start-up, delivery, evaluation, reporting, close-out.
2. Break each phase into tasks that can be assigned and completed.
3. Assign one responsible person to each task.
4. Set dates, allowing for start-up delays, holidays, and procurement time.
5. Mark milestones that show progress—for example, "first cohort completed."
6. Check that staff time in the work plan matches staff time in the budget.

:::example Kiara's work plan, first two quarters (illustrative)
| Task | Lead | Months | Milestone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sign partnership agreement; set up grant accounting | Foundation director | 1 | Agreement executed |
| Hire 0.4 FTE coordinator | Foundation director | 1–2 | Coordinator starts |
| Finalize curriculum and skills checklist | Kiara | 1–2 | Curriculum approved |
| Recruit and train 10 volunteers | Coordinator | 2–3 | 10 volunteers trained |
| Outreach through library, senior center, clinics | Coordinator | 2–11 | 30 sign-ups by month 3 |
| Cohorts 1–6 (three overlapping sections) | Kiara | 3–5 | 48 participants complete |
| Baseline and exit assessments | Coordinator | 3–12 | Data entered monthly |
| Community scam-awareness talk 1 | Kiara | 4 | ~50 attendees |
| Quarterly report to funder | Foundation director | 3, 6 | Reports submitted on time |

After a two-month start-up, the plan runs three overlapping sections at a time (morning, afternoon, and evening), each meeting weekly for six weeks. That pace completes about six cohorts every quarter and a half, reaching 20 cohorts by month 11 and leaving month 12 for final assessments and reporting. Kiara's teaching load is about six hours a week, which matches the contractual line in the budget.
:::

## Staffing and management

Describe each key role, its time commitment (as a percentage of full-time or hours per week), and the qualifications of named individuals. Include an organizational chart if the project involves multiple organizations. Explain how decisions are made and problems are escalated.

One practical protocol for team decision-making is the "1-3-1" rule taught by entrepreneur Dan Martell: when a team member brings a problem, they define one problem, propose three options, and recommend one [20], [21]. In grant-funded projects with several partners, a protocol like this reduces bottlenecks and creates a written record of decisions—useful when a funder later asks why a change was made. Martell's methods are his own and not TTI's.

## Risks and mitigation

Reviewers respect applicants who anticipate problems. Identify the most likely and most damaging risks and explain your response.

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low enrollment | Medium | High | Multiple referral partners; waitlist; flexible schedules |
| Participant dropout | Medium | Medium | Reminder calls; volunteer buddies; make-up sessions |
| Coordinator turnover | Low | High | Documented procedures; cross-trained volunteer lead |
| Technology failure | Low | Medium | Spare devices; offline materials |
| Delayed start | Medium | Medium | Two-month start-up buffer in work plan |

:::checklist Project plan review
- The approach is explained in plain language and justified.
- Recruitment and retention strategies are described.
- Every task in the work plan has one responsible person.
- Dates allow for start-up, procurement, and holidays.
- Milestones show measurable progress.
- Staff time in the work plan matches the budget.
- The top risks and mitigations are stated.
- Decision-making and escalation are explained.
:::

:::summary
The project plan describes and justifies the approach, including how participants will be recruited and retained. A work plan lists tasks, owners, dates, and milestones, and must match the budget. Describe staffing and management clearly; a decision protocol such as Martell's 1-3-1 rule can help partner teams. Identify likely risks and explain mitigation.

# Chapter 8: Budgets and Budget Justification

:::objectives
- Organize a project budget using standard cost categories.
- Write a budget justification that explains every cost.
- Distinguish direct and indirect costs, and explain the federal de minimis rate.
- Understand cost sharing, matching, and allowability.
:::

The budget translates the project plan into dollars. Reviewers read it as a test of realism and integrity: do the costs match the activities, are they reasonable, and are they allowable under the funder's rules? A budget that is padded, inconsistent with the narrative, or missing obvious costs undermines confidence in the entire proposal.

## Standard categories

Federal applications typically use the categories on the SF-424A budget form, and many other funders use similar ones:

| Category | Includes | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Personnel | Salaries and wages for staff time on the project | Show % of time or hours |
| Fringe benefits | Payroll taxes, health insurance, retirement | Usually a rate applied to salaries |
| Travel | Mileage, lodging, airfare for project purposes | Show trips, people, rates |
| Equipment | Items above a capitalization threshold with useful life over a year | Federal threshold is set in the regulations; check the current figure |
| Supplies | Consumables and items below the equipment threshold | Itemize major supplies |
| Contractual | Contractors and subrecipients | Describe scope and basis of cost |
| Other | Rent, printing, participant support, software | Explain each |
| Indirect costs | Shared organizational costs | Negotiated rate or de minimis rate |

## Direct and indirect costs

Direct costs can be identified specifically with the project—an instructor's time, workshop supplies. Indirect costs, sometimes called facilities and administrative costs or overhead, are shared costs that benefit multiple programs, such as accounting, general administration, and building costs.

Under the federal Uniform Guidance, 2 CFR Part 200, recipients and subrecipients without a current negotiated indirect cost rate may elect to charge a de minimis rate of up to 15 percent of modified total direct costs (MTDC) [22]. This rate was raised from 10 percent in revisions effective in 2024, and the Federal Emergency Management Agency notes that the de minimis rate does not require documentation to justify its use [23]. MTDC excludes certain items, such as equipment, participant support costs, and the portion of each subaward above a set amount; check the regulation for the current exclusions. Foundations set their own indirect cost policies, which range from none to a stated percentage.

## Allowability

Under federal cost principles, a cost must generally be necessary and reasonable for the project, allocable to it, consistent with the organization's policies, and adequately documented. Some costs are unallowable on federal awards regardless of purpose—for example, entertainment and alcohol. Foundations may also exclude costs such as endowments, capital campaigns, or lobbying.

## Cost sharing and match

Cost sharing (or matching) is the portion of project costs not paid by the funder. Some programs require it; others consider voluntary cost sharing in scoring; still others discourage it. Match can be cash or in-kind—donated space, volunteer time valued at a documented rate, or donated equipment. Only commit match you can document; committed cost sharing becomes an obligation you must track and report.

## The budget justification

The budget justification (or narrative) explains the basis for each cost. A good justification shows the calculation and connects the cost to a specific activity.

:::example Kiara's partnership budget (illustrative)
| Line | Calculation | Request |
|---|---|---|
| Personnel: coordinator | 0.4 FTE × $46,000 × 12 months | $18,400 |
| Fringe | 22% × $18,400 | $4,048 |
| Contractual: Wells Digital Skills LLC instruction | 20 cohorts × 6 sessions × 2 hours × $55/hr = $13,200; curriculum adaptation 40 hrs × $55 = $2,200 | $15,400 |
| Supplies: 10 tablets | 10 × $280 | $2,800 |
| Supplies: printed guides | 200 × $9 | $1,800 |
| Other: volunteer training and background checks | 10 × $45 | $450 |
| Other: outreach printing | Flyers and posters | $600 |
| Travel: local mileage | 1,500 miles × $0.70 (illustrative rate) | $1,050 |
| Total direct costs | | $44,548 |
| Indirect (15% de minimis of MTDC) | 15% × $44,548 | $6,682 |
| Total request | | $51,230 |
| In-kind match: library lab space | 240 hours × $40 | $9,600 (not requested) |

Notes: The tablets fall below the equipment threshold, so they are supplies. For simplicity here, all direct costs are treated as part of MTDC; in a real federal proposal, the team would confirm which costs are excluded from MTDC and how contractor costs are treated. The state program's cap is $75,000, so the $51,230 request is well within range. The volunteer and staff time in the budget matches the work plan in Chapter 7.
:::

- Each line shows its calculation.

# Chapter 9: Evaluation and Performance Measurement

:::objectives
- Distinguish process evaluation from outcome evaluation.
- Select indicators, data sources, and collection methods for each objective.
- Establish baselines and realistic targets.
- Describe evaluation limitations honestly.
:::

An evaluation plan tells the funder how you will know whether the project worked. It also tells you. A well-designed plan improves the project during implementation, produces evidence for future proposals, and protects you from claiming results you cannot support.

## Two kinds of evaluation

Process evaluation asks whether the project was carried out as planned: Were activities delivered on schedule? Did the intended participants enroll? Were sessions delivered with fidelity to the curriculum? It explains why outcomes did or did not occur.

Outcome evaluation asks whether the intended changes happened: Did participants' knowledge, skills, behaviors, or conditions change? By how much? For whom?

Small projects rarely have the resources for rigorous designs with randomized control groups, and reviewers do not expect them. They do expect a plan proportionate to the project, with clear indicators, credible data, and honest acknowledgment of limits.

## Indicators and data

An indicator is a specific, observable measure of progress toward an objective. For each objective, specify the indicator, the data source, the collection method and timing, who collects it, and the target.

| Method | Good for | Cost | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attendance and service records | Outputs, dosage | Low | Only as good as record-keeping |
| Pre/post skills assessment | Short-term outcomes | Low–medium | Practice effects; design carefully |
| Surveys | Attitudes, self-reported behavior | Low | Self-report bias; low response |
| Observation checklists | Skill demonstration | Medium | Observer training needed |
| Follow-up contacts | Intermediate outcomes | Medium | Attrition |
| Administrative data | Conditions over time | Varies | Access and privacy rules |

## Baselines and targets

A baseline is the value of an indicator before the project begins. Without a baseline, you cannot show change. When a baseline is unknown, plan to measure it at intake, and say so. Set targets that are ambitious but plausible, informed by pilot data, research, or comparable programs. Overpromising is a common and costly error: a funder may hold you to the number you proposed.

## A step-by-step evaluation plan

1. List each objective from your logic model.
2. Choose one to three indicators per objective, mixing outputs and outcomes.
3. Identify the data source and method for each indicator.
4. Set timing: intake, mid-point, exit, follow-up.
5. Establish or plan to establish baselines.
6. Set targets.
7. Assign responsibility for collection, entry, and analysis.
8. Describe how findings will be used during the project and reported.
9. State limitations and how you will address them.
10. Budget for evaluation time and any tools.

:::example Kiara's evaluation matrix (illustrative)
| Objective | Indicator | Source / method | Timing | Baseline | Target |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deliver 20 cohorts, 160 completers | Cohorts held; participants attending ≥4 of 6 sessions | Attendance log | Weekly | 0 | 20 cohorts; 160 completers |
| Core-task skills | % completing 3 core tasks unassisted | Observed skills checklist | Intake and session 6 | Measured at intake (pilot: ~25%) | 75% at exit |
| Scam recognition | % correctly identifying ≥4 of 5 sample messages | Five-item quiz | Intake and session 6 | Measured at intake (pilot: ~40%) | 75% at exit |
| Safe use in daily life | % reporting use of patient portal or video call in past month | Phone follow-up survey | 60 days after exit | Measured at intake | 60% |
| Process fidelity | % of sessions covering all planned modules | Instructor checklist | Each session | — | 90% |

Limitations stated in the proposal: the design has no comparison group, so improvements cannot be attributed solely to the program; the 60-day follow-up relies on self-report and may have attrition. The team will report response rates and compare follow-up respondents with non-respondents on intake characteristics.

Analysis: if 148 of 160 completers take both assessments and the share correctly identifying scam messages rises from 38 percent (56 of 148) to 79 percent (117 of 148), the team will report those counts, not just the percentages, and note that the target was met.
:::

## Using evaluation during the project

Review process data monthly. If attendance drops in a particular cohort, investigate and adjust. Funders generally welcome mid-course corrections that are documented and explained; they are less forgiving of problems discovered only in a final report. Federal rules require recipients to submit performance reports as specified in the award, no more often than quarterly and no less often than annually unless specific conditions apply [24].

:::summary

Proportionate designs are acceptable for small projects, but limitations must be stated honestly. Report counts as well as percentages, use process data to make documented mid-course corrections, and meet the reporting schedule set in the award.

# Chapter 10: Organizational Capacity, Partnerships, and Sustainability

:::objectives
- Present organizational capacity credibly and concisely.
- Structure partnerships with written agreements and letters of commitment.
- Write a sustainability plan that funders find realistic.
- Distinguish letters of support from letters of commitment.
:::

Funders invest in organizations as well as ideas. The capacity section answers the reviewer's question, "Can these people do this?" The sustainability section answers, "What happens when our money runs out?" Both require evidence, not assertion.

## Demonstrating capacity

Capacity has several dimensions: mission alignment, relevant track record, qualified staff, sound financial management, governance, and facilities. Present each concisely, with evidence: years of operation, numbers served, prior grants managed successfully, audit status, credentials of key staff, and partner strengths. New organizations can draw on the experience of key individuals and partners, and on pilot results, while being honest about their youth.

| Capacity dimension | Evidence to include |
|---|---|
| Track record | Prior programs, numbers served, outcomes achieved |
| Staff | Names, roles, qualifications, time on project |
| Financial management | Accounting system, audit or review status, prior grant compliance |
| Governance | Board composition and oversight role |
| Facilities and equipment | Locations, accessibility, technology |
| Partnerships | Roles, commitments, history of collaboration |

## Partnerships

Partnerships can strengthen proposals when each partner contributes something necessary, but reviewers are skeptical of lists of partners with vague roles. For each partner, state what they will do, what they will contribute, and how coordination will work. Formalize significant partnerships with a memorandum of understanding (MOU) or subaward agreement that specifies roles, deliverables, payment terms, data sharing, and dispute resolution.

A letter of support expresses general endorsement. A letter of commitment specifies resources a partner will provide—staff time, space, referrals, match—and is far more persuasive. Draft letters for partners if they agree, but ensure each letter is accurate and specific to the partner; identical letters from multiple partners signal a lack of real engagement.

## Sustainability

Sustainability means continuing the project's benefits after the grant ends. Few funders expect a single grant to create permanent funding, but they want to see a realistic plan. Options include:

1. Diversified grant funding from additional sources.
2. Earned revenue, such as modest fees or contracts with institutions that benefit.
3. Integration into a partner's ongoing operations or budget.
4. Volunteer or peer-led models that reduce ongoing costs.
5. Scaling down to a sustainable core while preserving key benefits.

Be specific about which option, with what evidence. "We will seek additional funding" is not a plan. "The library has agreed to include drop-in practice sessions in its regular programming after year one, staffed by trained volunteers, at an estimated annual cost of $4,200 from its operating budget" is.

:::case Why funders emphasize trust and capacity
The Yield Giving open call described in Chapter 4 asked applicants to describe the impact they had already had on the well-being of people in their communities, and then used peer review, an external panel, and due diligence to select awardees [14]. Lever for Change, which ran the process, describes its open calls as supporting a transparent and trust-based dialogue between funders and grantees [25]. Whatever a particular funder's philosophy, the pattern is common: funders look for evidence that an organization has already earned its community's trust and has the systems to use money well. For small applicants, the practical implication is to document existing work—numbers served, testimonials gathered with permission, partner relationships—long before a large opportunity appears. The awardees' and organizers' results are their own and not TTI's.
:::

# Chapter 11: Writing, Submission, and Scam Avoidance

:::objectives
- Apply principles of clear writing to proposal narratives.
- Organize an internal review process before submission.
- Submit on time and avoid technical failures.
- Recognize and avoid grant scams.
:::

With the substance in place—need, objectives, logic model, plan, budget, evaluation, capacity—the remaining work is to present it clearly, check it thoroughly, and submit it correctly. Small failures at this stage defeat strong proposals every cycle.

## Writing for reviewers

Reviewers are busy, often reading many proposals under deadline. Write so that they can score you quickly.

1. Mirror the solicitation's headings and order, using its terms.
2. Lead each section with its main point; support it afterward.
3. Use short paragraphs, plain words, and active voice.
4. Define acronyms at first use, and use few of them.
5. Use tables for timelines, budgets, and evaluation plans.
6. Replace adjectives with evidence: not "highly successful program" but "79 percent of pilot participants completed."
7. Keep claims consistent across sections; reviewers notice when numbers differ.
8. Respect page limits and formatting rules exactly.

| Weak phrasing | Stronger phrasing |
|---|---|
| "We are uniquely positioned to address this critical need." | "The library already receives about 400 requests a year for this help and has the space and partners to meet them." |
| "Participants will be empowered." | "Participants will complete three core online tasks without help." |
| "Innovative, cutting-edge curriculum" | "A six-session curriculum adapted from our 2025 pilot, in which 19 of 24 participants completed" |

## Internal review

Schedule a review at least a week before the deadline. Ideally, use two kinds of reviewers: a content reviewer who knows the field and scores the draft against the published criteria, and a "cold reader" unfamiliar with the project who reports what is unclear. Then a final compliance check against the matrix from Chapter 4.

## Submission

Technical problems are a leading cause of missed deadlines. Grants.gov guidance distributed with federal application packages recommends submitting at least 24 to 48 hours before the close date to allow time to correct technical issues [26]. After submitting, confirm receipt and validation; Grants.gov issues tracking numbers and status notifications, and agencies may reject packages that fail validation. For foundation portals, save drafts often and download a copy of the submitted application.

## Grant scams

Fraud targets people looking for grants. The FTC warns that offers of free money from government grants—for education, home repairs, home business expenses, or unpaid bills—are scams, and its guidance describes common tactics: unsolicited calls, texts, or social media messages claiming you qualify, requests for a fee to "release" the grant, and requests for bank account information [3]. The FTC published a further alert in March 2026 about grant scams offering free money for personal expenses [27].

Warning signs include: you are told you qualify for a grant you never applied for; you must pay a fee to receive it; the sender uses an official-sounding name not found on government websites; you are asked for bank or Social Security information by phone or message; or you are pressured to act immediately. Real federal opportunities are listed on Grants.gov, and registering in SAM.gov is free.

# Chapter 12: After the Decision: Awards and Rejections

:::objectives
- Understand the obligations that follow a grant award.
- Set up financial and programmatic systems for compliance.
- Identify federal requirements for records and audits.
- Use reviewer feedback to improve and resubmit.
:::

The decision letter is not the end of the process. An award begins a period of performance with legal obligations; a declination begins a learning cycle. Experienced grant professionals treat both as part of the same discipline.

## When you are funded

Read the award document, terms and conditions, and any incorporated regulations before spending money. Note the period of performance, the approved budget, reporting deadlines, prior-approval requirements (for example, for budget changes or key staff changes), and closeout procedures. Costs incurred before the start date are generally not allowable unless the award explicitly permits them.

Set up systems:

1. Financial: Track grant revenue and expenses separately, by budget category. Keep receipts, invoices, and payroll records.
2. Time and effort: Document staff time charged to the grant.
3. Program data: Collect evaluation data as planned.
4. Calendar: Enter every report due date and milestone.
5. Communication: Notify the program officer promptly of significant changes or problems.

## Federal requirements to know

Federal awards are governed by the Uniform Guidance, 2 CFR Part 200, and by each agency's terms. Two requirements are especially important:

- Records must generally be retained for three years from the date of submission of the final financial report [28].
- A non-federal entity that expends $1,000,000 or more in federal awards during its fiscal year must have a single or program-specific audit for that year [29].

Performance and financial reports must be submitted as the award requires; annual performance reports are due no later than 90 calendar days after the reporting period [24].

| Obligation | Frequency | Responsible | Record kept |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expense tracking by category | Ongoing | Finance | Ledger, receipts |
| Time and effort documentation | Each pay period | Staff and supervisor | Timesheets |
| Performance report | As specified (quarterly to annually) | Program lead | Report and data |
| Financial report | As specified | Finance | Report |
| Prior approval requests | As needed | Director | Correspondence |
| Closeout reports | End of award | Director and finance | Final reports |
| Record retention | 3 years after final financial report (federal) | Finance | Archive |

## When you are declined

Most applications are declined, including many good ones. Treat a declination as information:

1. Request reviewer comments or a debrief, if the funder offers them.
2. Sort comments into weaknesses you can fix (clarity, missing data, budget errors), weaknesses that reflect fit (priorities differ), and comments you disagree with.
3. Decide whether to resubmit, revise for another funder, or drop the opportunity.
4. If resubmitting, address each significant comment explicitly, where the funder allows.
5. Record lessons in a reusable "boilerplate library" of verified organizational descriptions, data, and budgets.

Funders' resubmission rules differ. NIH, for example, permits a single resubmission (designated A1) of an unfunded new, renewal, or revision application, which must be submitted within 37 months of the original [30].

:::example Kiara's two decisions (illustrative)
The regional bank foundation awarded $15,000. The state subgrant declined, with reviewer scores of 16/20 for need, 18/25 for objectives and timeline, 17/20 for capacity, 12/20 for evaluation, and 13/15 for budget—76 of 100, while funded applications scored 82 or higher.

Analysis: the evaluation section lost the most points. Reviewer comments said the 60-day follow-up lacked a plan for reaching participants and that the skills checklist was not described. Objectives lost points for an unclear timeline. Both are fixable.

Plan: the team adds a follow-up protocol (two call attempts plus a mailed postcard), attaches the skills checklist, and adds a Gantt chart. Using the bank foundation's grant, they run eight cohorts, generating outcome data that strengthen the next state application. They also set up the award systems: a separate class in the foundation's accounting software, monthly time records for the coordinator, and a calendar of the bank foundation's two reports. If both evaluation and objectives scores rose by 4 points each, the resubmission would score about 84, above the prior funding line—though funding lines change each cycle and nothing is assured.
:::

## Writing SMART objectives: a formula

By [date], [number or percentage] of [specific population] will [one measurable action or change], as measured by [instrument or data source], compared with a baseline of [value].

Example: By June 30 of the project year, at least 75 percent of the 160 program completers will correctly identify four of five sample scam messages, as measured by a five-item quiz at exit, compared with an intake baseline measured in the first session.

# Appendix F: Letter of Commitment Template

A letter of commitment should be written on the partner's letterhead, addressed to the applicant or funder as instructed, and signed by someone authorized to commit the partner's resources. Adapt the following structure; do not send identical letters from multiple partners.

1. Opening: Identify the partner organization and the signer's role.
2. Relationship: Describe the history of collaboration with the applicant in one or two sentences.
3. Understanding of the project: State briefly what the project will do.
4. Specific commitments: List exactly what the partner will provide—staff time (hours or percentage), space (hours and value), referrals (number or process), data, or cash match—and for what period.
5. Value: Where relevant, state the dollar value of in-kind contributions and the basis for it.
6. Contact: Provide the name and contact information of the person who will coordinate.
7. Signature: Name, title, date.

Sample commitment paragraph (illustrative): "The Eastside Senior Center will refer eligible members to the program through its monthly newsletter and front-desk sign-up sheet, host four community scam-awareness talks in its main hall at no cost (an in-kind value of $400 based on our standard room rental of $100 per event), and designate our program director as the point of contact."

# Glossary

Allowable cost: A cost that may be charged to a grant under the funder's rules; for federal awards, it must generally be necessary, reasonable, allocable, consistent, and documented.

Assistance Listing: The SAM.gov description of a federal program that provides grants or other assistance.

Baseline: The value of an indicator before a project begins.

Boilerplate: Verified, reusable proposal content such as organizational descriptions.

Compliance matrix: A table mapping every solicitation requirement to a proposal location and owner.

Cost sharing (match): Project costs not paid by the funder, in cash or in-kind.

De minimis rate: Under 2 CFR Part 200, an indirect cost rate of up to 15 percent of modified total direct costs available to entities without a negotiated rate.

Direct costs: Costs identified specifically with a project.

Fiscal sponsor: An established tax-exempt organization that accepts and oversees grant funds for a project lacking its own status.

Indicator: A specific, observable measure of progress toward an objective.

Indirect costs: Shared organizational costs that benefit multiple programs.

In-kind contribution: A non-cash contribution such as donated space, equipment, or volunteer time.

Letter of commitment: A partner letter specifying resources the partner will provide.

Logic model: A visual summary linking inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, and impact.

Modified total direct costs (MTDC): The base for calculating certain indirect cost rates, excluding specified items.

NOFO: Notice of funding opportunity, the federal solicitation document.

Objective: A specific, measurable result to be achieved within the project period.

Outcome: A change in participants or conditions resulting from a project.

Output: A direct, countable product of project activities.

Period of performance: The time during which a grantee may incur costs and carry out work under an award.

Program officer: Funder staff responsible for a grant program and point of contact for applicants.

SAM.gov: The federal System for Award Management, where entities register to receive federal awards.

SBIR / STTR: Federal programs funding research and development by small U.S. businesses; STTR requires a research-institution partner.

Single audit: An audit required of non-federal entities that expend $1,000,000 or more in federal awards in a fiscal year.

SMART objective: An objective that is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound.

Subrecipient: An entity that receives a subaward to carry out part of a federal program.

Unique Entity ID (UEI): The identifier assigned by SAM.gov to entities doing business with the federal government.
