# How to Use This Book

Short-form video clips cut from long podcasts, livestreams, and interviews now fill TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, and X. Behind many of those clips are marketplaces that pay independent editors, usually called clippers, a set rate per thousand verified views. Social media is full of videos promising that anyone can "get paid to clip streamers" from a phone. Some people do earn money this way. Many more spend hours and sometimes money on tools without earning anything.

This workbook takes the subject seriously and examines it carefully. It explains how clipping campaigns actually work, using the published terms of a major marketplace; works through the arithmetic of rates, minimums, fees, and caps; explains copyright, platform originality rules, and advertising disclosure requirements; and shows how to run a small, low-cost test and evaluate the result honestly. It also describes a second path that many editors find steadier: cutting clips from recordings that a business client owns, for an agreed fee rather than for views.

## Who this book is for

The workbook is written for beginners who have seen clipping advertised and want the facts before investing time or money, for creators who want to understand the business behind clips, and for small-business owners and freelancers who are considering clip editing as a service. It assumes no editing experience. It is not a promise of income, a course in viral tricks, or legal or tax advice.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this workbook, you should be able to explain the clip economy and why brands pay for clips; read a campaign brief and identify its rate, minimum, cap, budget, platforms, and rules; calculate how many verified views a clip needs to earn anything and what you keep after fees; describe how payouts, holds, withdrawals, and taxes work; recognize copyright and licensing limits, including the narrow scope of fair use; follow platform originality rules; disclose paid content as the Federal Trade Commission expects; select moments and edit clips that add real value; build a no-cost workflow; avoid scams and account bans; run and evaluate a 14-day test; and decide whether a client-service model is a better fit.

## How each chapter works

Each chapter opens with learning objectives and closes with a summary and review questions. In between you will find an explanation of the relevant rules or evidence, a step-by-step framework, a worked example, a table, and a checklist or worksheet. Worked examples are labeled illustrative: the people and numbers in them are invented to demonstrate method. Case-study boxes describe real, publicly documented sources, such as marketplace terms, agency guidance, news reporting, and practitioner accounts, with numbered citations to the References section. Their findings are their own, not Total Transformation Inc.'s, and they are not predictions of what you will earn.

## A note on dates and change

Marketplace terms, platform policies, and tool prices change frequently. The specific rules quoted in this workbook come from documents current in November 2026, and each is cited so that you can read the latest version. Before you act on any rule, open the source and check it.

# Chapter 1: The Clip Economy

:::objectives
- Explain what clipping is and why brands and creators pay for it.
- Describe the main platforms where clips are distributed.
- Distinguish marketplace clipping from client-service editing.
- Identify the main criticisms of the clipping model.
:::

A clip is a short excerpt, usually 15 to 90 seconds, cut from a longer video and reformatted for vertical, short-form platforms. Clipping is not new; television networks have long cut highlights for news programs. What is new is the scale. Creators and brands now fund campaigns that pay large numbers of independent editors to post clips on their own accounts, and pay them according to the views those clips receive.

## Why brands and creators pay for clips

Long-form content, such as a two-hour podcast or a six-hour livestream, reaches only people who choose to watch it. Short clips reach people who are scrolling and would never have searched for the original. In May 2026, NPR described "a new shadow economy" in which "thousands of clippers are inundating social media platforms with bite-sized clips of podcast interviews, sports games, films and other long-form content" [1]. NPR reported examples of campaigns paying $1 per thousand views for baseball clips, $25 per thousand views for clips about an AI product, and 50 cents per thousand views with a total budget of $70,000 [1].

Analyst Ed Elson told NPR that clips had become the primary way many people encountered his podcast: "clips aren't the promotional material for the content, clips are the content" [1]. For a creator, paying many clippers can be cheaper than buying advertising, because payment is tied to views.

## Where clips are watched

Clips are distributed mainly on YouTube Shorts, TikTok, Instagram Reels, Facebook Reels, and X. The Pew Research Center's 2025 survey of 5,022 U.S. adults found that 84 percent use YouTube, 71 percent use Facebook, 50 percent use Instagram, and 37 percent use TikTok [2]. Usage varies sharply by age, and younger adults are much more likely to use TikTok and Instagram [2]. Each platform has its own rules for originality, monetization, and disclosure, which later chapters explain.

## Two ways to earn from clipping

There are two broad models. In marketplace clipping, you choose campaigns from a marketplace, post clips on your own accounts, and are paid per thousand verified views, per approved post, or from a shared pool, subject to the campaign's rules and budget. In client-service editing, a business or creator hires you to cut clips from recordings they own, for an agreed fee per batch or per month, and usually posts the clips on their own accounts.

| Feature | Marketplace clipping | Client-service editing |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the source footage | Campaign sponsor (or third parties) | Client |
| Where clips are posted | Your accounts | Usually the client's accounts |
| How you are paid | Per 1,000 views, per post, or pool share | Agreed fee per deliverable or period |
| Income predictability | Low; depends on views, approval, and budget | Higher; depends on client relationship |
| Main risks | Zero payout, rejection, account flags | Scope creep, late payment |
| Skills emphasized | Speed, moment selection, platform rules | Communication, quality, reliability |

## Criticisms of the model

The clipping economy has critics. Marketing consultant Lou Paskalis told NPR that "arbitrage players" repackaging content may not satisfy consumers, may not deliver good value to advertisers, and can strip original creators of the ability to monetize their work [1]. NPR also reported that platforms both reward clips with distribution and crack down on accounts that post many duplicates of the same moment, which can look spammy or automated [1]. Anyone entering the field should understand that platforms, sponsors, and rights holders all have interests that can work against an individual clipper.

## Five steps to orient yourself

1. Watch twenty clips in a niche you know and note what makes the better ones work.
2. Identify the original source of each clip and who likely paid for it.
3. Decide which platforms you already use and understand.
4. Decide whether marketplace clipping, client-service editing, or both interest you.
5. Set a firm limit on time and money for your first test: this workbook recommends 14 days and no paid tools.

:::case NPR on the clipping economy
In a May 2026 report, NPR's Bobby Allyn described marketplaces such as Content Rewards and Vyro that let agencies post "clip-for-cash campaigns" [1]. The report profiled a former warehouse and sandwich-shop worker who said he earned $12 one night and about $2,500 two weeks later through affiliate links, and who now runs a network of freelance clippers, as well as a 19-year-old student who said he makes about $4,000 a month [1]. These are self-reported figures in a news story, not audited earnings and not TTI results. The same report quoted critics and noted platform crackdowns on duplicate content [1].

# Chapter 2: How Clipping Marketplaces Work

:::objectives
- Describe the roles of the marketplace, the brand, the clipper, and the payment processor.
- Identify the four common campaign types.
- Read a campaign brief for rate, minimum, cap, budget, and platform rules.
- Explain submission rules such as the 30-minute window and one campaign per post.
:::

A clipping marketplace connects organizations that want clips with creators who will make and post them. This chapter uses the published Creator Terms of Service of Content Rewards, one of the marketplaces named by NPR, as a detailed example [1][3]. Other marketplaces have different terms; always read the terms of any platform you use.

## The parties

Content Rewards' terms describe a "short-video clipping and user-generated-content (UGC) marketplace" [3]. A brand or organization funds a campaign budget. A creator posts a clip and submits its public link. Content Rewards verifies views through the social platforms' interfaces, applies a validation window, and credits the payout to the creator's balance. Whop, a separate company, handles withdrawals and related identity checks [3]. Creators must be at least 18; the terms state that minors are not permitted "under any circumstances, including with parental consent" [3].

## Campaign types

The terms describe four campaign types [3].

1. CPM campaigns pay per 1,000 verified views. The brand may set a minimum a clip must earn before it is paid and a maximum it can earn.
2. Per-post campaigns pay a flat amount per approved clip, sometimes subject to a minimum view requirement.
3. Retainer campaigns pay per cycle when the creator delivers an agreed number of qualifying clips.
4. Pool campaigns divide a fixed amount among eligible creators in proportion to their counted views when the pool ends; any estimate shown before then "is an estimate, not guaranteed earnings" [3].

## Reading a brief

Every campaign brief sets its own rules. The key fields are the rate, the per-clip minimum (sometimes called the floor), the per-clip maximum (the cap), the remaining budget, the eligible platforms, required hashtags or mentions, source files, and any content restrictions. A rate may also differ by country of the viewers [3].

| Brief field | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | Gross pay per 1,000 verified views, or flat amount | Before the creator fee |
| Minimum | Earnings a clip must reach before payment | Below it, you earn nothing for that clip |
| Cap | Maximum one clip can earn | Limits upside from a viral clip |
| Budget | Funds remaining in the campaign | If exhausted, unpaid earnings may never be paid |
| Platforms | Where clips may be posted | Clips on other platforms do not count |
| Source files | Footage you may use | Using other footage can break rights rules |
| Requirements | Hashtags, mentions, overlays, disclosure | Missing items are common rejection reasons |

## Submission rules

Several rules catch beginners. Under the current terms, a post "can be submitted to one Campaign only," unless the marketplace has configured campaigns to share posts, and a rejected post generally cannot be submitted again [3]. A creator "must submit a post within 30 minutes of publishing it"; older posts cannot be submitted [3]. The organization decides whether to approve or reject each submission, and draft approval does not guarantee approval of the published clip [3].

## Fees

Content Rewards charges a 10 percent creator fee on CPM and pool payouts, and 10 percent on per-post and retainer campaigns with budgets under $5,000; per-post and retainer campaigns with budgets of $5,000 or more carry no creator fee [3]. Rates shown on briefs are before the fee [4].

## Six steps to read a campaign

1. Open the brief on the marketplace's official site, not through a link from a stranger.
2. Record the rate, minimum, cap, and remaining budget.
3. List the eligible platforms and required hashtags, mentions, and overlays.
4. Confirm that source files are supplied and note any rights limits.
5. Note the submission window and the one-campaign-per-post rule.
6. Decide whether you can realistically meet the minimum on an eligible platform.

:::case Content Rewards' Creator Terms
Content Rewards' Creator Terms, last updated November 17, 2026, define CPM, per-post, retainer, and pool campaigns; set a 10 percent creator fee on CPM and pool payouts; require submission within 30 minutes of posting; limit each post to one campaign; and state that the organization decides whether to approve each submission [3]. They also state that if a campaign lacks funds, the marketplace "cannot pay unfunded earnings from that Campaign if sufficient funds never become available" [3]. These are one company's terms, not TTI's, and they change. They show that a campaign rate is not a promise of payment.
:::

Priya finds a podcast campaign offering $1.20 per 1,000 views on TikTok and Instagram, with a $4 minimum, a $250 cap, and about $3,000 left in the budget. The brief supplies a folder of approved episodes and requires the show's handle and a disclosure. She notes that she must reach about 3,334 views on one clip to earn anything, that YouTube is not eligible, and that she must submit within 30 minutes of posting.
:::

# Chapter 3: The Money Math

:::objectives
- Calculate gross and net earnings for a clip.
- Determine how many views a clip needs to reach a campaign minimum.
- Calculate the views needed to cover tool costs.
- Estimate an effective hourly return.
:::

The most important skill for a beginning clipper is arithmetic. Advertised rates sound attractive until you account for minimums, caps, fees, rejected clips, and time. This chapter shows how to calculate what a clip can actually earn.

## What rates look like

Content Rewards reported that on November 16, 2026, the median listed rate across 710 live campaigns on its platform was $1.00 per 1,000 views, with half of campaigns listing between $1.00 and $2.00, and a median per-clip minimum of $2.00 [4]. The company's own explanation gives the arithmetic: at $1.00 per 1,000 views, a clip with 10,000 views earns $10 before the fee and $9 after the 10 percent fee [4]. These are listed rates, not average earnings; they say nothing about how many clips are approved or how many views a typical clip receives.

## The basic formulas

Gross earnings equal views divided by 1,000, multiplied by the rate. Net earnings equal gross minus the creator fee. Views needed to reach a minimum equal the minimum divided by the rate, multiplied by 1,000.

| Rate per 1,000 | Minimum | Views to reach minimum | Net at minimum (after 10%) | Views for $24 net |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0.50 | $5.00 | 10,000 | $4.50 | 53,334 |
| $1.00 | $10.00 | 10,000 | $9.00 | 26,667 |
| $1.20 | $4.00 | 3,334 | $3.60 | 22,223 |
| $2.00 | $20.00 | 10,000 | $18.00 | 13,334 |

The last column shows the approved views needed in a month to earn $24 net, roughly the cost of a low-tier monthly editing subscription. A clip that falls short of the minimum earns nothing, even if it received thousands of views.

## The cap

A cap limits what one clip can earn. If a campaign pays $1.00 per 1,000 views with a $250 cap, a clip with 1 million views still earns $250 gross, not $1,000. Content Rewards notes that "the cap matters more as views grow" [4].

## Time and the effective hourly return

Earnings mean little without time. Finding a moment, editing, captioning, adding disclosures, posting, and submitting can take 20 minutes to more than an hour per clip for a beginner. The effective hourly return equals settled net earnings divided by total hours spent, including research and failed clips.

## Beware the survivorship view

Content Rewards reports that more than 3 million creators have signed up, that more than 600,000 have posted, and that it has paid out more than $18 million to creators [4]. Those are the company's own figures, and they cannot be divided to estimate typical earnings, because payouts are likely concentrated among a minority of creators. They do suggest that most people who sign up never post. Stories of high earners are real for some people, but they are not representative.

## Six steps to run the numbers

1. Record the rate, minimum, cap, and fee.
2. Calculate the views needed to reach the minimum.
3. Estimate realistic views for a new account, starting conservatively.
4. Calculate net earnings at that estimate, applying the cap.
5. Estimate time per clip, including research and posting.
6. Calculate the effective hourly return and compare it with your alternatives.

:::case Content Rewards' rate data
In a November 2026 article, Content Rewards reported a median listed rate of $1.00 per 1,000 views across 710 live campaigns, with YouTube campaigns at a median of $1.10 and TikTok, Instagram, and X at $1.00 [4]. It gave a worked example in which a clip earning $1.75 per 1,000 views reached 8,000 views, earned $14.00 gross, paid a $1.40 fee, and returned $12.60 [4]. These are the marketplace's own figures, describing listed rates rather than creator earnings, and they are not TTI's. They show that meaningful income requires large numbers of approved views.
:::

:::example Illustrative: an honest week
Sam spends 9 hours in a week making 8 clips for a campaign paying $1.00 per 1,000 views with a $10 minimum. Two clips are rejected for missing a required hashtag. Of the six approved, four receive between 1,200 and 6,000 views and earn nothing because they fall below the minimum. Two reach 14,000 and 22,000 views, earning $36 gross and $32.40 net. His effective hourly return is about $3.60 before taxes. He decides to focus on fewer, better clips and to study why the two successful ones worked.

# Chapter 4: Getting Paid: Holds, Withdrawals, and Taxes

:::objectives
- Describe the stages between a posted clip and money in your account.
- Explain validation holds and payout reversals.
- Protect your identity and financial information during payout setup.
- Understand basic tax obligations for gig income.
:::

Views do not turn into money instantly. Between a posted clip and a bank deposit are approval, a validation period, settlement, and withdrawal. Understanding these stages prevents frustration and helps you plan.

## From clip to cash

Content Rewards' terms state that CPM payout rounds "normally consist of a 7-day earning period followed by a 3-day hold," so a funded round generally settles about 10 days after it opens [3]. Per-post payouts usually settle quickly for verified organizations and within three business days for others, once any minimum view requirement is met [3]. Pool payouts are allocated when the pool ends [3]. Settled funds are withdrawn through Whop, whose stated timing of about three to five business days, or instant for a fee, is "a payout initiation estimate" that the marketplace does not control [3].

| Stage | What happens | Typical timing (per current terms) |
|---|---|---|
| Submission | You submit the live post link | Within 30 minutes of posting |
| Approval | The brand approves or rejects | Varies; marketplace may review after 7 days pending |
| Earning period | Eligible views counted | 7 days per CPM round |
| Validation hold | Views verified, fraud checks run | About 3 days |
| Settlement | Funds credited to available balance | About 10 days after round opens |
| Withdrawal | Processor sends funds to bank or wallet | About 3–5 business days, or instant for a fee |

## Reversals and fraud checks

An approved payout that has not settled can be reversed if the submission is later rejected or a fraud flag is upheld; once settled, it is not reversed through that process [3]. Content Rewards uses a 0 to 100 "Bot Score" as one input to fraud review, and botting decisions are made by a person [3]. Buying or generating views results in a permanent ban [3].

## Identity and financial safety

Payout processors require identity verification and tax information. Complete these steps yourself, only on the official website or app, and never share identification documents, tax numbers, bank details, passwords, or verification codes with anyone who offers to "help" with setup. Turn on two-factor authentication for your email, social accounts, marketplace account, and payout account.

## Taxes

In the United States, the IRS states that gig economy income must be reported on a tax return even if it is from part-time or side work, even if no information form such as a Form 1099-K is issued, and even if paid in cash, property, or virtual currency [5]. Keep records of income, fees, and expenses such as software and equipment. Self-employment tax may also apply. If you live elsewhere, check your own country's rules, and consult a qualified tax professional for your situation.

## Six steps to set up payouts safely

1. Create accounts only on official domains.
2. Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere.
3. Complete identity and tax steps yourself.
4. Record each payout, fee, and withdrawal in a simple ledger.
5. Keep receipts for business expenses.
6. Set aside a portion of earnings for taxes and seek professional advice.

:::case Content Rewards' payout timing
Content Rewards' Creator Terms describe CPM rounds as a 7-day earning period followed by a 3-day hold, state that a clip's recurring earning window normally ends 30 days after it first meets its minimum or at the end of its tracking window, and assign withdrawals and related identity checks to Whop under Whop's own terms [3]. The terms also allow unsettled payouts to be reversed if a submission is rejected or a fraud flag is upheld [3]. These are one company's published terms, not TTI's. They show that cash flow from clipping is delayed and conditional.
:::

:::example Illustrative: planning cash flow
Elena posts her first approved clip on the 1st of the month. Its first CPM round runs through the 7th, the hold ends around the 10th, and she requests a withdrawal on the 11th. The funds reach her bank on the 15th. She realizes that clipping cannot cover a bill due in the first week of her test and plans accordingly.
:::

# Chapter 5: Rights, Copyright, and Fair Use

:::objectives
- Explain why rights matter in clipping.
- Describe the four fair use factors and why there is no safe clip length.
- Distinguish a campaign license from rights held by third parties.
- Apply a simple rule when rights are unclear.
:::

Clipping means using someone else's video. Whether that use is lawful depends on who owns the material and what permission you have. A campaign brief can grant only the rights its sponsor actually holds.

## Copyright basics

Copyright protects original creative works, including videos, music, and video games. Generally, using a copyrighted work requires permission from the owner, unless an exception such as fair use applies. Many clips contain several layers of rights: the stream or podcast itself, any music played in it, any video game shown, and the likenesses of guests.

## Fair use

The U.S. Copyright Office explains that fair use permits unlicensed use of copyrighted works in certain circumstances, and that courts weigh four factors: the purpose and character of the use, including whether it is commercial and whether it is transformative; the nature of the copyrighted work; the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and the effect of the use on the potential market for the original [6]. The Office emphasizes that fair use is evaluated case by case and that it cannot give legal advice about specific uses [6].

There is no rule that a clip under a certain number of seconds is automatically fair use. Paid clipping is commercial by nature, and a clip of the most memorable moment of a show may be the "heart" of the work. Do not rely on fair use as a business model.

## Campaign licenses

When a brand supplies footage for a campaign, it grants permission to use that footage for that campaign, under the marketplace's terms. That permission cannot extend to rights the brand does not hold, such as a record label's music or a game publisher's footage. Content Rewards' terms make creators responsible for any required rights or releases for identifiable people in their clips and prohibit content that infringes intellectual property [3].

## Game footage

Many popular streams show video games. Rockstar Games' published policy says that Take-Two Interactive generally does not object to fans using its materials "for non-commercial uses," and defines non-commercial to mean "that you don't make money through the game footage you post or use the material as part of a promotion for a product or service" [7]. Paid clipping of game footage therefore falls outside that general tolerance. Other publishers have different policies.

| Layer of rights | Who usually owns it | Covered by a campaign brief? |
|---|---|---|
| The stream or podcast | The creator or production company | Yes, if the sponsor owns it |
| Music played on stream | Record labels and publishers | Usually not |
| Video game footage | Game publisher | Usually not |
| Guest appearances | Guests (likeness and publicity rights) | Depends on agreements |
| Third-party clips shown on stream | Original owners | Usually not |

## Downloading footage

Use only source files supplied or authorized by the campaign. YouTube's Terms of Service restrict downloading content except through features YouTube provides or with permission [8]. Unauthorized download tools can violate platform terms and may expose you to rights claims.

## Six steps to check rights

1. Confirm that the sponsor owns or controls the footage.
2. Use only supplied or authorized source files.
3. Check for music, game footage, and third-party clips in the moment you choose.
4. Check the game publisher's policy if games appear.
5. Avoid relying on fair use for paid clips.
6. Skip any campaign where rights are unclear.

:::case Rockstar Games' fan content policy
Rockstar Games' policy on posting copyrighted material, updated January 2025, states that Take-Two generally does not object to non-commercial fan uses that do not spoil the plot, defines non-commercial as not making money from posted game footage, and reserves the right to have material taken down at any time [7]. It also notes that the policy addresses occasional fan use and does not apply to advertisements [7]. This is a publisher's policy, not TTI's, and it may change. It shows that paid clips containing game footage may require separate permission.
:::

:::example Illustrative: spotting a rights problem
Marcus finds a campaign for a streamer's channel. The moment he likes shows the streamer reacting to a popular song playing in the background while playing a commercial game. He realizes the campaign can license the streamer's commentary but not the song or the game. He chooses a different moment in which the streamer talks directly to the camera with no music or game on screen.
:::

:::checklist Rights check
- Sponsor owns or controls the footage.
- Source file supplied or authorized.
- No unlicensed music in the clip.
- No game footage, or publisher permission confirmed.
- No third-party clips embedded.
- Guests' appearance covered by agreements, or clip avoids them.
- Not relying on fair use.
:::

:::summary
Clips often contain several layers of rights. Fair use is weighed case by case using four factors, and there is no safe clip length; paid clipping is commercial. A campaign brief licenses only what its sponsor owns. Game publishers such as Rockstar generally tolerate only non-commercial fan use. Use supplied files and skip campaigns where rights are unclear.
:::

# Chapter 6: Platform Originality Rules

:::objectives
- Explain why platforms limit reach for unoriginal content.
- Describe Instagram's and YouTube's originality policies.
- Distinguish material edits from superficial changes.
- Keep campaign payouts and platform monetization separate.
:::

Even when a clip is lawful, the platform where it is posted may limit its distribution or monetization if it looks unoriginal. Platforms want to reward people who create or meaningfully transform content, not accounts that repost the same material repeatedly.

## Instagram

Instagram's help center says it encourages original content "to get the best reach and distribution" and distinguishes original, materially edited, and unoriginal content [9]. It states that "simply stitching together clips or adding a watermark does not qualify as materially edited content," and recommends posting content you created or with approved partners, making material edits, or reposting with credit rather than posting unoriginal content [9].

## YouTube

YouTube's channel monetization policies say monetized content should "be your original creation" and that "if you borrow content from someone else, you need to change it significantly to make it your own" [10]. Its reused content policy covers channels that repurpose content "without adding significant original commentary, substantive modifications, or educational or entertainment value," and it applies to the channel as a whole [10]. In July 2025, YouTube renamed its repetitious content policy "inauthentic content" to clarify that it includes repetitive or mass-produced content [10].

## TikTok and others

TikTok's community guidelines address integrity and authenticity, including unoriginal and automated behavior, and its creator rewards program has its own eligibility rules [11]. X and Facebook also have policies on spam and inauthentic behavior. Read the current rules for each platform you use.

## What counts as a material edit

| Superficial change | Material edit |
|---|---|
| Adding a watermark | Selecting a strong moment and cutting dead air |
| Stitching clips together without context | Adding accurate captions and context |
| Changing speed or flipping the image | Adding commentary or explanation that adds value |
| Reposting the same clip on many accounts | Reframing for vertical with deliberate composition |
| Adding a generic music bed | Creating a clear hook and payoff |

## Campaign pay versus platform pay

Campaign payouts and platform monetization programs are separate. A clip may earn a campaign payout and still be ineligible for platform revenue sharing because it is reused content. Never assume you will earn both.

## Six steps to stay within originality rules

1. Read each platform's originality and monetization policies.
2. Make material edits that add clear value.
3. Avoid posting identical clips across many accounts.
4. Use a dedicated, honestly labeled account for campaign clips.
5. Keep campaign earnings separate from platform monetization expectations.
6. Watch for reach drops or warnings and adjust.

:::case YouTube's reused content policy
YouTube's channel monetization policies state that monetized content should be original and authentic, that borrowed content must be changed significantly, and that reused content, meaning repurposed material without significant original commentary, modification, or value, can lead to monetization being removed from an entire channel [10]. The policy explicitly reviews "commentary, clips, compilations, and reaction videos" [10]. This is YouTube's policy, not TTI's. It shows that a clip account built on minimally edited reposts is at risk.
:::

:::example Illustrative: from repost to material edit
Aisha's first clips are straight cuts with a watermark. They get little reach. She changes her approach: she opens each clip with a one-line caption stating the question being answered, removes pauses, adds accurate captions, and ends at the punchline. Her later clips reach more viewers, and she stops worrying that her account looks like a repost farm.
:::

:::checklist Originality review
- Strong moment selected, not a random excerpt.
- Dead air removed.
- Accurate captions added.
- Context or hook added.
- No watermark-only or speed-change tricks.
- Not duplicated across many accounts.
- Account honestly labeled.
:::

:::summary
Platforms limit reach and monetization for unoriginal content. Instagram says stitching clips or adding watermarks is not material editing; YouTube requires borrowed content to be changed significantly and reviews clips under its reused content policy. Make material edits, avoid duplicate posting, and keep campaign pay separate from platform monetization.

# Chapter 7: Disclosure and the FTC

:::objectives
- Explain why paid clips require disclosure.
- Apply the FTC's guidance on where and how to disclose.
- Use plain disclosure language on screen, in captions, and aloud.
- Use platform disclosure tools in addition to your own disclosure.
:::

When you are paid, or expect to be paid, for posting a clip that promotes a creator, product, or brand, viewers have a right to know. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission's Endorsement Guides and related guidance explain how endorsers should disclose material connections with brands.

## Why disclosure is required

The FTC's guide for social media influencers explains that a "material connection" includes a financial relationship, such as a brand paying you, and that endorsements should make such relationships obvious [12]. The guide states that it is the endorser's responsibility to make disclosures and warns: "Don't rely on others to do it for you" [12]. Content Rewards' FTC compliance terms state that all content posted in connection with its campaigns constitutes a material connection requiring disclosure, because the creator receives or expects compensation based on performance [13].

## Where to disclose

The FTC says disclosures should be "hard to miss" and placed with the endorsement itself [12]. Disclosures are likely to be missed if they appear only on a profile page, at the end of a video, or where a viewer must click "more," and they should not be mixed into a group of hashtags [12]. For video, "the disclosure should be in the video and not just in the description," and viewers are more likely to notice disclosures made in both audio and video [12].

Content Rewards' terms add platform-specific expectations for short-form video: an oral disclosure within the first 10 seconds if the creator appears on camera or provides voiceover, a text overlay visible long enough to read, a written disclosure in the caption, and platform-native paid partnership tools where available [13].

## What to say

The FTC notes that terms such as "advertisement," "ad," and "sponsored" are often enough if placed where they are hard to miss, and it advises against vague terms such as "sp," "spon," or "collab" [12]. It also says not to assume a platform's disclosure tool is enough, but to consider using it in addition to your own disclosure [12].

| Location | Recommended disclosure | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| On screen | "Ad · Paid campaign for @Sponsor" near the top, readable | Tiny text, covered by buttons, shown briefly |
| Caption | Start with "Ad |" or "Paid partnership with @Sponsor" | Disclosure after "more" or among hashtags |
| Spoken | "This is a paid promotion for [Sponsor]" early in the clip | Mumbling, or only at the end |
| Platform tool | Turn on paid partnership or commercial content label | Relying on the tool alone |
| Title (YouTube) | "(Ad)" or description first line "Ad — paid campaign" | Disclosure only in a link |

## Truthful claims

The FTC also warns that endorsers cannot talk about experience with a product they have not tried, cannot say a product is terrific if they thought it terrible, and cannot make claims that would require proof the advertiser does not have [12]. Clippers should avoid adding claims to captions that the source does not support.

## Six steps to disclose properly

1. Assume every paid campaign clip requires disclosure.
2. Add a readable on-screen label at the start of the clip.
3. Start the caption with a plain disclosure.
4. Disclose aloud early if you speak in the clip.
5. Turn on the platform's paid partnership tool as well.
6. Check that captions contain no unsupported claims.

:::case The FTC's Disclosures 101 guide
The FTC's staff guide, "Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers," says to disclose any financial relationship with a brand, to place disclosures where they are hard to miss, to include disclosures in the video itself rather than only the description, and to use clear terms such as "ad" or "sponsored" rather than vague abbreviations [12]. It states that U.S. law applies to posts from abroad if it is reasonably foreseeable that they will affect U.S. consumers [12]. This is federal agency guidance, not TTI policy. It applies directly to paid campaign clips.
:::

:::example Illustrative: fixing a hidden disclosure
Leo's first caption reads: "Wait for it 😂 #funny #podcast #viral #ad." The "#ad" is buried among hashtags. He rewrites it: "Ad | Paid partnership with @ShowName — the moment the guest realized he'd been wrong for ten years." He adds "Ad · Paid campaign for @ShowName" in readable text at the top of the frame for the first several seconds and turns on the platform's paid partnership label.
:::

:::checklist Disclosure check
- On-screen label at the start, readable and uncovered.
- Caption begins with a plain disclosure.
- Disclosure spoken early if I speak or narrate.
- Platform paid partnership tool turned on.
- No vague terms such as "sp" or "collab."
- Caption makes no unsupported claims.
:::

:::summary
Paid campaign clips involve a material connection that must be disclosed. The FTC says disclosures must be hard to miss, placed with the endorsement, included in the video itself, and written in plain terms such as "ad." Use on-screen, caption, and spoken disclosures, and turn on platform tools in addition to, not instead of, your own disclosure.
:::

# Chapter 8: The Craft of a Good Clip

:::objectives
- Select moments that stand on their own.
- Structure a clip with a hook, development, and payoff.
- Edit for vertical viewing, captions, and sound-off audiences.
- Review a clip before posting.
:::

Rules and math determine whether a clip can earn. Craft determines whether anyone wants to watch it. A good clip is a complete, small story: it makes a viewer curious, delivers something worth their time, and ends cleanly.

## Selecting moments

Strong moments share several traits. They make sense without the rest of the episode. They contain a clear idea, a surprising statement, a vivid story, a strong emotion, or a useful piece of advice. And they are short enough to deliver in under a minute or so. Gary Vaynerchuk's widely cited advice to "document, don't create" argues that ordinary, honest moments from real work are often more compelling than polished productions [14]. For clippers, this means the best moments are often unscripted.

## Hook, development, payoff

Viewers decide quickly whether to keep watching. A useful structure has three parts: a hook in the first seconds that raises a question or makes a claim; development that delivers the substance; and a payoff that resolves the question or ends on the strongest line. Cut anything that does not serve one of the three.

| Part | Purpose | Techniques |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | Make the viewer curious in the first seconds | Start mid-sentence at the key line; on-screen question; surprising statement |
| Development | Deliver the substance | Remove pauses and filler; keep context needed to understand |
| Payoff | Resolve and end cleanly | End on the punchline or conclusion; avoid trailing chatter |
| Captions | Serve sound-off viewers and clarity | Accurate, readable, synchronized |
| Framing | Fit vertical screens | Center the speaker; keep text in safe zones |

## Editing for vertical screens

Reframe horizontal footage for a 9:16 vertical screen, keeping the speaker's face centered. Place text and disclosures where platform buttons and captions will not cover them. Add accurate, synchronized captions; many viewers watch without sound. Keep visual effects modest; they should support, not distract from, the moment.

## Accuracy and fairness

Do not cut a clip in a way that changes what someone meant. Misleading edits can harm the speaker, damage the sponsor's trust, violate platform rules, and in some cases create legal risk. A good test: would the speaker agree that the clip fairly represents what they said?

## Six steps to make a clip

1. Watch or skim the source and mark three candidate moments.
2. Choose the one that stands alone best and has a clear payoff.
3. Cut to start at the hook and end at the payoff.
4. Reframe for vertical and add accurate captions.
5. Add the required disclosure, handles, and hashtags.
6. Watch the full clip once with sound and once without before posting.

:::case Gary Vaynerchuk's "Document, Don't Create"
In a 2016 essay, Gary Vaynerchuk argued that many creators are paralyzed by trying to make perfect content, and advised them to "document" their real process instead of fabricating an image [14]. He wrote that talking about your process is often "much more fruitful" than offering the advice you think you should give [14]. This is one practitioner's view, not research and not TTI's. It helps explain why authentic, unscripted moments in long-form content often make the strongest clips.
:::

:::example Illustrative: tightening a clip
Nina's first cut of a podcast moment runs 95 seconds. It opens with the host's long question, includes two tangents, and ends with laughter and crosstalk. She restarts at the guest's first striking sentence, removes one tangent, keeps the brief context needed for the story, and ends on the guest's final line. The clip runs 48 seconds and is easier to follow.
:::

:::worksheet Clip plan
Source and timestamp: ___
Why this moment stands alone: ___
Hook (first line or on-screen text): ___
Development (what must stay): ___
Payoff (final line): ___
Captions checked (yes/no): ___
Disclosure placed (yes/no): ___
Fair representation of speaker (yes/no): ___
:::

:::summary
A good clip is a small, complete story. Choose moments that stand alone, structure them with a hook, development, and payoff, reframe for vertical screens, and add accurate captions. Never edit in a way that changes the speaker's meaning. Watch the finished clip with and without sound before posting.
:::

# Chapter 9: Tools and Workflow on a $0 Budget

:::objectives
- Choose free tools adequate for a first test.
- Understand the limits of free and paid AI clipping tools.
- Build a repeatable production workflow.
- Organize files and records.
:::

Many clipping promotions push paid AI tools. These tools can save time for experienced editors, but they are not necessary for a first test, and subscription costs can exceed earnings. This workbook recommends spending nothing on tools until you have evidence that clipping works for you.

## Free options

Most smartphones and computers include basic video editors. Free tiers of popular editors, such as CapCut, provide manual trimming, captions, and vertical formats [15]. Several AI clipping services, such as OpusClip, offer free tiers with limits such as monthly credits or watermarks [16]. Free tiers change often; read the current pricing page before relying on one, and confirm that any watermark is acceptable under the campaign brief.

## What AI tools can and cannot do

AI clipping tools can suggest candidate moments, generate captions, and reframe footage automatically. They cannot judge rights, guarantee originality under platform policies, or ensure accurate disclosure. Their suggestions often need manual trimming. Platforms also warn against mass-produced content; YouTube's policies specifically note that AI-generated content made with generic templates giving "the impression of mass production" is not eligible for monetization [10].

| Need | Free approach | When a paid tool might help |
|---|---|---|
| Trimming | Built-in or free editor | High volume of long sources |
| Captions | Free auto-captions, then manual correction | Many clips per day |
| Vertical reframing | Manual crop | Many speakers moving on screen |
| Moment finding | Watch at higher speed; use transcripts | Very long streams |
| Disclosure overlay | Text tool in free editor | Never necessary |

## A repeatable workflow

A consistent workflow reduces errors. Re-check the brief and budget; download supplied source files; mark moments; edit; caption; add disclosure, handles, and hashtags; export; post on an eligible platform; copy the live link; submit within the window; and log the clip.

## Organizing files and records

Create a folder for each campaign containing the brief, source files, exports, and a simple log. Name files with the campaign, date, and clip number. Keep a spreadsheet of every clip: date, campaign, platform, link, submission time, approval status, views, and net payout.

## Six steps to set up a $0 workflow

1. Choose one free editor and learn trimming, captions, and text.
2. Create campaign folders and a clip log.
3. Write a per-clip checklist and keep it next to your editor.
4. Time yourself on the first five clips.
5. Correct auto-captions manually.
6. Postpone any paid tool until your 14-day test shows a reason to buy.

:::case YouTube on mass-produced AI content
YouTube's channel monetization policies list, among content not allowed to monetize, "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates giving the impression of mass production without adding the creator's original, authentic insights or perspective" [10]. The same policies say monetized content should be original and authentic [10]. This is YouTube's policy, not TTI's. It shows that automating clip production without human judgment can work against a clipper.
:::

:::example Illustrative: timing the workflow
Ken times his first five clips with a free editor. The first takes 70 minutes; the fifth takes 28 minutes, mostly because he has learned his editor's caption tools and written a checklist. He calculates that a paid AI tool might save him 10 minutes per clip, which would not justify its cost at his current output.
:::

:::checklist Per-clip workflow
- Brief and budget re-checked.
- Supplied source file used.
- Moment selected and trimmed.
- Vertical framing and captions done.
- Disclosure, handles, hashtags added.
- Posted on eligible platform.
- Submitted within the window.
- Logged with time spent.
:::

:::summary
A first test needs no paid tools. Free editors provide trimming, captions, and vertical formats, and free AI tiers carry limits. AI tools cannot judge rights, originality, or disclosure, and platforms discourage mass-produced content. A consistent workflow, organized files, and a clip log reduce errors and make evaluation possible.
:::

# Chapter 10: Avoiding Scams and Account Risks

:::objectives
- Recognize common clipping and coaching scams.
- Protect accounts and personal information.
- Avoid fake engagement and the bans it triggers.
- Evaluate paid courses and communities critically.
:::

Any popular money-making idea attracts scams. Clipping is no exception. Some promoters sell expensive coaching with promises of easy income, some ask for fees to join campaigns, and some seek account access or personal information.

## Coaching and business-opportunity scams

The FTC warns that if a business offer or coaching program "promises guaranteed income, large returns, or a 'proven system,' it's likely a scam" [17]. It gives examples of promoter claims such as "You can make 5-6 figures if you follow our system" and "You can be making money now. Guaranteed!" and notes that "no one can guarantee that you'll make money in business" [17]. Even a free or low-cost "system" can lead to expensive upsells [17].

## Fee and access scams

Legitimate marketplaces do not charge creators to join campaigns. Be wary of anyone who asks for an "activation," "whitelist," or "payout release" fee, or who asks for your password, verification codes, identification, or bank login. Sign in only on the official domain, typed directly or saved as a bookmark.

## Fake engagement

Buying views, likes, or followers, or joining engagement pods, violates marketplace and platform rules. Content Rewards' terms state that buying or generating views or engagement results in a permanent ban, and that the company may act on accounts that appear to be related or operated by the same person [3]. Fake engagement also wastes sponsors' money and harms the clipping ecosystem.

| Red flag | Example | Safe response |
|---|---|---|
| Income guarantee | "Make $300 a day guaranteed" | Walk away |
| Fee to join | "Pay $49 to unlock campaigns" | Walk away |
| Account access | "Send me your login so I can set it up" | Never share |
| Income screenshots | Unverified dashboards | Treat as marketing, not evidence |
| Urgency | "Only 3 spots left tonight" | Take time; research |
| Fake engagement offers | "Boost your clip with 50,000 views" | Decline; it can get you banned |

## Evaluating courses

Some courses teach useful skills. Before paying, ask what exactly you will learn, whether the instructor's claims are verifiable, whether refunds are available in writing, and whether the same information is available free. Be skeptical of earnings claims; the FTC notes that promoters often use "made-up numbers" [17].

## Six steps to stay safe

1. Use only official marketplace domains.
2. Never pay to join a campaign.
3. Never share passwords, codes, or identity documents.
4. Never buy views, likes, or followers.
5. Treat income claims and screenshots as marketing.
6. Research any paid course and get refund terms in writing.

:::case The FTC on coaching and business-opportunity scams
The FTC's consumer guidance "When a Business Offer or Coaching Program Is a Scam" warns that promises of guaranteed income, large returns, or a "proven system" are signs of a scam, and describes online business coaching schemes that claim people can make big money with little experience [17]. It states that hundreds of thousands of people have lost thousands of dollars to bogus coaching services [17]. This is federal consumer guidance, not TTI's. It applies directly to the many clipping courses and communities advertised online.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a suspicious offer
After posting a few clips, Tara receives a direct message offering to "add her to a private high-paying campaign" for a $79 onboarding fee and asking for her marketplace login. She checks the marketplace's official site, finds that joining campaigns is free, and blocks the account. She turns on two-factor authentication and changes her password as a precaution.
:::

:::checklist Scam filter
- Offer does not promise guaranteed income.
- No fee to join campaigns.
- No request for passwords, codes, or documents.
- No fake engagement offered or used.
- Official domain confirmed.
- Refund terms in writing for any paid course.
- Earnings claims verified or ignored.
:::

:::summary
Clipping attracts coaching scams, fee scams, and account-takeover attempts. The FTC warns that promises of guaranteed income or a "proven system" signal scams. Never pay to join campaigns, never share credentials, and never buy engagement, which can bring permanent bans. Evaluate courses skeptically and get refund terms in writing.
:::

# Chapter 11: The 14-Day Test

:::objectives
- Design a time-limited, low-cost test of marketplace clipping.
- Track the right measures during the test.
- Calculate results honestly at the end.
- Decide whether to continue, change, or stop.
:::

The safest way to learn whether clipping suits you is a short, structured test. A test limits your risk, produces real data about your own results, and replaces hope with evidence. This workbook recommends 14 days, one or two campaigns, no paid tools, and a fixed number of hours.

## Designing the test

Choose one or two campaigns whose rights are clear, whose budget is healthy, and whose minimum you can plausibly reach. Decide in advance how many hours you will spend, for example one hour a day, and how many clips you will make, for example one a day. Commit to completing the test before judging it; early clips are usually weaker.

## What to track

| Measure | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Clips made and submitted | Measures output |
| Submission on time (within window) | Measures process discipline |
| Clips approved | Measures rule compliance and quality |
| Views per approved clip | Measures audience response |
| Clips reaching the minimum | Measures what actually earns |
| Settled net dollars | Measures real income |
| Hours spent | Measures cost of your time |
| Money spent | Measures direct cost |

## Calculating results

At the end of 14 days, calculate the approval rate, the share of approved clips reaching the minimum, settled net dollars, and the effective hourly return, meaning settled net dollars divided by hours. Remember that some earnings may still be in a hold; count them separately as pending, not as settled.

## Making a decision

Compare your effective hourly return with your alternatives, including other work and the client-service path in Chapter 12. Consider also what you learned: whether your approval rate improved, whether certain moments performed better, and whether you enjoyed the work. Possible decisions include continuing as is, changing campaigns or niches, shifting toward client work, or stopping.

## Six steps to run the test

1. Choose one or two campaigns with clear rights and healthy budgets.
2. Set time, clip, and money limits in writing.
3. Make and log one clip per day.
4. Review rejections immediately and fix the cause.
5. At day 14, calculate settled and pending earnings and hours.
6. Decide to continue, change, or stop, and write down why.

:::case Content Rewards on rejections
Content Rewards' guidance states that rejected clips earn nothing and that "most rejections come from a detail the clipper skipped," and it advises reading the brief twice, adding a clear ad disclosure at the start of each clip, submitting within 30 minutes of posting, and never buying views [4]. The company also says that for most people clipping "starts as a side income" and that pay "changes week to week" [4]. These are the marketplace's own statements, not TTI's. They support tracking approval rates and treating early results as a learning period.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a 14-day result
Dana makes 14 clips in 15 hours. Eleven are approved; three are rejected for missing a required mention, a mistake she fixes on day five. Four approved clips reach the campaign minimum, earning $41 net, with $12 still pending. Her settled effective hourly return is about $2.70. She notices that her best clips were all from interview segments, not gameplay. She decides to try one more 14-day test focused on interviews, while also building a sample reel for client work.
:::

# Chapter 12: The Client-Service Path

:::objectives
- Describe client-service clip editing as an alternative to marketplace clipping.
- Build a sample portfolio from material you own.
- Define a clear service scope and agreement.
- Avoid promises of views or virality.
:::

Many editors find that the steadier opportunity is not chasing views but helping businesses turn recordings they already own, such as webinars, podcasts, interviews, and talks, into short clips for their own accounts. Clients pay for agreed deliverables, not for views, and they own the source footage, which simplifies rights.

## Why businesses need clips

Many small businesses and professionals record long content but lack time to cut it. The NPR report noted that some creators now see clips as the primary way audiences encounter their work [1]. Gary Vaynerchuk's "document, don't create" approach encourages businesses to record their real work regularly [14], which produces raw material for clips. But content alone does not create customers. Justin Welsh, a solo business writer, has said he published content daily for 8.5 months without gaining a single customer until he changed his approach to write for one specific person, share experience rather than generic advice, and end with a clear next step [18]. Clips work best for businesses when they serve a clear audience and purpose.

## Building samples ethically

Do not use someone else's footage in your portfolio without permission. Build samples from recordings you made yourself, from content you have written permission to use, or from public-domain or properly licensed material. A short sample reel of three to five clips is enough to start.

## Defining the service

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Deliverables | 8 vertical clips per month from client-supplied recordings |
| Specifications | 30–60 seconds, captions, client branding, vertical format |
| Revisions | One round of revisions per clip |
| Timeline | Clips delivered within 5 business days of receiving recordings |
| Rights | Client confirms it owns or licenses the footage and music |
| Posting | Client posts, or editor posts with client approval |
| Fee and terms | Fixed monthly fee, paid in advance |
| Results | No guarantee of views, followers, or sales |

## No view guarantees

Do not promise views, followers, leads, or sales. You can control the quality and timeliness of your work, not platform algorithms or audience behavior. State in writing that results are not guaranteed. If a client posts endorsements or paid content, remind them of disclosure obligations.

## Finding first clients

Start with people you know or businesses whose content you already watch. Offer a short, specific proposal: a sample clip from one of their public recordings, created with permission or shared privately as a demonstration, along with a clear description of what you would deliver monthly. Respect a no.

## Six steps to start client work

1. Build three to five sample clips from material you own or are licensed to use.
2. Write a one-page service description with deliverables, specifications, timeline, and fee.
3. Include a rights confirmation and a no-guarantee clause.
4. Identify ten businesses that already record long content.
5. Offer a specific, low-risk first project.
6. Deliver on time, gather feedback, and refine your service.

:::case Justin Welsh on content without customers
In a 2025 LinkedIn post, Justin Welsh wrote that he published content every day for 8.5 months "without getting a single customer," then reviewed his last 50 pieces and concluded that his writing was vague, his stories were surface-level, and his calls to action were unclear [18]. He described three changes: writing for one person, sharing his own experience, and ending with a simple next step [18]. This is one practitioner's self-reported account, not research, and his results are his own, not TTI's. It suggests that businesses buying clips need a clear audience and purpose, not just volume.
:::

:::example Illustrative: a first client

# Glossary

**Cap.** The maximum amount a single clip can earn in a campaign.

**Campaign.** A brand-funded program that pays creators for posting clips under specified rules.

**Clip.** A short excerpt from a longer video, reformatted for short-form platforms.

**Clipper.** An editor who cuts and posts clips, often for marketplace campaigns.

**CPM.** Cost or pay per thousand views; in clipping, the gross rate paid per 1,000 verified views.

**Creator fee.** The percentage a marketplace deducts from a creator's earnings.

**Effective hourly return.** Settled net earnings divided by total hours spent.

**Fair use.** A U.S. legal doctrine permitting some unlicensed uses of copyrighted works, evaluated case by case using four factors.

**Material connection.** A relationship with a brand, such as payment, that could affect how viewers weigh an endorsement and must be disclosed.

**Material edit.** A change that adds substantial value, such as selection, captions, context, or commentary, rather than a superficial alteration.

**Minimum.** The amount a clip must earn before any payment is made.

**Pool campaign.** A campaign in which a fixed amount is divided among eligible creators in proportion to their counted views.

**Reused content.** Under YouTube policy, content repurposed without significant original commentary, modification, or value.

**Settlement.** The point at which a payout moves into a creator's available balance.

**Source file.** Footage supplied or authorized by a campaign sponsor for clipping.

**Validation hold.** A period during which views are verified and fraud checks are run before payment settles.
