# How to Use This Book

Generative AI tools can now draft emails, summarize notes, outline proposals, organize checklists, and prepare reports in seconds. For a small business owner who wears every hat, this is genuinely useful. It also creates a new management problem. Without structure, AI becomes one more source of tabs, drafts, and half-finished ideas, and a source of confident errors that can reach customers under the owner's name.

This playbook treats the owner as the chief executive of a small team that includes AI assistants. A chief executive's job is not to do every task. It is to set direction, assign work clearly, review quality, make final decisions, and remove the constraints that slow the business down. The playbook organizes that job into a weekly rhythm built from single pages: a North Star sheet, a Monday goals sheet, a task handoff sheet, a daily one-page report, an approval charter, a Friday bottleneck review, and a 90-day map.

The methods are grounded in established management research and public guidance: goal-setting research by Edwin Locke and Gary Latham ([Locke & Latham, 2002](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgPsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20(2002).pdf)); Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer's work on progress and small wins ([Harvard Business Review, 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)); Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints ([Goldratt Research Labs](https://www.goldrattresearchlabs.com/introduction-to-toc)); the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle ([Institute for Healthcare Improvement](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement)); the NIST AI Risk Management Framework ([NIST](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)); and security guidance from CISA and the FTC. It also draws on Dan Martell's delegation methods as summarized publicly ([Deven Davis, summary of Buy Back Your Time](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)).

## Who This Book Is For

- Solo owners and founders of small teams who want a calm, repeatable way to run the week.
- Owners who already use AI chat tools and want to use them with more structure and less risk.
- Managers who delegate to a mix of AI tools, contractors, and employees.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Define a one-page North Star that keeps weekly work aligned with your core business.
- Map your business into four desks and decide what AI may prepare at each.
- Set three weekly outcomes with a clear definition of done.
- Write task handoffs that produce usable work from AI or people.
- Run a ten-minute daily one-page report.
- Apply Red, Yellow, and Green approval rules and basic account security.
- Find and relieve your main bottleneck each Friday and plan 90 days ahead.

## How the Book Is Organized

Twelve chapters follow the weekly rhythm, from foundations (role, North Star, desk map) through the Monday, daily, and Friday routines, to safety rules and the 90-day horizon. Each chapter includes objectives, definitions, a framework, a table, a worked illustrative example, a worksheet or checklist, a summary, and review questions. Selected chapters include case studies from public sources. Examples follow Marcus, the hypothetical owner of a residential painting company with four employees; he is invented to illustrate the method. Appendices provide printable one-page templates and a prompt library.

AI tools can produce inaccurate or inappropriate output, and you remain responsible for anything you publish, send, or decide. # Chapter 1: The Owner as AI CEO

:::objectives
- Define the owner's role when AI assistants are part of the team.
- Distinguish preparation work from authority.
- Explain the buyback idea behind delegation.
- Describe the weekly one-page rhythm.
:::

## From Doer to Director

Most small business owners began as skilled practitioners: the painter, the accountant, the designer. As the business grows, the owner's time becomes the scarcest resource. AI assistants offer a new form of help, but help only matters if someone directs it. In this playbook, being the **AI CEO** means four things: setting direction, assigning work clearly, reviewing quality, and keeping final authority over decisions that carry money, legal, safety, or reputational consequences.

## Preparation Versus Authority

The central distinction in this playbook is between **preparation work** and **authority**. Preparation work includes drafting, summarizing, formatting, organizing, researching, and suggesting options. AI tools can often do a useful first pass at preparation. Authority includes approving spending, signing agreements, setting prices, publishing statements, handling customer money, and making commitments. Authority stays with people, because people and businesses are accountable for outcomes. As one tribunal put it in a case about an airline chatbot, "It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot" ([CBC News](http://www.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.7116416)).

## The Buyback Idea

Dan Martell's book Buy Back Your Time argues, in a summary's words, "Don't hire to grow your business, hire to buy back your time," and frames delegation as a loop of audit, transfer, and fill: audit how you spend time, transfer low-value or draining tasks, and fill the freed time with higher-value work ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). The same summary describes his DRIP Matrix, which sorts tasks by how much money they produce and how much energy they give or take. AI assistants extend this idea: some preparation tasks can be transferred to tools, but only if the transfer is clear and the output is checked.

## The Weekly Rhythm

| When | Page | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Once, then quarterly | North Star sheet and four-desk map | 60 minutes |
| Monday | Weekly goals sheet and task handoffs | 20 to 30 minutes |
| Tuesday through Friday | Daily one-page report | 10 minutes |
| As needed | Approval charter | Reference |

:::case Dan Martell's Buyback Principle
Dan Martell, a Canadian entrepreneur who founded and sold software companies including Flowtown and Clarity and later started SaaS Academy, published Buy Back Your Time in 2023. A detailed public summary recounts an example from the book: a founder named Stuart who was working 70-hour weeks audited his time, found he was spending hours worth $100 on $10 tasks, filmed himself doing tasks to hand to two new hires, and freed more than 30 hours a week within two months ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). The book also describes a "pain line" at which founders tend to sell, sabotage, or stall. These are anecdotes reported in a business book rather than controlled research, and individual results vary widely. The value for this playbook is the method: audit before delegating, document tasks so others can do them, and reinvest freed time deliberately. The results described are the author's and his clients', not TTI's.
:::

# Chapter 2: The North Star Sheet

:::objectives
- Write a one-page statement of what your business is focused on.
- Identify your core offer, ideal customer, and controllable activities.
- Set a cash floor and a "not right now" list.
- Use the North Star to filter weekly work and AI requests.
:::

## Why One Page

A **North Star sheet** is a single page that states the business's current focus. It answers what you sell, to whom, how customers find and buy from you, which activities you control, how much cash you protect, and what you are deliberately not doing for the next 90 days. It is the filter for every weekly goal and every task handed to AI. Without it, AI tools will happily help you pursue any idea, including the wrong ones.

## The Seven Fields

| Field | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Core offer | What single product or service produces most revenue now? | Interior repaints for homeowners |
| Ideal customer | Who gets the best result and pays on time? | Homeowners within 20 miles, homes 10 to 40 years old |
| Customer path | How do customers find, buy, and receive the work? | Referrals and search, free estimate, deposit, two- to four-day job |
| Controllable activities | Which weekly actions drive results? | Estimates scheduled, quotes sent within 24 hours, follow-ups, reviews requested |
| Cash floor | What minimum balance do you protect? | One month of payroll and rent |
| Boundaries | What will you never do? | Never quote without seeing the job; never skip prep work |
| Not right now | What will you defer for 90 days? | Commercial bids, a second location, a new app |

## Controllable Activities

Distinguish **outcomes** (revenue, profit, reviews) from **controllable activities** (quotes sent, follow-ups made). Outcomes depend partly on factors you do not control; activities are within your control and predict outcomes over time. Weekly goals and daily reports should emphasize activities while tracking outcomes.

:::example Illustrative: Marcus's North Star
Core offer: interior repaints. Ideal customer: homeowners within 20 miles. Customer path: referral or search, estimate within three days, quote within 24 hours, 30 percent deposit. Controllable activities: estimates booked, quotes sent within 24 hours, two follow-ups per open quote, review requests after every job. Cash floor: one month of payroll and rent. Boundaries: no quotes without a site visit. Not right now: commercial bids and exterior expansion.
:::

# Chapter 3: The Four-Desk Map

:::objectives
- Divide the business into four desks.
- Identify repeatable preparation work at each desk.
- Name the human gate that stays with you at each desk.
- Choose the first task to systematize.
:::

## Four Desks in Every Business

Even a one-person business has distinct kinds of work. This playbook uses four **desks**: the CEO seat (direction and decisions), marketing (attracting attention and inquiries), sales and follow-up (turning inquiries into customers), and operations and money (delivering work, billing, and administration). Mapping work to desks makes it easier to see where AI can help and where it must not act.

## The Desk Map

| Desk | AI preparation work | Human gate (never delegated) |
|---|---|---|
| CEO seat | Turn notes into a Monday plan; draft the daily report; summarize options | Set goals, approve spending, sign agreements, verify facts |
| Marketing | Draft post ideas, captions, FAQs, and flyers from real jobs | Verify facts and photos, ensure permission, publish |
| Sales and follow-up | Draft replies, quote summaries, and follow-up reminders | Confirm prices and dates, send messages, make commitments |
| Operations and money | Format checklists and procedures, draft overdue-invoice notes, organize records | Deliver work, access bank accounts, pay bills, file taxes |

## Choosing the First Task

Pick one desk that is most backed up and one repeatable task there. Good first tasks are frequent, low-risk, and easy to check, such as drafting quote follow-up emails from a standard template. Poor first tasks involve money movement, legal commitments, or facts the AI cannot verify.

:::example Illustrative: Marcus's Desk Map
Marcus finds that sales and follow-up is most backed up: twelve open quotes with no follow-up. His first task is a weekly AI-drafted follow-up email for each open quote, using only facts from the quote, which he reviews and sends himself. Marketing comes next: one post per week drafted from photos of a finished job, published only after he checks the facts and confirms the homeowner gave permission.
:::

# Chapter 4: Monday Weekly Goals

:::objectives
- Apply goal-setting research to weekly planning.
- Reduce a long task list to three weekly outcomes.
- Write a definition of done for each outcome.
- Schedule outcomes into the week before it begins.
:::

## What Research Says About Goals

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham summarized decades of research showing that specific, difficult goals generally lead to higher performance than vague instructions such as "do your best." They identified mechanisms through which goals work, including directing attention, increasing effort, increasing persistence, and prompting the use of relevant strategies, and noted that goals work best with commitment and feedback on progress ([Locke & Latham, 2002](https://goal-lab.psych.umn.edu/orgPsych/2020/readings/5.%20Motivation/Locke%20&%20Latham%20(2002).pdf)). A weekly goals sheet applies these findings: a few specific outcomes, a clear finish line, and a daily check on progress.

## The Rule of Three

A **weekly outcome** is a specific, finished result you commit to by the end of the week. Limit yourself to three, typically one each for sales or leads, delivery or quality, and money or systems. Three outcomes are enough to move the business and few enough to finish alongside routine work.

## From Brain Dump to Three Outcomes

1. **Brain dump.** Write every task and idea on your mind.
2. **Sort.** Place each item into one of four buckets: must finish this week (serves paying customers, open quotes, or cash), quick preparation tasks for AI or helpers, park for later, and human-only administration.
3. **Filter by North Star.** Remove anything on the "not right now" list.
4. **Choose three.** Pick the outcomes with the greatest effect on customers or cash.
5. **Define done.** Write what "finished by Friday at 4 p.m." means for each.
6. **Schedule.** Block time for each on a specific day.

AI can help with the sorting step if you paste your list and the bucket definitions, but you make the final choice.

## Designing the Week Ahead

Martell's Perfect Week concept, as summarized publicly, is a templated weekly calendar that reduces wasted gaps, matches tasks to energy, and limits meetings that run over. The summary quotes him: "When you design your week ahead of time and account for all your time, you'll understand exactly what you must say no to in order to say yes to something that comes up sporadically" ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). Scheduling the three outcomes on Monday is a small-scale version of that idea.

| Outcome slot | Weak version | Strong version with definition of done |
|---|---|---|
| Sales or leads | Follow up on quotes | Send a personal follow-up to all 12 open quotes by Wednesday noon and log responses |
| Delivery or quality | Improve job prep | Finalize a one-page prep checklist and use it on both jobs starting Thursday |
| Money or systems | Get on top of invoices | Send all outstanding invoices and one reminder for each overdue invoice by Tuesday 5 p.m. |

:::example Illustrative: Marcus's Monday
Marcus's brain dump has 23 items, including a new website, a commercial bid, a truck repair, and twelve open quotes. The commercial bid is on his "not right now" list. He chooses three outcomes: follow up on all open quotes by Wednesday noon; finalize a prep checklist by Thursday; send all outstanding invoices by Tuesday. He asks his AI assistant to draft the quote follow-ups using his template and blocks Tuesday morning to review and send them.
:::

:::worksheet Monday Weekly Goals

Outcome 2 (delivery/quality): ___ Done means: ___ Day scheduled: ___

:::summary

# Chapter 5: The Five-Part Task Handoff

:::objectives
- Use a five-part formula to delegate to AI or people.
- Write a definition of done that removes ambiguity.
- Provide real facts so the AI does not guess.
- Use the 1-3-1 rule for problems handed back to you.
:::

## Why Handoffs Fail

Vague instructions produce vague work. "Write some marketing emails" invites generic text, invented details, and claims you cannot support. A **task handoff** is a written instruction that gives the person or tool everything needed to produce usable work. The same discipline applies whether you delegate to an AI assistant, a contractor, or an employee.

## The Five Parts

| Part | What you write | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Plain goal | One sentence describing the output | Draft three follow-up emails for open interior-paint quotes |
| 2. Real facts | Accurate details the work must rely on | Quote amounts, scope, customer first names, our phone number, our policy on scheduling |
| 3. Output format | Exact shape and length | Subject line plus body under 80 words each |
| 4. Guardrails | What must not appear | No discounts, no invented deadlines, no claims about competitors |
| 5. Approval level | Green, Yellow, or Red (Chapter 8) | Yellow: I review and send |

## Definition of Done

A **definition of done** spells out what "finished" means. The Deven Davis summary of Martell's book describes borrowing the concept from Scrum: a simple task is done when it meets a concrete physical description, and larger tasks should specify facts, feelings, and functionality, such as a report submitted by a date, accurate enough to trust, and easy to use ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). For AI handoffs, the definition of done becomes your review checklist.

## Real Facts Prevent Invention

AI language models can produce fluent text that is wrong. When a handoff lacks facts, the model may fill gaps with plausible inventions: a discount, a warranty, a deadline. Always supply the facts, instruct the model to use only those facts, and ask it to mark anything it is unsure of with brackets for you to fill in.

## The 1-3-1 Rule

Delegation also flows upward: people bring problems back to the owner. The same summary describes the 1-3-1 Rule, attributed to Brad Pedersen: define one problem, offer three solutions, and recommend one ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). You can apply this to AI as well: ask it to frame one problem, propose three options, and recommend one with reasons, then you decide.

:::example Illustrative: A Strong Handoff
Marcus writes: "Goal: Draft three follow-up emails for open interior quotes. Facts: [pasted table of customer first name, rooms, quote amount, date sent]; our scheduling lead time is two weeks; phone (555) 010-0100. Format: subject line plus body under 80 words, friendly and plain. Guardrails: no discounts, no urgency claims, no promises about start dates. Mark anything uncertain in brackets. Approval: Yellow, I review and send. Done means: each email references the correct rooms and amount, includes the phone number, and contains no invented details."
:::

:::worksheet Task Handoff Sheet
Task and desk: ___ Assigned to: ___ Due: ___

1. Plain goal: ___
2. Real facts provided: ___
3. Output format and length: ___
4. Guardrails: ___
5. Approval level (Green/Yellow/Red): ___
Definition of done: ___
Review check: facts verified, tone plain, no invented claims. ___
:::

:::summary
A five-part handoff (plain goal, real facts, output format, guardrails, approval level) produces usable work from AI or people. A definition of done turns into the review checklist, real facts reduce invention, and the 1-3-1 rule structures problems handed back to the owner.

# Chapter 6: Desk Prompts That Produce Usable Work

:::objectives
- Turn the handoff formula into reusable prompts.
- Build a small prompt library for each desk.
- Write a human check for every prompt.
- Maintain prompts as the business changes.
:::

## Prompts as Standard Operating Procedures

A **prompt** is the instruction you give an AI tool. A reusable prompt is a standard operating procedure for a repeatable preparation task: it captures the context, facts, format, and guardrails so that you do not reinvent them each week. Store prompts in one document, name them, date them, and attach a **human check**: the specific review you perform before using the output.

## Prompt Structure

Each library prompt should include: role and context ("I run a residential painting company in [city]"), the task, placeholders for real facts, output format, guardrails, an instruction to mark uncertainties, and the human check. Appendix C provides a starter library.

## Starter Prompts by Desk

| Desk | Prompt | Human check |
|---|---|---|
| CEO | Sort my brain dump into four buckets | I choose the three outcomes |
| CEO | Ask me five hard questions before I spend money on [item] | I apply a 48-hour wait to nonessential purchases above my limit |
| Marketing | Draft one post from this finished job description and photos list | Verify facts and customer permission before publishing |
| Sales | Draft follow-ups for these open quotes | Confirm names, amounts, and dates; I send |
| Operations | Turn these notes into a numbered job checklist | Test on a real job before adopting |
| Money | Draft a polite overdue-invoice reminder using these details | Check the amount and invoice number against my accounting system |

## Keeping Prompts Current

Prompts age as prices, policies, and services change. Review the library monthly, update facts, and retire prompts you no longer use. When a prompt's output repeatedly needs heavy editing, rewrite the prompt rather than editing the output each week.

:::example Illustrative: Marcus Improves a Prompt
Marcus's post prompt kept producing phrases like "transform your space with our unmatched quality." He added a guardrail ("no superlatives or claims we cannot prove; describe the actual work done"), a format ("three short sentences plus a question for readers"), and an example of a post he liked. Output improved, and his editing time dropped. He still checks each post for accuracy and confirms that homeowners agreed to photos being shared.
:::

:::checklist Prompt Library Standards
- Each prompt has a name, date, desk, and owner.
- Each includes role, task, fact placeholders, format, and guardrails.
- Each instructs the AI to mark uncertainties in brackets.
- Each has a written human check.
- No prompt includes passwords, full account numbers, or unnecessary personal information.
- Library reviewed monthly.
:::

:::summary
Reusable prompts act as standard operating procedures for preparation tasks. Each should contain role, task, fact placeholders, format, guardrails, an uncertainty instruction, and a human check. Maintain the library monthly and rewrite prompts whose output needs repeated heavy editing.

# Chapter 7: The Daily One-Page Report

:::objectives
- Explain why visible daily progress matters.
- Design a ten-minute daily report.
- Track leads, quotes, deliveries, and cash.
- Use AI to prepare, not judge, the report.
:::

## The Progress Principle

Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer analyzed knowledge workers' diaries and found that nothing contributed more to a positive inner work life, the mix of emotions, motivations, and perceptions critical to performance, than making progress in meaningful work; even small wins helped, while setbacks had strongly negative effects ([Harvard Business Review, 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)). A daily one-page report makes progress visible and catches setbacks early.

## What the Report Contains

The **daily one-page report** is a short review completed before opening email or social media. It covers:

| Section | Questions |
|---|---|
| Leads | How many new inquiries? Has each received a response? |
| Quotes | Which quotes are open? Which need follow-up today? |
| Deliveries | What work is scheduled today? Any risks to quality or timing? |
| Cash | What came in? What is overdue? Are we above the cash floor? |
| Weekly outcomes | Progress on each of the three outcomes |
| Today's one thing | The single most important action today |
| Blocked | Anything stuck that needs a decision |

## Using AI to Prepare the Report

AI can assemble a draft report from information you paste in, such as a list of inquiries, a quote tracker export, and the day's schedule. It can format and summarize. It should not be the source of truth for cash: check balances directly in your bank and accounting system, and do not paste full account numbers or credentials into AI tools.

## The Ten-Minute Routine

1. Open the lead and quote tracker (two minutes).
2. Check today's schedule (two minutes).
3. Check bank balance and overdue invoices (two minutes).
4. Update progress on weekly outcomes (two minutes).
5. Choose today's one thing and note blockers (two minutes).

:::example Illustrative: Marcus's Tuesday Report
Leads: two new inquiries, both answered. Quotes: nine open, three due for follow-up today. Deliveries: one job day two of three, on schedule; second crew starts Thursday. Cash: one deposit received; two invoices overdue; above floor. Outcomes: invoices sent (outcome 3 done), quote follow-ups drafted and reviewed (sending today), checklist half written. One thing: send quote follow-ups by noon. Blocked: paint supplier delay for Thursday job; need to call by 9 a.m.
:::

# Chapter 8: Red, Yellow, and Green Approval Rules

:::objectives
- Classify AI-assisted work into three approval levels.
- Write an approval charter for your business.
- Define human red lines that are never delegated.
- Recognize accountability for AI output.
:::

## Three Approval Levels

An **approval charter** is a one-page rule set describing who may approve what. For AI-assisted work, use three levels:

| Level | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Green | AI output may be used internally without review | Brainstorms, internal outlines, formatting your own notes, first-draft checklists for testing |
| Yellow | Human reviews and edits before anything leaves the business | Customer emails, social posts, quotes, job descriptions, invoices, website text |
| Red | Human-only; AI may not perform or decide | Moving money, signing contracts, setting prices, legal or tax filings, hiring and firing decisions, responding to legal threats, access to bank accounts |

## Human Red Lines

**Red lines** are decisions and actions that never pass to AI regardless of convenience. Write them down. Common red lines include approving any payment, sharing passwords, committing to dates or prices, making statements about safety or legal matters, and making employment decisions. Red-line items can still use AI for preparation (for example, summarizing a contract for your questions to an attorney), but the decision and action are human.

## Accountability for AI Output

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework places "Govern" at the center of AI risk management, emphasizing policies, accountability, and a culture of risk awareness ([NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)). An approval charter is a small-business version of that governance. Two public cases illustrate why.

:::case Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024)
A customer relied on Air Canada's website chatbot, which told him he could request a bereavement fare refund after travel, contrary to the airline's policy page. The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal held the airline responsible, stating that it "did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate," and awarded about $812 (Canadian) in damages, interest, and fees ([American Bar Association, Business Law Today](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/)). For an owner, the lesson is that customer-facing AI output belongs in the Yellow category at minimum: you are accountable for it. The outcome belongs to the parties, not TTI.
:::

:::case FTC Action Against DoNotPay (2024)
In its September 2024 Operation AI Comply sweep, the FTC announced an action against DoNotPay, which had marketed "the world's first robot lawyer." The FTC alleged the company did not test whether its chatbot's output matched a human lawyer's and did not retain attorneys. DoNotPay agreed to a proposed order requiring a $193,000 payment and barring claims that its service could substitute for a professional without evidence ([FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes)). FTC Chair Lina Khan said "there is no AI exemption from the laws on the books." For owners, the lesson applies both to marketing claims about your own use of AI and to professional advice: keep legal, tax, and similar advice in the Red category. The outcome belongs to the parties, not TTI.
:::

Green: brainstorming post ideas, outlining checklists, summarizing his own notes. Yellow: quote follow-ups, social posts, invoice reminders, website changes; he reviews every one. Red: pricing, discounts, deposits and refunds, banking, contracts, hiring decisions, responding to complaints that mention damage or lawyers. He prints the charter and tapes it beside his desk.

# Chapter 9: Account Security and Data Handling

:::objectives
- Turn on multifactor authentication for critical accounts.
- Decide what information may be shared with AI tools.
- Apply the FTC's data protection principles.
- Prepare a simple incident plan.
:::

## Why Security Belongs in a CEO Playbook

As AI tools connect to email, calendars, documents, and accounts, the cost of a compromised login rises. Security is an owner-level responsibility because it protects cash, customer trust, and the ability to operate.

## Multifactor Authentication

**Multifactor authentication (MFA)** requires two or more forms of verification before granting access: something you know (a password or PIN), something you have (an authentication app or a code on your phone), or something you are (a fingerprint or face scan). CISA explains that even if an attacker compromises one factor, such as a password, they will be unable to meet the second requirement, and that users who enable MFA are significantly less likely to be hacked ([CISA, More than a Password](https://www.cisa.gov/MFA)). Turn on MFA first for email, banking, accounting, payroll, domain and website hosting, social media, and every AI tool connected to business data.

## What to Share With AI Tools

| Category | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Public business facts | Generally fine to share |
| Your own drafts and notes | Fine, subject to the tool's data settings |
| Customer names and job details | Share only what the task needs; check the tool's data use and retention settings |
| Payment card numbers, bank account numbers, Social Security numbers | Do not share |
| Passwords and MFA codes | Never share |
| Health, legal, or other sensitive personal information | Avoid unless you have confirmed the tool and use are appropriate |

## FTC Principles

The FTC's guide to protecting personal information recommends five principles: Take Stock (know what personal information you have), Scale Down (keep only what you need), Lock It (protect what you keep), Pitch It (properly dispose of what you no longer need), and Plan Ahead (create a plan to respond to security incidents) ([FTC, Protecting Personal Information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/protecting-personal-information-guide-business)). Apply them to AI tools: know which tools hold business data, limit what you upload, secure accounts with MFA, delete old uploads and chats you no longer need where the tool allows, and know what you will do if an account is compromised.

## A One-Page Incident Plan

Write down: how to reset passwords and revoke access for each critical account; whom to call at your bank; how to contact your IT help, if any; how to notify affected customers if required; and when to seek legal advice.

:::example Illustrative: Marcus's Security Hour
Marcus spends one hour turning on MFA for email, bank, accounting, website hosting, and his AI tool, using an authentication app instead of text messages where available. He reviews the AI tool's data settings, removes an old upload containing a customer spreadsheet he no longer needs, and writes a one-page incident plan with his bank's fraud number at the top.
:::

:::checklist Security Basics
- MFA on email, banking, accounting, payroll, website, social media, and AI tools.
- Unique passwords stored in a password manager.
- AI tool data settings reviewed; unnecessary uploads deleted.
- No card numbers, account numbers, Social Security numbers, or passwords in AI tools.
- Former employees and contractors removed from all accounts.
- One-page incident plan written and stored offline.
:::

:::summary
Security is an owner responsibility. Turn on MFA for every critical account, as CISA recommends, limit what you share with AI tools, apply the FTC's Take Stock, Scale Down, Lock It, Pitch It, and Plan Ahead principles, and keep a one-page incident plan.
:::

# Chapter 10: The Friday Bottleneck Review

:::objectives
- Explain the Theory of Constraints in plain terms.
- Identify the week's main bottleneck.
- Apply the five focusing steps to a small business.
- Separate real constraints from shiny objects.
:::

## The Weakest Link

Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints holds that, like the weakest link in a chain, the performance of any organization is limited by its system constraint or bottleneck ([Goldratt Research Labs](https://www.goldrattresearchlabs.com/introduction-to-toc)). A **bottleneck** is the step that limits how much the business can produce or sell. Improving anything other than the bottleneck rarely improves results.

## The Five Focusing Steps

| Step | Meaning ([Goldratt Research Labs](https://www.goldrattresearchlabs.com/introduction-to-toc)) | Small-business application |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identify | Find what limits the organization | Where did work pile up or stall this week? |
| 2. Exploit | Do not waste the constraint | Protect the bottleneck's time; remove interruptions |
| 3. Subordinate | Align other rules to support the constraint | Schedule other work around the bottleneck |
| 4. Elevate | Add capacity when exploiting is not enough | Hire, train, buy equipment, or delegate |
| 5. Go back to step 1 | Do not let inertia become the constraint | Review again next Friday |

## Common Small-Business Bottlenecks

- The owner's time for estimates or approvals.
- Slow follow-up on quotes.
- A single skilled employee everyone waits for.
- Cash collection.
- Lead flow (not enough inquiries).

## Shiny Objects Versus Constraints

A **shiny object** is an appealing project that does not address the current bottleneck, such as a website redesign when the real constraint is unanswered quotes. Before starting any new project, ask: does this relieve the bottleneck? If not, park it.

## The Fifteen-Minute Review

1. Review the five daily reports and the three weekly outcomes.
2. Ask where work piled up or waited.
3. Name the bottleneck in one sentence.
4. Choose one exploit or subordinate action for next week.
5. Note whether elevation is needed later.
6. Do a weekly cash check against the floor.

:::example Illustrative: Marcus Finds His Bottleneck
Marcus notices that quotes waited an average of three days for him to write them after estimates. The bottleneck is his evening quote-writing time. Exploit: he blocks 7 to 8 a.m. on estimate days for quotes and uses an AI-assisted quote template he reviews. Subordinate: he stops scheduling estimates late in the day. Elevate later: train his lead painter to do smaller estimates. A new website idea goes on the parked list.
:::

Bottleneck (one sentence): ___
Exploit action: ___
Subordinate action: ___
Elevate later? ___
Shiny objects parked: ___

:::

:::summary
The Theory of Constraints says a system's output is limited by its bottleneck. Each Friday, identify the bottleneck, choose actions to exploit it and subordinate other work to it, elevate when necessary, and park shiny objects that do not address the constraint.

# Chapter 11: The 90-Day Map

:::objectives
- Translate the North Star into 90-day milestones.
- Use Plan-Do-Study-Act to test changes.
- Score options by impact, confidence, and ease.
- Place important commitments first.
:::

## Why 90 Days

Ninety days is long enough to build a meaningful system and short enough to stay concrete. The **90-day map** lists a few milestones, one improvement per month, and the routines that continue every week.

## Plan-Do-Study-Act

The Institute for Healthcare Improvement's Model for Improvement combines three questions, including "What are we trying to accomplish?", with the Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle to test and adapt changes ([IHI Model for Improvement](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement)). In the 90-day map, each improvement is a test: plan the change, do it on a small scale, study the results, and act by adopting, adapting, or abandoning it.

## Choosing Improvements

Martell's Preloaded Year, as summarized publicly, scores tactics with ICE: impact, confidence, and ease, each rated 1 to 10, then places the highest-scoring "big rocks" on the calendar first ([Deven Davis summary](https://www.devendavis.com/book-summaries/buy-back-your-time)). Use the same scoring for 90-day improvements.

| Option | Impact | Confidence | Ease | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quote template with AI-assisted draft | 8 | 8 | 7 | 23 |
| Train lead painter on small estimates | 8 | 6 | 5 | 19 |
| New website | 5 | 4 | 3 | 12 |

## One Build at a Time

Keep routine customer work, follow-up, and billing steady every week, and add no more than one new system or improvement at a time. This protects delivery while the business improves.

:::example Illustrative: Marcus's 90 Days
Month 1: quote template and 24-hour quote rule (PDSA: measure quote turnaround weekly). Month 2: review-request routine after every job. Month 3: begin training the lead painter on small estimates. Routines: Monday goals, daily report, Friday review. Big rocks placed first: two family events and a week of vacation in month three.
:::

# Appendix H: Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need special AI software to use this playbook?** No. The pages work on paper or in any document tool. A general-purpose AI chat tool is enough for the prompts in Appendix C. If you use tools that connect to email, calendars, or files, review their data settings and turn on multifactor authentication first.

**How much time does the system take?** The design target is about 20 to 30 minutes on Monday, 10 minutes per day, and 15 to 20 minutes on Friday, plus an hour each quarter. Actual time varies by business. The point is to replace scattered checking with short, deliberate reviews, not to add a large new burden.

**What if my week is consumed by emergencies?** Keep the daily report even on hard days, because it shows what slipped. If emergencies are frequent, they may be the bottleneck; use the Friday review to find the cause, such as unclear procedures, missing equipment, or a single person everyone depends on.

**Can AI choose my weekly goals for me?** AI can sort and suggest, but goals are a CEO decision. Research on goal setting emphasizes commitment, and commitment is stronger when you choose the goals yourself.

**Should employees use the same pages?** Many teams adapt the daily report and handoff sheet for employees. Keep the approval charter consistent for everyone, and make clear who may approve Yellow items.

**How do I know if AI output is accurate?** Compare it against your real facts: quotes, prices, policies, and records. Instruct the AI to mark uncertainties in brackets and to use only the facts you provide. Anything that leaves the business must be reviewed by a person.

**What if I fall off the routine?** Restart with the Monday sheet the following week. A missed week is information, not failure; note in the next Friday review what caused the lapse and adjust the timing or length of the routine.

# Glossary

**AI CEO:** The owner's role of setting direction, assigning work, reviewing quality, and keeping authority when AI assistants are part of the team.

**Approval charter:** A one-page rule set defining Green, Yellow, and Red approval levels.

**Bottleneck:** The step that limits how much a system can produce or sell.

**Controllable activity:** A weekly action within the owner's control that predicts outcomes.

**Daily one-page report:** A ten-minute review of leads, quotes, deliveries, cash, and progress.

**Definition of done:** A clear statement of what "finished" means for a task.

**Desk:** A category of work: CEO seat, marketing, sales and follow-up, or operations and money.

**Five focusing steps:** Identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, and repeat, from the Theory of Constraints.

**ICE scoring:** Rating options by impact, confidence, and ease.

**Multifactor authentication (MFA):** Verification requiring two or more types of factors.

**North Star sheet:** A one-page statement of current business focus.

**PDSA:** Plan-Do-Study-Act, a cycle for testing changes.

**Preparation work:** Drafting, summarizing, formatting, and organizing tasks that support decisions.

**Prompt:** An instruction given to an AI tool.

**Red line:** A decision or action never delegated to AI.

**Shiny object:** An appealing project that does not address the current bottleneck.

**Task handoff:** A written instruction with goal, facts, format, guardrails, and approval level.

**Weekly outcome:** A specific, finished result committed to by the end of the week.
