# How to Use This Book

This book is written for owners, managers, and senior staff of small and mid-sized businesses who must decide which artificial intelligence (AI) tools to adopt, how to test them, and how to keep them under control once they are in use. It assumes no technical background. It does assume that you are responsible for real customers, real money, and real employees, and that a careless decision about software can harm all three.

The approach is the one a good business-school course would take. Each chapter begins with learning objectives, defines its terms in plain language, presents a step-by-step framework, and then applies that framework in a worked example with numbers you can check. Where a real company has publicly documented an experience that illuminates the chapter, a case study box summarizes what was reported and cites the source. Each chapter closes with a summary, a checklist or worksheet, and review questions that a reader, a study group, or a management team can use to test understanding.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of the book you should be able to:

- Describe the main classes of AI tools available in 2026 and the kinds of work each is suited to.
- Choose a first use case using an explicit, defensible method rather than enthusiasm or vendor pressure.
- Build a fair test that compares tools on your own tasks, with a scoring rubric and a pass threshold set in advance.
- Set data boundaries that protect customers, employees, and your firm's confidential information.
- Write clear instructions for AI systems and design a review step that catches errors before they reach a customer.
- Estimate the full cost of a tool, including the hidden costs of review, training, and maintenance.
- Run a time-limited pilot with a human in control, and decide on evidence whether to expand, revise, or stop.
- Write a short AI use policy and a monitoring routine that a small team can actually follow.

## How the chapters are built

The chapters follow the order in which a sensible adoption unfolds: understanding the tools (Chapters 1–3), testing them (Chapters 4–6), piloting and costing them (Chapters 7–8), using them with customers (Chapter 9), bringing staff along (Chapter 10), governing them (Chapter 11), and putting it all into a ninety-day plan (Chapter 12). The appendices contain templates you may copy for use inside your own business: a use policy, a vendor questionnaire, a test-set worksheet, a prompt library, an incident log, and a cost worksheet.

The worked examples use invented businesses with invented numbers. They are labelled "illustrative" and are teaching devices, not predictions. The case studies describe publicly reported facts about other companies; their results are their own, not TTI's, and nothing in this book suggests you will obtain similar results.

:::callout A note on product names
This book deliberately avoids recommending specific products or quoting prices. AI products change monthly, and a textbook that ranks vendors is out of date before it is printed. Instead you will learn a method for comparing whatever tools are available when you read this.
:::

# Chapter 1: What AI Tools Actually Do

:::objectives
- Define artificial intelligence, machine learning, and generative AI in operational terms.
- Distinguish five classes of AI tools commonly sold to small businesses.
- Explain why AI output is probabilistic and what that implies for review.
- Identify the kinds of tasks for which AI is a poor fit.
:::

Most confusion about AI in business comes from vocabulary. Vendors use the same words to describe very different products, and managers who cannot tell them apart end up comparing a spreadsheet macro with a language model as if they were the same thing. This chapter establishes a working vocabulary that the rest of the book relies on.

## Definitions that matter for decisions

**Artificial intelligence (AI)** is a broad label for computer systems that perform tasks we usually associate with human judgment: recognizing speech, classifying documents, predicting a number, or producing text. For management purposes, the label is less useful than the question "what does this particular system take in, and what does it put out?"

**Machine learning** is the dominant technique behind modern AI. Instead of following rules written by a programmer, a machine-learning system is trained on many examples and learns statistical patterns. A spam filter that learns from millions of labelled emails is a machine-learning system.

**Generative AI** refers to machine-learning systems that produce new content, such as text, images, audio, or code, in response to an instruction called a **prompt**. The most familiar examples are **large language models (LLMs)**, which are trained on very large amounts of text and generate fluent written responses.

**Automation** is not the same thing as AI. A rule that says "when an invoice is paid, move the customer to the 'active' list" is automation. It is deterministic: the same input always yields the same output. Many products sold as "AI" are mostly automation with a small generative component.

**An AI agent** is a system that not only produces content but also takes actions, such as sending an email, updating a record, or placing an order, often over several steps. Agents raise the stakes because an error becomes an action, not just a draft.

## Five classes of tools

| Class | Typical input | Typical output | Example small-business task |
|---|---|---|---|
| Writing and reasoning assistants (LLMs) | Instructions, documents | Drafts, summaries, answers | Drafting a reply to a product question |
| Speech and transcription tools | Audio | Text transcripts, notes | Turning a recorded sales call into notes |
| Image and design generators | Text description, images | Images, layouts | Producing a first-draft social graphic |
| Retrieval and knowledge tools | Question plus your documents | Answer with cited passages | Finding the warranty clause in a manual |
| Workflow automation with AI steps | Events in your software | Actions, records, messages | Routing an inbound form to the right person |

The classes overlap, and many products combine two or three. The table is useful because each class has a characteristic failure. Writing assistants can state false things fluently. Transcription tools mishear names and numbers. Image generators can produce misleading or infringing content. Retrieval tools can cite the wrong passage. Workflow automations can act on a misclassified input at scale.

## Why AI output must be reviewed

A language model does not look up a fact the way a database does. It predicts likely sequences of words based on patterns in its training data and the material you give it. Most of the time the most likely continuation is also correct. Sometimes it is not, and the model produces a plausible but false statement, often called a **hallucination** or, more precisely, a **confabulation**. Because the output is fluent, errors are harder to spot than a typo.

The practical consequence is simple: AI output is a draft until a responsible person has checked it, unless you have measured its error rate on your task and decided the risk is acceptable. This principle recurs throughout the book.

:::case The chatbot that made a promise
In February 2024 the British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal decided *Moffatt v. Air Canada*. A customer had asked the airline's website chatbot about bereavement fares and was told he could apply for the discount after travel. The airline's policy page said otherwise. The airline argued that the chatbot was responsible for its own statements; the tribunal called that "a remarkable submission" and held the airline liable for negligent misrepresentation, ordering it to pay $812.02 in damages, interest, and fees [1][2]. The amount was small; the principle is not. A business is answerable for what its AI tells customers.
:::

## Where AI is a poor fit

AI tools are typically weak where a task requires guaranteed accuracy with no review, where the correct answer depends on facts the system cannot see, where the decision has legal significance for a person (credit, employment, housing, insurance), or where the volume is so low that setting up and checking the tool takes longer than doing the work by hand. In such cases a template, a checklist, or a simple rule may be the better tool.

:::example Illustrative: classifying a task before buying anything
Rivera Bicycle Repair (a fictional shop) receives about 40 emails a week. The owner sorts them into four groups. About 22 are questions about hours, prices, or turnaround times; 10 are repair status checks; 5 are warranty disputes; 3 are spam. Status checks are answered from the shop's job system, so a workflow automation (no generative AI) can handle them. Hours and price questions suit a writing assistant drafting from an approved fact sheet, with the owner approving each draft at first. Warranty disputes are low volume and high stakes, so they stay fully human. Spam is already filtered by the email provider. Only about 22 of 40 messages, or 55 percent, are candidates for generative AI at all.
:::

:::checklist Tool-class identification
- I can state, for each candidate tool, what goes in and what comes out.
- I know whether the tool only drafts, or also takes actions.
- I have identified the characteristic failure of each tool class I am considering.
- I have listed tasks where a template or rule would do the job without AI.
- I have marked any task that affects a person's legal rights as human-only.
:::

:::summary
AI is a family of techniques, not a single product. Generative tools produce drafts that are usually fluent and sometimes wrong, so review is a design requirement rather than an afterthought. Classifying tools by input, output, and characteristic failure lets you compare like with like and keeps simple automation and templates in the running.
:::

2. Why does fluency make errors from a language model harder to detect?
3. What legal principle did the *Moffatt v. Air Canada* decision illustrate?
4. Name two tasks in your business for which AI would be a poor fit, and explain why.

# Chapter 2: Start With the Work, Not the Tool

:::objectives
- Conduct a time and task inventory using a structured method.
- Calculate an hourly value for your time and use it to rank candidate tasks.
- Score tasks for frequency, effort, risk, and measurability.
- Select one first use case and write a one-paragraph task definition.
:::

The most common mistake in AI adoption is to begin with a product demonstration. The demonstration is impressive; the team buys a subscription; six weeks later nobody uses it, because nobody decided what job it was supposed to do. The disciplined alternative is to begin with an inventory of the work itself.

## Borrowing a method from time management

Dan Martell, a software entrepreneur and coach, popularized a simple method in his 2023 book *Buy Back Your Time* [3]. He recommends logging what you do in fifteen-minute increments for one to two weeks, marking each activity by how much value it creates (one to four dollar signs) and whether it drains or energizes you, and then handing off the low-value, draining items first [4]. He also proposes a **Buyback Rate**: your annual income divided by 2,000 working hours, divided by four. Tasks that someone (or something) could do for less than that rate are candidates to delegate [4][5].

Martell designed the method for hiring people, but it transfers directly to software. An AI tool is another way of buying back time, and the same discipline applies: identify specific, recurring, low-value work, then decide how to hand it off.

:::case Dan Martell's Buyback Rate and Camcorder Method
Martell describes calculating a Buyback Rate (for example, $80,000 a year ÷ 2,000 hours = $40 per hour; ÷ 4 = $10 per hour) and then recording yourself doing a task, narrating as you go, so that someone else can turn the recording into a written playbook [4][5]. His published materials describe these as tools from his coaching practice; any outcomes he reports are his and his clients', not TTI's. Chapter 10 returns to the recording method as a way to create the written instructions AI tools need.
:::

## The four-factor task score

Once you have a list of recurring tasks, score each from 1 (low) to 5 (high) on four factors:

1. **Frequency**: how often the task recurs. Daily tasks score 5; quarterly tasks score 1.
2. **Effort**: total time per month. Tasks that take more than ten hours a month score 5.
3. **Risk** (scored in reverse): 5 means an error is easily caught and cheap to fix; 1 means an error could harm a customer, breach a law, or cost real money.
4. **Measurability**: 5 means you can tell clearly whether the output is right; 1 means quality is subjective or only visible months later.

Multiply the four scores. The product ranges from 1 to 625. The multiplication is deliberate: a task that scores 1 on any factor drops sharply, which is what you want. A frequent, time-consuming task that is also dangerous should not be your first experiment.

## Writing the task definition

For the top-ranked task, write a definition with six parts: the trigger (what starts the task), the inputs, the desired output, the reviewer, the "never" list (outcomes that must not happen), and the measure of success. If you cannot complete all six, the task is not yet ready for any tool.

:::example Illustrative: ranking five tasks at a small accounting practice
A two-person bookkeeping firm logs its time for two weeks and identifies five recurring tasks.

| Task | Freq. | Effort | Risk (rev.) | Measurable | Product |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Summarize client meeting notes | 4 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 192 |
| Draft month-end reminder emails | 4 | 2 | 5 | 5 | 200 |
| Categorize bank transactions | 5 | 5 | 2 | 4 | 200 |
| Answer tax-law questions | 2 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 8 |
| Draft blog posts | 2 | 3 | 4 | 2 | 48 |

Reminder emails and transaction categorization tie at 200. The firm chooses reminder emails as its first experiment because an error there is cheaper (risk score 5 versus 2). Transaction categorization goes on the list for a later, more carefully controlled test. Tax-law questions are excluded: they score 8 because errors could cause real harm and are hard to measure.

The owner's take-home pay is $90,000. Her hourly value is $90,000 ÷ 2,000 = $45, and her Buyback Rate is $45 ÷ 4 = $11.25. Reminder emails take her about 6 hours a month, worth about $270 of her time at $45 an hour. Any tool, including review time, that costs much less than that is worth testing.
:::

## Common traps

- **The shiny-demo trap.** A vendor shows a spectacular result on a task you rarely do. Return to your inventory.
- **The everything trap.** The team tries to apply AI to ten tasks at once and cannot tell what worked. Start with one.
- **The invisible-quality trap.** The chosen task has no clear right answer, so nobody can tell whether the tool helps. Choose measurable work first.

:::worksheet Task definition for your first use case
Task name: ___
Trigger (what starts it): ___
Inputs (documents, data, messages): ___
Desired output (format, length, tone): ___
Reviewer (name or role): ___
Never list (what must never happen): ___
___
Success measure (number, threshold, time frame): ___
Four-factor score (F × E × R × M): ___
:::

:::summary
Begin with a time inventory, not a product. Score recurring tasks on frequency, effort, reverse risk, and measurability, multiply the scores, and pick the top task with low risk. Write a six-part task definition before evaluating any tool. Martell's Buyback Rate offers a simple way to put a dollar value on the time you hope to recover.
:::

# Chapter 3: Comparing Tool Classes, Not Hype

:::objectives
- Compare general-purpose assistants, embedded features, and specialist tools.
- Apply a build, buy, or configure decision framework.
- Identify the questions that separate substantive claims from marketing language.
- Produce a short list of no more than three candidate tools.
:::

With a defined task in hand, you can now ask which kind of tool fits it. In 2026 most small businesses choose among three broad options.

## Three ways AI arrives in a business

**General-purpose assistants** are chat-style products that can help with many tasks: drafting, summarizing, brainstorming, analysis. They are flexible and inexpensive per user, but they know nothing about your business unless you tell them, and the quality of their output depends heavily on the instructions you give.

**Embedded features** are AI functions added to software you already use: an email client that suggests replies, an accounting package that proposes categories, a scheduling tool that drafts reminders. They are convenient and already connected to your data, but you have less control over how they work and fewer ways to test them.

**Specialist tools** are products built for one job, such as call transcription, review response, or invoice extraction. They are often more accurate on that job and come with sensible defaults, but each adds a vendor, a login, a data flow, and a bill.

| Factor | General assistant | Embedded feature | Specialist tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flexibility | High | Low | Low to medium |
| Setup effort | Low to medium | Very low | Medium |
| Access to your data | Only what you paste or connect | Already connected | Through integration |
| Control over behavior | High (via instructions) | Low | Medium |
| New vendor risk | One vendor, many uses | None new | One new vendor per job |
| Best for | Varied drafting and analysis | Small conveniences | High-volume single tasks |

## Build, buy, or configure

Small businesses rarely need to build AI systems. The realistic choice is between **buying** a finished product, **configuring** a general tool (writing standing instructions, connecting a document library, setting up templates), or **combining** an AI step with a workflow automation tool. A useful rule of thumb: configure before you buy, and buy before you build. Configuration is cheap to reverse; a purchased specialist tool brings a contract; custom builds bring maintenance obligations that small teams often underestimate.

## Separating claims from marketing

The Federal Trade Commission has made clear that there is no AI exemption from the laws against deceptive claims. In September 2024 it announced a law-enforcement sweep called Operation AI Comply, including actions against a company marketing an "AI lawyer" and several business-opportunity schemes that promised AI-powered passive income [6]. The settlement with DoNotPay required it to pay $193,000 and barred it from claiming its service could substitute for a professional without evidence [7].

These cases concern sellers, but they teach buyers what to look for. When a vendor makes a claim, ask:

1. **Compared with what?** "Saves 10 hours a week" relative to which baseline and which task?
2. **Measured how?** Was the result from a controlled test, a customer survey, or a single anecdote?
3. **On whose data?** Results on a vendor's demonstration data may not transfer to your documents.
4. **What happens when it is wrong?** Every tool errs. A vendor who will not discuss error handling has not thought about your risk.
5. **Where does my data go?** You will need the answer for Chapter 5.

:::case Operation AI Comply
The FTC's 2024 sweep included complaints against Ascend Ecom, Ecommerce Empire Builders, and FBA Machine for allegedly using AI-themed earnings claims to sell online-store business opportunities, and against Rytr for offering a tool that generated fake consumer reviews [6][7]. For a small business, the lesson is twofold: be skeptical of tools sold with income promises, and never use AI to fabricate reviews or testimonials about your own business.
:::

:::example Illustrative: narrowing to a short list
A landscaping company wants help drafting quotes from site-visit notes. It considers seven products. Two are rejected because they would require uploading customer addresses to a vendor whose terms allow training on customer data. One is rejected because it only works in a language the crew does not use. One embedded feature in the firm's existing invoicing software is kept because it requires no new vendor. Of the remaining three general and specialist tools, the firm keeps two whose vendors answered all five questions in writing. The short list is three tools: one embedded feature, one general assistant configured with a quote template, and one specialist quoting tool.
:::

:::checklist Short-list criteria
- The tool can accept the inputs named in my task definition.
- The vendor has answered the five claim questions in writing.
- The data terms are compatible with my data boundary (Chapter 5).
- The tool lets a person review output before it reaches a customer.
- I can cancel without losing my data.
- My short list has three or fewer tools.
:::

:::summary
AI arrives as general assistants, embedded features, or specialist tools, each with characteristic strengths and costs. Configure before you buy, and buy before you build. Treat vendor claims as hypotheses to test, using five questions, and remember that regulators have acted against exaggerated AI claims.
:::

# Chapter 4: Building a Fair Test

:::objectives
- Assemble a representative test set from your own work.
- Design a scoring rubric with explicit criteria and weights.
- Set a pass threshold before testing begins.
- Run a blind or semi-blind comparison and interpret the results.
:::

Choosing a tool on the strength of a demonstration is like hiring an employee on the strength of a firm handshake. A fair test uses your own tasks, compares candidates under the same conditions, and decides the standard of success before anyone sees the results.

## Building the test set

A **test set** is a collection of real or realistic inputs, each paired with a note about what a good output looks like. For most small-business tasks, 20 to 30 items are enough to reveal large differences between tools. Include:

- **Typical cases** (about 60 percent): the everyday version of the task.
- **Hard cases** (about 25 percent): ambiguous requests, missing information, unusual formats.
- **Trap cases** (about 15 percent): inputs where the correct response is to refuse, ask a question, or escalate to a person, such as a customer asking for a refund outside policy.

Remove or replace personal information before using real messages. Chapter 5 explains why.

## The scoring rubric

A **rubric** turns quality into numbers. For each output, score three to five criteria on a simple scale, such as 0 (fails), 1 (needs major edits), 2 (needs minor edits), and 3 (usable as is). Typical criteria are:

| Criterion | What a 3 looks like | Suggested weight |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Every fact matches the source material | 40% |
| Completeness | Answers the whole request, no gaps | 20% |
| Tone and policy fit | Matches house style; respects policy limits | 20% |
| Safe handling | Escalates or refuses when it should | 20% |

Weights reflect your priorities. For customer-facing work, accuracy and safe handling should dominate. For internal brainstorming, completeness might matter more.

## Setting the threshold in advance

Write down the passing standard before you test. For example: "A tool passes if its weighted average is at least 2.4 out of 3, and it scores 3 on safe handling for every trap case." Setting the threshold first protects you from the very human tendency to move the goalposts toward the tool you already like.

## Running the comparison

Give each tool the same instructions and the same inputs. Where possible, have someone other than the person who ran the tool score the outputs without knowing which tool produced which (a **blind** review). In a two-person business, true blinding may be impossible; at least shuffle the outputs and remove tool names. Record the time spent editing each output, because editing time is the real cost of a mediocre draft.

:::example Illustrative: scoring two tools on 24 test items
A property-management firm tests two tools on 24 tenant emails (15 typical, 6 hard, 3 trap). The threshold is a weighted average of 2.4 and perfect safe handling on all trap cases.

| Result | Tool A | Tool B |
|---|---|---|
| Weighted average (out of 3) | 2.55 | 2.62 |
| Trap cases handled correctly | 3 of 3 | 2 of 3 |
| Average editing time per item | 1.8 minutes | 1.2 minutes |
| Pass? | Yes | No (failed a trap case) |

Tool B produced slightly better drafts and needed less editing, but on one trap case it promised a rent credit that the firm's policy does not allow. Under the pre-set rule, Tool B fails. The firm could revise Tool B's instructions and retest, but it may not simply overlook the failure. This is exactly the kind of error that created liability in the Air Canada case [1].
:::

## Interpreting small samples

With 24 items, a difference of 0.07 in average score is noise; a difference of 0.5 probably is not. Treat close results as ties and decide on cost, data terms, or ease of use. Treat any failure on a trap case as serious, because such failures tend to be the costly ones.

:::worksheet Test plan
Task being tested: ___
Number of test items (typical / hard / trap): ___
Criteria and weights: ___
___
Pass threshold (written before testing): ___
Who runs the tools: ___
Who scores, and is it blind?: ___
Date results will be reviewed: ___
:::

:::summary
A fair test uses 20–30 of your own tasks, including hard and trap cases; scores outputs on a weighted rubric; and applies a threshold set in advance. Editing time is an essential measure. Trap-case failures should disqualify a tool until its instructions are fixed and the tool is retested.
:::

1. What proportion of a test set should be trap cases, and why include them?
2. Why must the pass threshold be written before testing?
3. In the worked example, why did the higher-scoring tool fail?
4. How can a very small business approximate a blind review?

# Chapter 5: Setting a Boundary for Data

:::objectives
- Classify business information into four sensitivity tiers.
- Read the key data terms in an AI vendor agreement.
- Apply data minimization and redaction before sharing information with a tool.
- Recognize the legal duties that commonly apply to customer data.
:::

Every AI tool is also a data pipeline. When you paste a customer email into an assistant or connect a document folder to a retrieval tool, information leaves your control and enters a vendor's systems. A data boundary is a written rule that says what may cross that line and under what conditions.

## Four tiers of information

| Tier | Description | Examples | Default rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public | Already published | Website text, posted prices, public reviews | May be used with any approved tool |
| Internal | Not secret, but not public | Procedures, templates, meeting notes without personal data | Approved business accounts only |
| Confidential | Harmful if disclosed | Customer lists, pricing strategy, contracts, employee records | Only tools with contractual data protections; minimize |
| Restricted | Regulated or highly sensitive | Payment card numbers, Social Security numbers, health or financial account data, passwords | Never enter into general AI tools |

The tiers are simple on purpose. Staff should be able to classify a document in a few seconds.

## Reading the vendor's data terms

Before you approve any tool for internal or confidential information, find answers to these questions in the vendor's terms of service, privacy policy, or data processing agreement:

1. **Training use.** Will the vendor use your inputs or outputs to train its models? Many business plans allow you to opt out or exclude business data by default; many free consumer plans do not.
2. **Retention.** How long are inputs stored, and can you delete them?
3. **Access.** Which vendor employees or subcontractors can see your data, and under what circumstances?
4. **Location.** Where is data processed and stored?
5. **Security.** Does the vendor describe its security controls, such as encryption and independent audits?
6. **Breach notice.** Will the vendor tell you if your data is exposed, and how quickly?

If you cannot find the answers, ask in writing. Treat a refusal to answer as a reason to keep confidential data out of the tool.

## Minimization and redaction

**Data minimization** means sharing only the information a task requires. A tool drafting a reply to a complaint about a late delivery needs the complaint and the delivery policy; it does not need the customer's address, phone number, or order history. **Redaction** means removing or replacing identifying details before sharing, for example replacing "Maria Lopez, 14 Elm St." with "[Customer], [Address]."

The National Institute of Standards and Technology's AI Risk Management Framework encourages organizations to map the context in which an AI system is used, including the data it touches, before deciding whether to deploy it [8][9]. Minimization is the small-business version of that mapping step.

## Legal duties that commonly apply

The Federal Trade Commission enforces against unfair or deceptive practices, which can include failing to protect customer data you have promised to protect. Its guidance for businesses, *Protecting Personal Information*, recommends knowing what personal information you hold, keeping only what you need, protecting it, disposing of it properly, and planning for incidents [10]. Depending on your industry and state, additional rules may apply: health information, financial information, children's data, and state privacy laws each carry their own requirements. If you handle such data, consult a qualified adviser before connecting it to any AI tool.

:::warning Free accounts and personal accounts
Employees often use free personal AI accounts for work because they are convenient. Those accounts may carry consumer terms that permit training on inputs and offer the business no administrative control. Your policy (Appendix A) should require approved business accounts for any internal or confidential information.
:::

:::example Illustrative: redacting a support ticket
Original ticket: "Hi, this is Dana Whitfield (dana.w@example.com, 555-0142). My order #48812 shipped to 22 Harbor Lane but the tracking says delivered and I never got it. Card ending 4471."

Redacted for the drafting tool: "Customer reports order shows delivered per tracking but was not received. Request: help drafting a reply following our lost-package policy (attached)."

The redacted version contains everything the tool needs to draft a reply and nothing that identifies the customer. The staff member adds the name and order number back when sending. The redaction took about 20 seconds.
:::

:::checklist Data boundary
- Every document type we use with AI is assigned a tier.
- Restricted information is never entered into general AI tools.
- Each approved tool's training, retention, and access terms are on file.
- Staff use business accounts, not personal accounts, for work data.
- We redact names and identifiers when the task does not need them.
- We know which industry or state privacy rules apply to us.
:::

:::summary
An AI tool is a data pipeline. Classify information into four tiers, read vendor terms on training, retention, access, location, security, and breach notice, and share only what a task needs. Restricted data stays out of general tools. Regulators expect businesses to protect the personal information they hold, whatever software they use.
:::

# Chapter 6: Writing Instructions and Checking the Draft

:::objectives
- Structure an instruction (prompt) using a six-part template.
- Provide reference material so the tool draws on approved facts.
- Design a verification step proportionate to the risk of the task.
- Maintain a small library of tested instructions.
:::

The quality of output from a generative tool depends heavily on the quality of the instructions it receives. This is less mysterious than it sounds. A capable new employee given a vague request ("write something to this customer") will produce something vague. The same employee given context, a goal, constraints, and an example will produce something useful. AI tools behave similarly.

## The six-part instruction

1. **Role and context.** Who the output is for and what the business does. "You are drafting replies for a family-owned bicycle repair shop in a mid-sized city."
2. **Task.** The specific job. "Draft a reply to the customer email below."
3. **Source material.** The approved facts the tool must use. "Use only the policies in the attached fact sheet. If the answer is not in the fact sheet, say that a staff member will follow up."
4. **Constraints.** What must and must not appear. "Do not promise refunds, credits, or delivery dates. Keep it under 120 words."
5. **Format and tone.** "Plain, warm, professional. Short paragraphs. Sign as 'The Rivera Team.'"
6. **Example.** One short example of a good output, if you have it.

The third and fourth parts do most of the safety work. Telling the tool to rely only on provided material, and to say so when the material does not cover a question, sharply reduces invented answers, though it does not eliminate them.

## Grounding in approved sources

**Grounding** means giving the tool the specific documents it should rely on: a fact sheet, a policy, a price list, a product manual. Many tools now allow you to attach a standing set of documents. Keep these documents short, current, and owned by a named person. An out-of-date price list produces confidently wrong quotes.

## Proportionate verification

Not every output needs the same scrutiny. Match the review to the risk:

| Risk level | Example | Verification |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Internal brainstorming, first-draft social captions | Quick read for sense and tone |
| Medium | Customer replies on routine questions | Check every fact against the source; check against the never list |
| High | Quotes, contracts, anything involving money or legal rights | Line-by-line check by a qualified person; second reviewer for new templates |
| Prohibited | Decisions about credit, employment, housing, insurance, medical care | Do not use generative AI to make or recommend the decision |

A useful habit for medium-risk work is the **three-point check**: (1) Is every fact traceable to the source material? (2) Does anything violate the never list? (3) Would I be comfortable if this message were read aloud in a dispute?

:::example Illustrative: before and after an instruction rewrite
A salon owner asks a tool, "Write a reply to this client about rescheduling." The draft offers a 20 percent discount the salon does not offer. She rewrites the instruction using the six parts, attaches the salon's two-paragraph rescheduling policy, adds "Do not offer discounts or free services," and includes one example reply. She reruns the same five test emails. Before the rewrite, 2 of 5 drafts contained an unauthorized promise. After the rewrite, none did, and her average editing time fell from about 3 minutes to about 1 minute per draft. Five emails are too few to prove much, so she adds the instruction to her test set and continues measuring during the pilot.
:::

## The instruction library

Once an instruction works on your test set, save it in a shared document with its name, purpose, the date tested, the person responsible, and the source documents it relies on. This **instruction library** is a business asset. It lets new staff produce consistent work, and it lets you retest quickly when a vendor updates its model, which happens frequently and can change behavior.

:::summary

# Chapter 7: Piloting With a Person in Control

:::objectives
- Design a time-limited pilot with entry and exit criteria.
- Define human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, and human-out-of-the-loop operation.
- Track pilot metrics that reveal both benefits and failures.
- Make an evidence-based decision to expand, revise, or stop.
:::

A test tells you whether a tool can do a task under controlled conditions. A pilot tells you whether it does the task well in daily operations, with real interruptions, real customers, and real staff habits. Pilots should be short, bounded, and designed to produce a decision.

## Three levels of human control

- **Human in the loop.** A person reviews and approves every output before it takes effect. This is the default for any new customer-facing use.
- **Human on the loop.** The system acts on its own, but a person monitors a sample of outputs and can intervene. Appropriate only after a task has a measured, acceptable error rate.
- **Human out of the loop.** The system acts without routine human review. Rarely appropriate for small businesses using generative AI on anything that touches customers or money.

Moving from one level to the next should be a deliberate decision recorded in writing, based on pilot data.

## Designing the pilot

A good pilot specifies:

1. **Scope.** Which task, which staff, which customers or channels. Start narrow.
2. **Duration.** Typically two to six weeks; long enough to see variation, short enough to stay focused.
3. **Control level.** Almost always human in the loop at first.
4. **Metrics.** Volume handled, editing time, error rate (errors caught in review), escaped errors (errors that reached a customer), customer feedback, and staff feedback.
5. **Stop rules.** Conditions that end the pilot immediately, such as any escaped error involving money, a privacy incident, or a customer complaint attributable to the tool.
6. **Decision date.** When and by whom the expand, revise, or stop decision will be made.

:::case Klarna: scale, then rebalancing
In February 2024 the payments company Klarna announced that its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, about two-thirds of its customer service chats, and said this was equivalent to the work of 700 full-time agents; it also reported that customers could still choose a live agent [11]. These were company-reported figures. Later reporting summarized statements from the company in 2025 that it would again hire human agents so that customers always had the option of a person, with the chief executive saying cost had been too dominant a factor [12]. Whatever one concludes about Klarna, the episode shows why pilots need quality metrics and a guaranteed route to a human, not only volume and cost metrics. Klarna's results are its own, not TTI's.
:::

## Reading pilot data

The most important number in a pilot is often the **escaped error rate**: errors that got past review and reached a customer. A tool that saves time but lets errors escape is shifting cost from your staff to your customers, and eventually back to you through complaints, refunds, or reputation. The second most important number is **editing time**, because it reveals whether the tool is producing usable drafts or merely creating rework.

Research supports measured optimism. A study of 5,179 customer-support agents at a large software company found that access to a generative AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by about 14 percent on average, with gains of about 34 percent for newer and less-skilled agents and minimal gains for the most experienced [13]. The study measured a tool that suggested responses to human agents, who could accept or ignore them: a human-in-the-loop design. Its findings are about that firm and that setting; your pilot should measure your own.

:::example Illustrative: four-week pilot results
A home-cleaning company pilots AI-drafted replies to booking inquiries, with the office manager approving every draft.

| Week | Inquiries | Avg. minutes per reply (before: 6.0) | Errors caught in review | Escaped errors |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 62 | 4.5 | 7 | 0 |
| 2 | 58 | 3.6 | 4 | 0 |
| 3 | 71 | 3.2 | 3 | 1 (wrong service-area town) |
| 4 | 66 | 3.0 | 2 | 0 |

Time per reply fell from 6.0 to 3.0 minutes. Across 257 inquiries, that is about 3 minutes saved per reply, or roughly 12.9 hours over four weeks. One escaped error, a reply saying the company served a town it does not, was traced to an out-of-date service-area list in the source documents. The list was corrected. Decision: expand to all booking inquiries, keep human-in-the-loop for at least another eight weeks, and add service area to the weekly source-document check.
:::

:::checklist Pilot design
- Scope is limited to one task and named staff.
- Duration and decision date are written down.
- Every output is reviewed by a person (human in the loop).
- We track editing time, errors caught, and escaped errors.
- Stop rules are defined and known to staff.
- Customers can always reach a person.
:::

:::summary
Pilots test tools in real operations under human control. Specify scope, duration, control level, metrics, stop rules, and a decision date. Escaped errors and editing time are the most revealing metrics. Public evidence, including Klarna's reported experience and the Brynjolfsson study, supports a design in which AI assists people and customers can always reach one.
:::

# Chapter 8: Counting the Full Cost

:::objectives
- Identify the direct, indirect, and risk costs of an AI tool.
- Build a monthly total-cost-of-ownership estimate.
- Calculate a simple payback period and break-even volume.
- Avoid common errors in estimating time savings.
:::

Subscription prices are the most visible cost of an AI tool and often the smallest. A sound decision counts everything.

## Three categories of cost

**Direct costs** include subscription fees, usage charges (many tools bill by volume), add-ons, and any integration or setup fees.

**Indirect costs** include staff time spent learning the tool, writing and maintaining instructions, reviewing outputs, updating source documents, and handling exceptions. Review time is the largest indirect cost for most customer-facing uses and is easy to forget.

**Risk costs** are expected losses from errors: refunds, credits, rework, complaints, and in rare cases legal exposure. You cannot know these precisely, but you can estimate them from your pilot's error rates.

## The total-cost-of-ownership estimate

Total cost of ownership (TCO) per month = direct costs + (indirect hours × loaded hourly cost) + estimated risk cost.

The **loaded hourly cost** of an employee is wages plus payroll taxes and benefits, divided by hours worked. For owners, use the hourly value from Chapter 2.

## Payback and break-even

**Monthly net benefit** = (hours saved × loaded hourly cost) − TCO.
**Payback period** = one-time setup cost ÷ monthly net benefit.
**Break-even volume** = the number of tasks per month at which savings equal costs.

:::example Illustrative: cost model for AI-drafted booking replies
Continuing the cleaning company from Chapter 7. Figures are invented for teaching.

| Item | Monthly amount |
|---|---|
| Subscription (2 seats) | $60 |
| Usage charges | $15 |
| Instruction and source-document upkeep: 2 hours × $28 | $56 |
| Review time (already included in the 3.0 minutes per reply) | — |
| Estimated risk cost: 1 escaped error per month × $40 average remedy | $40 |
| **Total cost of ownership** | **$171** |

Savings: about 280 inquiries per month × 3 minutes saved = 840 minutes = 14 hours × $28 loaded cost = $392.

Monthly net benefit = $392 − $171 = $221. One-time setup took 10 hours (= $280), so payback ≈ $280 ÷ $221 ≈ 1.3 months.

Break-even volume: each reply saves 3 minutes × ($28 ÷ 60) = $1.40. Fixed monthly costs (subscription, upkeep, risk) are about $156, and usage charges are roughly $0.05 per reply. Break-even ≈ $156 ÷ ($1.40 − $0.05) ≈ 116 replies per month. Below that volume the tool costs more than it saves.
:::

## Errors in estimating savings

- **Counting theoretical minutes.** Saved minutes only become value if they are used for something worthwhile. If the office manager's day simply gets shorter, that may be a real benefit, but it is not cash.
- **Ignoring the learning curve.** Early weeks are slower. Use pilot data from later weeks.
- **Forgetting maintenance.** Policies, prices, and tools change. Budget upkeep time every month.
- **Assuming linear scaling.** Doubling volume does not always halve unit cost; review time scales with volume.

:::warning No promised returns
The figures in this chapter illustrate a method. Your costs and savings depend on your task, volume, staff, and tool. Do not budget on the assumption that any tool will pay for itself; measure it.
:::

:::worksheet Monthly cost of ownership
Subscription and usage fees: ___
Setup cost (one time): ___
Upkeep hours × loaded rate: ___

Estimated risk cost: ___

Hours saved per month × loaded rate: ___

:::summary

# Chapter 9: AI in Front of Customers

:::objectives
- Decide when customers should be told they are interacting with AI.
- Design reliable escalation to a human.
- Apply truth-in-advertising principles to AI-generated marketing.
- Prevent the most common customer-facing failures.
:::

Internal uses of AI forgive mistakes; customer-facing uses do not. When a tool speaks to customers, its words are your words, as the *Moffatt* tribunal made explicit [1]. This chapter sets out the minimum standards for any AI that customers see or hear.

## Disclosure

Telling customers when they are dealing with an automated system is good practice and, in some places and contexts, a legal requirement. Rules vary by jurisdiction and change frequently, so check current requirements for your state and industry. Independent of the law, disclosure sets expectations: customers who know they are chatting with an assistant are more forgiving of limits and more likely to ask for a person when they need one. A simple line works: "I'm an automated assistant for Rivera Bicycle Repair. I can answer questions about hours, prices, and repair status, or connect you with a person."

## Escalation that works

Every customer-facing AI needs a reliable path to a human. Design it around three triggers:

1. **Customer request.** Any request for a person is honored promptly, without making the customer repeat themselves.
2. **Topic triggers.** Refunds, complaints, safety, legal threats, health, and anything on your never list route to a person.
3. **Confidence triggers.** When the tool's source material does not cover the question, it says so and hands off.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warned in 2023 that poorly designed chatbots in consumer finance can leave customers stuck in loops without reaching a human, which it described as a potential source of consumer harm [14]. The warning applies by analogy to any business: a closed loop is a complaint waiting to happen.

## Marketing content

Generative tools make it easy to produce product descriptions, ads, and social posts. The legal standard for that content has not changed: claims must be truthful, not misleading, and supported by evidence. The FTC's action against Rytr, a tool that could generate fake reviews, and its rules on consumer reviews make one line absolute: never use AI to fabricate reviews, testimonials, or endorsements [6][15]. Images also deserve care. A generated photograph of a renovation you never performed is a misleading claim about your work.

## Common failures and controls

| Failure | Example | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Invented policy | Chatbot promises a refund window you do not offer | Ground in policy documents; never list; topic escalation |
| Out-of-date facts | Old prices or hours | Named owner for source documents; weekly check |
| Overconfident answers | Tool answers a question outside its sources | Instruction to say "I don't know" and hand off |
| Tone failure | Flippant reply to a serious complaint | Complaint topics escalate to a person |
| Privacy leak | Tool reveals one customer's details to another | Do not connect tools to records they do not need; test with trap cases |

:::example Illustrative: designing a website assistant's scope
A dental-supply retailer plans a website assistant. It writes a scope statement: the assistant may answer questions about shipping times, return windows, store hours, and product specifications taken from manufacturer sheets; it may not discuss clinical use, pricing for bulk contracts, or order disputes. It writes 30 test questions, including 8 out-of-scope traps ("Is this product safe for a patient with a latex allergy?"). The first configuration answers 3 of 8 trap questions instead of escalating. After instruction changes, it escalates all 8 in two consecutive test runs. Only then does the retailer launch, with a visible "Talk to a person" button and a daily review of 20 randomly sampled conversations for the first month.
:::

:::checklist Customer-facing minimum standard
- Customers are told when they are dealing with an automated system.
- Customers can reach a person at any point.
- Refunds, complaints, safety, and legal topics route to a person.
- The tool is grounded in current, owned source documents.
- We never generate reviews, testimonials, or images of work we did not do.
- We review a sample of conversations at least weekly.
:::

:::summary
Customer-facing AI speaks for your business. Disclose automation, guarantee escalation to a person, ground answers in current documents, and apply the same truth-in-advertising standards to AI-generated marketing as to anything else. Test scope limits with trap questions before launch and sample conversations afterward.
:::

# Chapter 10: Bringing Your Team Along

:::objectives
- Explain the role of staff knowledge in making AI tools useful.
- Use screen-recording to turn tacit knowledge into written procedures.
- Train staff on both use and limits.
- Address common staff concerns honestly.
:::

AI tools do not adopt themselves. The firms that benefit are usually those whose staff understand the work well enough to instruct the tool, check its output, and notice when something is off. Adoption is therefore a training and change-management project as much as a technology project.

## Tacit knowledge is the raw material

Much of what makes a business run well lives in people's heads: how to calm an upset customer, which supplier to call when a part is late, what "rush" really means to a particular client. Economists call this **tacit knowledge**. AI tools cannot use knowledge that has never been written down. The instruction library (Chapter 6) and source documents (Chapter 5) are, in effect, tacit knowledge made explicit.

The Brynjolfsson study offers suggestive evidence that a well-built AI assistant can spread the practices of top performers to newer staff, helping them move up the experience curve faster [13]. The authors note that the gains were largest for less experienced agents. The implication for a small business is that capturing your best people's methods is valuable with or without AI, and doubly so with it.

## The recording method

Dan Martell's Camcorder Method offers a practical technique: record yourself doing a task, narrating your reasoning, ideally more than once to capture variations; then have someone else (or a tool) turn the recording into a written procedure and a checklist [4][5]. Martell describes using a transcript and an AI assistant to draft the procedure document. In this book's terms:

1. Record the expert doing the task three times, talking aloud.
2. Transcribe the recordings.
3. Ask a writing assistant to draft a step-by-step procedure and a closing quality checklist from the transcript.
4. Have the expert correct the draft. This step is not optional; the draft will contain errors and omissions.
5. Store the corrected procedure as a source document and use it in your instruction library.

## Training on use and limits

A one-hour session covering the following is usually enough to start:

- What the approved tools are and what they are approved for.
- The data tiers and the never list.
- How to use the instruction library.
- How to check output (the three-point check).
- How to report an error or incident.
- Who to ask when unsure.

Repeat a short refresher whenever a tool, policy, or major instruction changes.

## Addressing concerns

Staff may worry that AI will replace them, judge them, or make their work harder. Be honest. If the goal is to free time for higher-value work, say what that work is. If roles will change, say so early. Involve staff in choosing test cases and scoring outputs; they know the hard cases better than anyone. In the Brynjolfsson study, the authors report that AI assistance was associated with improved customer sentiment and higher employee retention [13], but that was one firm's experience and should not be presented to staff as a promise.

:::example Illustrative: capturing a dispatcher's know-how
A plumbing company's veteran dispatcher, who has done the job for twelve years, is the only person who knows how to prioritize emergency calls. The owner records her handling three typical mornings, narrating her decisions. A writing assistant drafts a nine-step triage procedure from the transcripts. She corrects four steps and adds two rules the draft missed (gas smell means call the utility first; elderly customers without heat jump the queue in winter). The procedure is now a training document for a new hire and a source document for a future AI tool that drafts call summaries. Total time: about four hours of her time and two of the owner's.
:::

Expert review date: ___

:::summary
AI tools depend on written-down knowledge. Recording experts at work and turning recordings into corrected procedures builds both a training library and AI source material. Train staff on use and limits, involve them in testing, and address concerns honestly.

# Chapter 11: Governing AI in a Small Firm

:::objectives
- Explain the four functions of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.
- Write a one-page AI use policy.
- Maintain a tool register and an incident log.
- Set a review cadence that a small team can sustain.
:::

Governance sounds like a word for large corporations. In a small firm it simply means knowing which tools you use, who is responsible for each, what the rules are, and how you find out when something goes wrong.

## A framework scaled down

The National Institute of Standards and Technology published its voluntary AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) in January 2023 [8][9]. Its core has four functions:

- **Govern**: cultivate a culture of risk management, with policies, roles, and accountability.
- **Map**: understand the context in which an AI system is used and the risks it may pose.
- **Measure**: assess and track those risks with appropriate methods.
- **Manage**: prioritize and act on risks, including plans to respond to and recover from incidents.

NIST notes that after mapping, users should have enough knowledge to make an initial go/no-go decision about deployment [9]. The chapters of this book map onto these functions: task definition and data boundaries are mapping; testing and pilots are measuring; policies and incident logs are governing and managing.

| NIST function | Small-firm practice | Where in this book |
|---|---|---|
| Govern | One-page policy; named owner per tool | Chapter 11, Appendix A |
| Map | Task definition; data tiers; never list | Chapters 2 and 5 |
| Measure | Test set; rubric; pilot metrics | Chapters 4 and 7 |
| Manage | Stop rules; incident log; review cadence | Chapters 7 and 11 |

## The one-page policy

A small-firm AI policy should fit on one page and cover: approved tools and their approved uses; data rules (tiers and never list); review requirements by risk level; customer-facing standards (disclosure, escalation); prohibited uses; how to report problems; and the policy owner and review date. Appendix A provides a template.

## Tool register and incident log

The **tool register** is a simple table listing every AI tool in use: name, purpose, owner, data tier allowed, account type, date approved, and date of last review. It prevents "shadow AI," tools staff adopted without anyone knowing.

The **incident log** records every escaped error, privacy concern, or customer complaint involving an AI tool: date, tool, what happened, impact, cause, fix, and follow-up. Patterns in the log tell you where to tighten controls. Appendix E provides a template.

## Review cadence

- **Weekly** (15 minutes): sample outputs from customer-facing tools; check source documents for changes.
- **Monthly** (30 minutes): review the incident log; check usage and costs against the TCO estimate.
- **Quarterly** (1 hour): review the tool register; retest key instructions; update the policy if needed.
- **On change**: whenever a vendor announces a major model or terms change, rerun the relevant test set.

:::example Illustrative: a quarterly review finds drift
At its quarterly review, a marketing agency reruns its 25-item test set for its proposal-drafting instruction. Average score has fallen from 2.6 to 2.2, below its 2.4 threshold. The vendor had updated its underlying model six weeks earlier. The agency revises two lines of the instruction, reruns the test, and recovers to 2.5. Without the scheduled retest, the decline would have shown up only as slowly rising editing time and, eventually, an error in front of a client.
:::

:::checklist Governance basics
- We have a one-page AI policy with an owner and review date.
- Every AI tool is listed in the tool register with a named owner.
- Incidents are logged and reviewed monthly.
- Key instructions are retested quarterly and after vendor changes.
- Staff know how to report a problem.
:::

:::summary
Governance in a small firm means knowing your tools, owners, rules, and problems. The NIST AI RMF's four functions (govern, map, measure, manage) provide a structure that scales down to a one-page policy, a tool register, an incident log, and a regular review cadence.
:::

# Glossary

**Agent (AI agent).** An AI system that takes actions, such as sending messages or updating records, rather than only producing drafts.

**Blind review.** Scoring outputs without knowing which tool produced them.

**Break-even volume.** The monthly task volume at which a tool's savings equal its costs.

**Confabulation (hallucination).** A fluent but false statement produced by a generative model.

**Data minimization.** Sharing only the information a task requires.

**Escaped error.** An error that passed review and reached a customer or took effect.

**Generative AI.** Machine-learning systems that produce new text, images, audio, or code from a prompt.

**Grounding.** Supplying a tool with specific approved documents to rely on.

**Human in the loop.** An operating mode in which a person approves every output before it takes effect.

**Incident log.** A record of errors and problems involving AI tools, used to find and fix patterns.

**Large language model (LLM).** A generative model trained on large amounts of text to produce written responses.

**Loaded hourly cost.** Wages plus payroll taxes and benefits, divided by hours worked.

**Never list.** Outcomes a tool must never produce, written into instructions and checked in review.

**Prompt (instruction).** The text given to a generative tool describing the task.

**Redaction.** Removing or replacing identifying details before sharing information.

**Rubric.** A scoring scheme with explicit criteria and weights.

**Tacit knowledge.** Know-how held in people's heads rather than in documents.

**Test set.** A collection of representative inputs used to evaluate tools.

**Total cost of ownership (TCO).** All direct, indirect, and risk costs of a tool over a period.

**Trap case.** A test input where the correct behavior is to refuse, ask, or escalate.
