# How to Use This Book

This book is for people who want to start a small business in a city: a neighborhood service, a home-based product line, a delivery or errand business, a market stall, or a storefront. Cities offer dense customer bases, short travel distances, and rich networks of suppliers and partners. They also bring high costs, crowded streets, complex permits, and intense competition. This book teaches a disciplined way to test an idea in that environment before committing serious money.

A running example appears throughout. Maya, a fictional entrepreneur, is testing a bike-based service that picks up completed print orders (flyers, menus, posters, business cards) from local print shops and delivers them to businesses within one mixed-use corridor. Her numbers are invented for teaching. Each chapter also includes a real case drawn from public sources: a city's cargo bike program, founders who started in a kitchen or a family home, a bakery that rebuilt hiring around its neighborhood, and others. Those results are their own, not TTI's, and they are not predictions of yours.

Each chapter includes learning objectives, definitions, a framework, a worked example labelled "Illustrative," a case, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. The appendices contain templates you can copy and use.

## What you will be able to do

- Define a narrow, testable business promise suited to a city neighborhood.
- Research customers and alternatives ethically and efficiently.
- Identify the permits, taxes, and insurance questions that apply to you, and whom to ask.
- Estimate startup costs and find responsible sources of small-business capital.
- Design operations, safety practices, and partnerships that build trust.
- Run a time-limited test and decide, using evidence, whether to stop, change, or grow.

:::warning Rules vary by place
Licenses, zoning, sidewalk and street rules, sales tax, and insurance requirements differ by city, county, and state, and they change. This book tells you which questions to ask and which offices usually answer them. Always confirm with the responsible agency or a licensed professional before acting.
:::

Disclaimer: this book is educational material only; it is not legal, tax, or financial advice, and no income or business result is promised or implied.

# Chapter 1: The Urban Opportunity

:::objectives
- Explain how density shapes the economics of a city business.
- Identify the main advantages and constraints of urban markets.
- Describe what a city's cargo bike program reveals about urban logistics.
- Select a corridor or neighborhood for a first test.
:::

**Density** is the number of people, households, or businesses in a given area. It is the defining feature of an urban market. High density means more potential customers within walking or cycling distance, more foot traffic past a storefront, and shorter trips between deliveries. It also means higher rents, more competitors, and more rules about how public space is used.

## Advantages and constraints

| Advantages of urban markets | Constraints of urban markets |
|---|---|
| Many customers within a small radius | High rent and cost of living |
| Short distances between stops | Congestion and limited curb space |
| Diverse suppliers and partners nearby | Dense competition |
| Strong word-of-mouth networks | Complex permits and zoning |
| Transit access for staff and customers | Higher insurance and security costs |
| Customers who value time savings | Noise, space, and neighbor considerations |

## A corridor as the unit of analysis

A **corridor** is a street or connected set of blocks with a mix of activity: shops, offices, restaurants, and homes. For a first test, a corridor is a better unit than a whole city. It is small enough to walk in an afternoon, to know personally, and to serve reliably. Maya's test is limited to one corridor of about 20 blocks containing four print shops and roughly 200 small businesses.

## Reading a neighborhood

Before choosing a corridor, read it the way an urban planner would. Walk it at different times of day and on different days of the week. Note the **anchors** (large employers, transit stations, schools, hospitals) that generate steady traffic; the **mix** of uses (offices, shops, restaurants, homes); the **rhythm** (when it is busy and when it is quiet); and the **signals of change** (new construction, vacancies, new businesses opening or closing). A corridor anchored by a hospital and a transit stop has different needs and rhythms from one dominated by nightlife.

Neighborhood change also raises ethical questions. A new business can serve long-time residents well or primarily cater to newcomers. Entrepreneurs who live in or come from a neighborhood often understand its needs best, and those who arrive from outside do well to listen first, hire locally where they can, and price for the community they intend to serve.

:::case New York City's commercial cargo bike pilot
In December 2019, the New York City Department of Transportation launched a Commercial Cargo Bicycle Pilot with three participants (UPS, DHL, and Amazon) and 100 bikes in Manhattan. By January 2021 it had six participants and more than 350 bikes, and cargo bike deliveries had grown 109 percent between May 2020 and January 2021, to more than 45,000 in January 2021 alone [1]. The city's evaluation found that about 80 percent of deliveries went to residential addresses, that bikes typically spent five minutes or less unloading at each stop, and that each bike covered an average of 20 service miles a day [1]. A Federal Highway Administration case study noted that about 94 percent of deliveries occurred in the denser parts of Manhattan, and highlighted density as a key factor in the program's effectiveness [2]. The city later reported that in 2022 cargo bikes made more than 130,000 trips delivering over 5 million packages, and in March 2024 it adopted rules formally authorizing pedal-assist cargo bikes [3].

These results belong to large carriers operating with city support; they are not TTI's and do not predict what a solo operator will achieve. The lesson for a small urban entrepreneur is about method. The city started small, collected data, and expanded where the evidence was strongest, and density mattered. That is exactly how a corridor test should work.
:::

:::example Illustrative: density and trip time
Maya compares two service areas. Corridor A: 200 businesses along 20 blocks. Suburban strip B: 200 businesses spread over 5 miles.

In Corridor A, the average distance between consecutive stops on a route of 10 deliveries is about 2 blocks, or roughly 0.1 mile; at 8 mph average riding speed including stops at lights, each leg takes under a minute, plus about 5 minutes per handoff. A 10-stop route takes roughly 10 × (1 + 5) = 60 minutes.

In area B, the average leg is about 0.5 mile; at the same speed each leg takes about 4 minutes, so a 10-stop route takes 10 × (4 + 5) = 90 minutes, 50 percent longer. The same price produces less revenue per hour in B. Density is not just a demographic fact; it changes unit economics.
:::

:::checklist Choosing a test corridor
- I can walk the whole corridor in under two hours.
- I have counted the businesses or households likely to need my service.
- I know where people park, load, and lock bikes.
- I have noted competitors and alternatives already present.
- I can reach the corridor easily at the hours I plan to operate.
:::

:::summary
Density gives urban businesses more customers within a short radius but also brings higher costs, competition, and rules about public space. A corridor is a practical unit for a first test. New York City's cargo bike program shows the value of starting small, collecting data, and expanding where density makes the model work.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choose a Small Promise

:::objectives
- Define a business promise and explain why narrow promises are easier to keep.
- Write a one-sentence promise with clear boundaries.
- Use the "minimum testable offer" concept.
- Learn from founders who started small.
:::

A **promise** is the specific result a customer can expect: what you do, for whom, where, when, and at what price. New businesses often start with promises that are too broad ("we deliver anything, anywhere, anytime"). Broad promises hide decisions and are hard to keep. A narrow promise is easier to explain, test, and deliver reliably.

## The promise framework

Write your promise using five elements:

1. **Who** it serves (a specific customer type).
2. **What** it delivers (a specific product or service).
3. **Where** (a mapped area).
4. **When** (a schedule or time window).
5. **Terms** (price, payment, limits, and what happens if something goes wrong).

Maya's first draft read: "Fast delivery for anything in the neighborhood." Her revised promise reads: "For small businesses in the Elm Street corridor, we pick up prepaid print orders from participating print shops by 1 p.m. and deliver them between 2 and 5 p.m. the same day, for a flat $12 per order, with a photo confirmation at handoff."

## The minimum testable offer

A **minimum testable offer** is the smallest version of your service that real customers can buy, which lets you learn whether they value it. It is not a half-finished product; it is a complete service with a narrow scope.

| Broad promise | Minimum testable offer |
|---|---|
| Deliver anything | Deliver prepaid print orders only |
| Anywhere in the city | One 20-block corridor |
| Anytime | Weekdays, 2–5 p.m. |
| Variable pricing | Flat $12 per order |
| Handles cash | Prepaid only |
| Unattended drop-offs | Hand-to-hand with photo confirmation |

:::case Lisa Price and Carol's Daughter: starting at a flea market
Lisa Price began making body butters and creams in her Brooklyn kitchen to address her own dry skin. In 1993, encouraged by her mother, Carol, she sold her products at a church flea market after a $100 investment, then spent the summer selling at craft fairs and flea markets [4]. When customers began asking for hair products, she developed them in response, and in 1994 she formally established Carol's Daughter, named after her mother [4]. Price later opened a store in Brooklyn, the brand joined L'Oréal in 2014 [6], and in 2025 Carol's Daughter became an independent company again with Price as its president [5].

Price's results are her own, not TTI's, and her path included many years of work, outside investment, and circumstances unique to her. What transfers is the shape of the start: a small, specific offer sold directly to customers in a familiar place, with product development driven by what those customers asked for.
:::

:::example Illustrative: the cost of a broad promise
Suppose Maya had promised "delivery of anything within 3 miles, any time 9–7." In her first week she receives 14 requests: 5 print orders within the corridor, 3 furniture items, 2 food orders, 2 trips over 3 miles, and 2 requests outside her hours. She can fulfill only the 5 print orders reliably; the other 9 (64 percent) must be refused or attempted poorly, creating disappointed customers. Under the narrow promise, the same 5 print orders arrive, and no customer is disappointed by an unkept promise. The narrow promise does not reduce real demand; it filters out demand she could not serve well.
:::

# Chapter 3: Research Customers Without Intruding

:::objectives
- Distinguish secondary and primary research.
- Conduct respectful observation and interviews.
- Avoid collecting unnecessary personal information.
- Estimate demand from research findings.
:::

**Market research** is the systematic gathering of information about customers, competitors, and conditions. The SBA describes it as a way to learn about consumer behavior and economic trends, and distinguishes between existing data, useful for general questions, and direct research such as interviews and surveys, which addresses your specific questions [7].

## Secondary research

**Secondary research** uses information others have collected: census data, city business directories, chamber of commerce lists, industry reports, and public data such as transportation studies. It answers questions like how many businesses operate in a corridor, what types they are, and how the area is changing.

## Primary research

**Primary research** is information you collect yourself. In a city neighborhood, the most useful forms are:

- **Observation**: watching how people and goods move. Maya counted delivery vans, noted when print shops were busiest, and saw how many people walked out carrying boxes of printed materials.
- **Interviews**: short conversations with potential customers and partners. Ask about their current behavior, not hypothetical future purchases.
- **Small tests**: offering the service and seeing who buys (Chapter 12).

## Research ethics

Respect privacy and property. Observe from public space. Do not photograph people without consent, record conversations without permission, or collect personal information you do not need. Ask before entering a business to talk, and keep visits brief. Never misrepresent who you are or imply affiliation with a business or agency.

## Interview questions that work

| Weak question | Better question |
|---|---|
| Would you use a delivery service? | How did your last print order get from the shop to you? |
| How much would you pay? | What did that trip cost you in time or money? |
| Do you like this idea? | What went wrong the last time you needed materials quickly? |
| Would you recommend it? | Who else around here handles printed materials? |

Questions about past behavior produce more reliable answers than questions about intentions, because people are often polite about ideas they would not actually buy.

## Sample size and bias

How many interviews are enough? For early discovery, many practitioners find that patterns begin to repeat after 15 to 30 conversations with a well-defined customer type. That is not a statistical sample; it is a way to learn the language customers use, the problems they mention most, and the alternatives they rely on. Watch for three common biases:

- **Selection bias**: talking only to people who are friendly, available, or similar to you. Visit at different times and include different business types.
- **Politeness bias**: people say they like an idea to be kind. Focus on what they have done, not what they say they would do.
- **Confirmation bias**: hearing what you hoped to hear. Write down exact quotes and count responses before interpreting them.

A useful habit is to write, before the interviews, what result would make you abandon or change the idea. If 25 of 30 businesses say they never order printed materials, the idea needs a new customer, not a better pitch.

:::example Illustrative: from interviews to demand estimate
Maya interviews 30 small businesses in the corridor. Findings:
- 21 (70 percent) ordered printed materials in the past three months.
- Of those, 12 picked up orders themselves, losing an average of 40 minutes per trip.
- 9 of the 12 said a delivery would have helped "a lot."

She estimates: 200 businesses × 70 percent ordering × (12 ÷ 21 picking up) × (9 ÷ 12 wanting help) ≈ 60 businesses with a clear need. If each orders about once a month and half try the service, that is about 30 orders a month initially. At $12, that is $360 a month: useful for a test, far from a living. This tells Maya that print delivery alone may be a starting wedge rather than a full business, and she should explore adjacent services later.
:::

:::checklist Ethical research
- I observe from public spaces and respect property rules.
- I ask permission before interviewing and keep it brief.
- I do not collect names or contact details unless people volunteer them for follow-up.
- I ask about past behavior, not hypothetical purchases.
- I record findings in a simple tally, not personal profiles.
:::

:::summary
Market research combines secondary data with primary observation, interviews, and small tests. Ethical research respects privacy and property. Questions about past behavior produce more reliable insight than questions about intentions. Simple arithmetic turns research findings into a realistic demand estimate.
:::

# Chapter 4: Alternatives, Competition, and Pricing

:::objectives
- Identify direct competitors and indirect alternatives.
- Build a comparison table from the customer's perspective.
- Set a price based on cost, value, and alternatives.
- Calculate contribution margin per order.
:::

Every customer already has a way to solve their problem, even if it is "do it myself" or "do nothing." Your real competition includes these alternatives.

## Types of alternatives

- **Direct competitors**: businesses offering the same service (other couriers).
- **Indirect alternatives**: different ways to meet the same need (ride-hail drivers, the print shop's own delivery, app-based couriers).
- **Self-service**: the customer does it themselves.
- **Doing nothing**: the customer waits or skips the need.

## Comparing from the customer's view

| Option | Price to customer | Time cost | Reliability | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pick up personally | $0 cash | 40 minutes | High | Owner leaves the business |
| App-based courier | $15–25 (varies) | 5 minutes to book | Variable | Pricing varies by demand |
| Print shop delivery | Varies; some shops do not offer | None | Depends on shop | Minimums may apply |
| Maya's service | $12 flat | 2 minutes to request | To be proven | Corridor only, 2–5 p.m. |

The numbers in this table are illustrative; gather real prices locally.

## Pricing

Three reference points guide price:

1. **Cost floor**: what each order costs you to deliver, plus overhead.
2. **Value ceiling**: what the customer's alternative costs them, including time.
3. **Market reference**: what similar options charge.

**Contribution margin** is the price minus the variable costs of one order. It is what each order contributes to fixed costs and profit.

## Bundles and subscriptions

Two pricing structures can stabilize revenue for a small service. A **bundle** sells several units at a modest discount (for example, 10 deliveries for $110 instead of $120), which rewards loyal customers and brings cash in earlier. A **subscription** charges a fixed monthly amount for a defined service level (for example, up to 8 deliveries a month for $90). Both reduce the effort of selling each order individually. Both also create obligations: track prepaid balances carefully, honor them reliably, and make cancellation and refund terms clear before the customer pays.

:::example Illustrative: contribution margin and break-even
Maya's variable costs per order: payment processing 3 percent of $12 = $0.36; bike maintenance and tire wear allocated $0.50; packaging protection $0.30. Total variable cost: $1.16. Contribution margin: $12.00 − $1.16 = $10.84.

Fixed monthly costs: insurance $90, phone and software $40, bike loan payment $85, marketing $60. Total: $275.

Break-even orders per month (before paying herself): $275 ÷ $10.84 ≈ 26 orders.

If she wants to pay herself $20 an hour for 60 hours a month ($1,200), she needs ($275 + $1,200) ÷ $10.84 ≈ 136 orders a month, about 7 per weekday. Her demand estimate (about 30 orders a month) shows a large gap, which reinforces her plan to add adjacent services, such as same-day document delivery for law offices and real estate agents, or to bundle print delivery into a partnership with shops.
:::

# Chapter 5: Permissions, Taxes, and Insurance

:::objectives
- Identify the main categories of permission a city business may need.
- Know which offices typically answer each question.
- Understand basic self-employment tax obligations.
- Assess insurance needs and downside risks.
:::

Before taking money from customers, find out what permissions apply. The SBA explains that the licenses and permits a business needs depend on its activity and location, and that requirements can come from federal, state, and local governments [8].

## Categories of permission

| Category | Typical question | Who usually answers |
|---|---|---|
| Business registration | Do I need to register a business name or entity? | State business filing office; city or county clerk |
| General business license | Does my city require a business license? | City or county licensing office |
| Zoning and home occupation | Can I operate from my home or this location? | City planning or zoning department |
| Activity-specific permits | Does my activity need a special permit (food, vending, transport)? | Relevant city or state agency |
| Sales tax | Must I collect sales tax on what I sell? | State tax or revenue department |
| Street and sidewalk use | Can I load, park, or sell on the street or sidewalk? | City transportation department |
| Employer obligations | What do I need if I hire? | State labor department; IRS |

## Taxes for the self-employed

If you operate as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC and earn net profit, you will generally owe income tax and **self-employment tax**. The IRS states that the self-employment tax rate is 15.3 percent, consisting of 12.4 percent for Social Security and 2.9 percent for Medicare, and that you may deduct the employer-equivalent portion when calculating adjusted gross income [9]. Self-employed people usually make estimated tax payments during the year. Work with a tax professional to understand your obligations.

:::example Illustrative: setting aside for taxes
Maya's test month produces $1,800 in revenue and $500 in expenses, for $1,300 net profit. Self-employment tax applies to 92.35 percent of net earnings (an adjustment shown on Schedule SE): $1,300 × 0.9235 × 0.153 ≈ $184. If her income tax rate on additional earnings is about 12 percent, income tax might add roughly $140 (before considering deductions). A simple rule of thumb many owners use is to set aside 25–30 percent of net profit for taxes; on $1,300 that is $325–$390. This is an estimate for planning, not tax advice.
:::

## Insurance and downside

Ask an insurance professional about:

- **General liability**: injury or damage to others caused by your business.
- **Commercial auto or bicycle coverage**: personal policies may exclude business use.
- **Inland marine or goods-in-transit coverage**: damage to items you carry.
- **Workers' compensation**: usually required when you have employees, rules vary by state.

Also list the downside: what happens if a delivery is lost, a rider is injured, or a customer disputes a charge? Decide in advance how you will respond, and put limits in your terms.

## Working with helpers: employees and contractors

As a business grows, the owner may bring in help. Whether a worker is an **employee** or an **independent contractor** is a legal question with tax and liability consequences. The IRS groups the evidence into three categories: behavioral control (whether the business controls what the worker does and how), financial control (how the worker is paid, who provides tools, whether expenses are reimbursed), and the type of relationship (written contracts, benefits, and permanence) [27]. State labor departments may apply different or stricter tests. Misclassifying an employee as a contractor can lead to back taxes, penalties, and wage claims. Before Maya asks a friend to cover deliveries on busy afternoons, she should ask an SBDC adviser or accountant how to structure the arrangement in her state, and confirm with her insurer that the helper is covered.

## Free help

The SBA's network of resource partners, including Small Business Development Centers (SBDCs), SCORE mentors, and Women's Business Centers, offers free or low-cost counseling and training [14]. A one-hour session with an SBDC adviser can save many hours of searching.

# Chapter 6: Startup Costs and Responsible Funding

:::objectives
- Estimate one-time and monthly startup costs.
- Compare funding sources, including savings, microloans, and CDFIs.
- Recognize the risks of personal guarantees and family money.
- Choose a funding level that matches the test.
:::

The SBA suggests calculating startup costs by identifying the expenses needed to open and operate, and separating one-time costs from ongoing ones [10]. For a test, the goal is to spend as little as possible while still delivering a complete, reliable service.

## Startup cost categories

| Category | Maya's estimate (illustrative) | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Used cargo bike with lock and lights | $1,800 | One-time |
| Weatherproof cargo box and padding | $250 | One-time |
| Helmet, reflective gear, rain gear | $180 | One-time |
| Phone mount and spare battery | $60 | One-time |
| Business registration and license | $150 | One-time (varies) |
| Insurance (first month) | $90 | Monthly |
| Phone plan share and booking software | $40 | Monthly |
| Simple website and printed cards | $120 | One-time |
| Repair and contingency reserve | $300 | One-time |
| Total one-time | $2,860 | |
| Total monthly | $130 | |

## Funding sources

- **Personal savings**: no repayment or interest, but money at risk.
- **Revenue**: pre-sales or deposits from committed customers.
- **Microloans**: the SBA's microloan program provides loans of up to $50,000 through intermediary lenders; the SBA says the average microloan is about $13,000 and the maximum repayment term is seven years [11].
- **CDFIs**: community development financial institutions are mission-driven lenders certified by the Treasury's CDFI Fund, including credit unions, banks, and loan funds that serve low-income communities [12]. Many provide training along with small loans [13].
- **Friends and family**: flexible but carry relationship risk; document terms in writing.
- **Credit cards**: fast but often expensive; use cautiously.

## Preparing for a lender conversation

Community lenders, including SBA microloan intermediaries and CDFIs, often evaluate the person and plan as well as the numbers, and many provide coaching alongside the loan. Arrive prepared with:

- A one-page description of the business and your minimum testable offer.
- Your startup budget, separating one-time and monthly costs.
- Evidence of demand: interview tallies, test results, partner letters.
- A simple 12-month cash projection with conservative and expected cases.
- Your personal financial picture, including existing debts.
- A clear statement of how much you need, what it buys, and how it will be repaid.

Ask the lender about the interest rate, fees, term, collateral, personal guarantees, and what happens if a payment is late. Compare offers by total cost over the life of the loan, not monthly payment alone.

:::case Daymond John and FUBU: the risk behind a famous start
Daymond John, founder of the apparel brand FUBU and later a Shark Tank investor, started by sewing hats in his mother's home in Hollis, Queens. His mother mortgaged the house to raise $100,000 and allowed part of it to be used as a makeshift factory [16]. As CNBC recounted, after FUBU won a $300,000 order and 27 banks turned him down, his mother took out the equity line; the money dwindled to $500, the mortgage was months behind, and John faced losing the house before a newspaper advertisement led to a financing deal with Samsung's textile division [15]. John has said his lack of financial knowledge nearly ended the business [15].

FUBU's later success is John's own, not TTI's, and not typical. The cautionary point is the one John himself emphasizes: borrowing against a family home exposes the family to losing it. A small test funded by savings, revenue, or a right-sized microloan limits the downside if the idea does not work.
:::

:::example Illustrative: comparing funding for $3,000
Option A: savings of $3,000. Cost: no interest; opportunity cost of the money.
Option B: a microloan of $3,000 at 10 percent annual interest over 24 months. Monthly payment ≈ $138; total repaid ≈ $3,322; interest ≈ $322.
Option C: a credit card at 24 percent APR paid over 24 months. Monthly payment ≈ $159; total repaid ≈ $3,806; interest ≈ $806.

If the test fails after three months, Option A loses up to $3,000 of savings but leaves no debt. Option B leaves about $2,650 of principal owed, and Option C about $2,700, each still accruing interest. Maya chooses to fund most of her test from savings and revenue, and to approach a CDFI lender for a small equipment loan only if the test shows demand. Rates are illustrative; actual loan terms vary.
:::

:::checklist Funding decisions
- I have listed one-time and monthly costs separately.
- I know the smallest amount needed for a complete test.
- I have compared at least two funding sources, including interest and total repayment.
- I understand any personal guarantee or collateral involved.
- Any money from friends or family is documented in writing.
- I know how much I can afford to lose if the test fails.
:::

:::summary
Separate one-time from monthly costs and fund only what a complete test requires. Funding sources include savings, revenue, SBA microloans of up to $50,000 through intermediaries, CDFI lenders, family money, and credit cards. Daymond John's story shows both how family capital can launch a business and how close it can come to losing a home; limit your downside.
:::

# Chapter 7: Operations and Trust at Every Handoff

:::objectives
- Map a service process from request to completion.
- Define chain of custody and proof of delivery.
- Write customer terms that set expectations clearly.
- Handle problems consistently.
:::

In a service business, operations are the product. Customers judge you by whether things happen as promised, every time.

## Process mapping

A **process map** lists every step in the order it happens and who is responsible. Maya's map:

1. Customer places request online or by text before 1 p.m.
2. Maya confirms the request and the print shop's ready time.
3. Maya picks up at the shop and checks the order against the request (quantity, name).
4. Maya photographs the sealed package.
5. Maya delivers between 2 and 5 p.m. to the named recipient.
6. Maya photographs the handoff location (not the person) and sends confirmation.
7. Payment is confirmed (prepaid).
8. Maya logs the delivery and any issue.

## Chain of custody and proof of delivery

**Chain of custody** is the documented record of who held an item from pickup to delivery. **Proof of delivery** is evidence that the item reached the right person or place. Together they protect the customer, the partner shop, and the business.

| Step | Record | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Pickup | Time, shop staff initials, photo of sealed package | Shows condition at pickup |
| Transit | Route log (optional) | Supports timing questions |
| Delivery | Time, recipient name, photo of location | Confirms completion |
| Exception | Note and photo of any damage or failed delivery | Supports fair resolution |

## Customer terms

Clear terms prevent disputes. Include price, payment, service area and hours, accepted items, what happens if no one is available to receive, liability limits, cancellation policy, and how to report problems.

## Privacy and sensitive items

Some deliveries carry information: printed materials may include customers' names, addresses, or confidential business details, and documents for law or real estate offices may be highly sensitive. Treat every package as private. Keep packages sealed, never open or photograph contents, keep the cargo box closed and locked between stops, and never leave items unattended in public. Photos for proof of delivery should show the package and location, not people or the contents. Delete delivery photos after a set period (for example, 60 days) unless a dispute is open. If a package is lost or misdelivered, notify the customer and partner immediately. These practices protect customers and make the business trustworthy to higher-value clients later.

:::example Illustrative: measuring reliability
Over a four-week test, Maya completes 64 deliveries. Eight arrive after 5 p.m. (12.5 percent late), and two have problems (one wrong address, one damaged corner). On-time rate: 56 ÷ 64 = 87.5 percent. Problem rate: 2 ÷ 64 ≈ 3.1 percent.

She reviews the late deliveries: six occurred when shops released orders after 2:30 p.m. She changes the process to confirm ready times by 1:30 p.m. and to schedule late-ready orders first. The next four weeks: 70 deliveries, 3 late (4.3 percent). The improvement is the result of a process change tracked with simple numbers.
:::

# Chapter 8: Safety and Respect for Public Space

:::objectives
- Identify safety risks for workers and the public in urban operations.
- Plan routes and schedules that reduce conflict.
- Follow local rules for streets, curbs, and sidewalks.
- Build a simple safety routine.
:::

An urban business shares streets, sidewalks, and curbs with everyone else. Operating safely and considerately is both an ethical obligation and a practical requirement: complaints and incidents can end a small business quickly.

## Safety risks

- Traffic collisions for riders or drivers.
- Pedestrian conflicts on sidewalks and at loading points.
- Theft of equipment or goods.
- Weather and visibility hazards.
- Lifting and carrying injuries.
- Fatigue during long shifts.

## Lessons from a city program

New York City says businesses using commercial cargo bikes for deliveries must review the city's Cargo Bike Training Guide to properly train the cyclists they employ, and allows commercial cargo bikes to load and unload in commercial loading zones and at designated cargo bike corrals [17]. The FHWA case study on the city's pilot noted that participants had to attend safety training and follow rules such as wearing reflective vests, and reported no recorded crashes among participating cargo bikes since the program began [2]. Your city may have different rules; check with your transportation department.

## Route and schedule planning

| Principle | Practice |
|---|---|
| Avoid peak conflict | Schedule deliveries outside rush hours where possible |
| Use legal stopping places | Loading zones, corrals, or side streets, not bike lanes or crosswalks |
| Keep sidewalks clear | Unload quickly; never block entrances or ramps |
| Plan for weather | Rain gear, slower speeds, fewer stops per hour |
| Limit load | Carry within the bike's rated capacity |

:::example Illustrative: time cost of safe routing
Maya's fastest route uses a busy avenue and takes 18 minutes. A quieter parallel street adds 4 minutes (22 minutes). Over 6 deliveries a day, the safer route adds 24 minutes. At her target pay of $20 an hour, that time is worth about $8 a day. She judges that the reduced collision risk, lower stress, and fewer conflicts with pedestrians and drivers are worth far more than $8, especially because one injury could end the business. Safety is a cost, but it is a small one compared with the downside.
:::

:::checklist Daily safety routine
- Bike checked: brakes, tires, lights, lock.
- Reflective gear and helmet on.
- Weather checked and route adjusted.
- Load within rated capacity and secured.
- Stopping places planned for each delivery.
- Phone mounted; no handheld use while riding.
- Incident report form available.
:::

:::summary
Urban operations carry risks for workers and the public. Follow local rules for streets, curbs, and sidewalks; New York City, for example, directs businesses to its Cargo Bike Training Guide and provides loading spaces. Plan routes and schedules to reduce conflict, and adopt a daily safety routine. The time cost of safety is small compared with the cost of an incident.
:::

# Chapter 9: Partnerships and Community

:::objectives
- Identify partners who share your customers.
- Structure a simple, fair partnership agreement.
- Understand how community-rooted businesses create value.
- Learn from a social enterprise built around its neighborhood.
:::

Urban businesses rarely succeed alone. Partners can provide customers, credibility, space, and supplies. For Maya, the four print shops in her corridor are the most important partners: they hold the orders her customers need delivered.

## Types of partners

- **Channel partners** who refer customers (print shops recommending Maya's delivery at checkout).
- **Complementary businesses** whose customers need your service (law offices, real estate agents, restaurants needing menus).
- **Community organizations** such as business improvement districts, merchant associations, and chambers of commerce.
- **Resource partners** such as SBDCs, SCORE, and CDFIs (Chapters 5 and 6).

## A simple partnership structure

| Element | Questions to agree on |
|---|---|
| Roles | Who tells the customer about the service? Who handles payment? |
| Economics | Is there a referral fee, a discount, or no money exchanged? |
| Standards | What are pickup times, packaging, and handoff procedures? |
| Communication | How are problems reported, and how fast? |
| Term | How long is the trial, and how can either side end it? |
| Branding | May each side use the other's name, and how? |

Never imply a partnership that has not been agreed. Put the essentials in writing, even in a short email both sides confirm.

## Working with merchant associations

Many urban corridors have a merchant association, chamber of commerce, or business improvement district (BID), an organization in which property owners or businesses fund shared services such as cleaning, events, and marketing. These groups know the corridor's businesses, hear their complaints, and often welcome services that help members. Attend a meeting, introduce your test briefly, and ask what problems members face. A short mention in a newsletter or a table at a street event can reach dozens of potential customers at once. In return, contribute: share what you learn about deliveries and curb use, show up reliably, and support other members' events.

:::case Greyston Bakery: a business rooted in its neighborhood
Greyston Bakery was founded in 1982 by Bernie Glassman to employ people facing barriers to work, using what it calls Open Hiring: no interviews, background checks, or résumés; applicants join a waiting list, and positions are offered first-come, first-served [18]. The bakery moved to Yonkers, New York, and later began supplying brownies to Ben & Jerry's after Glassman met co-founder Ben Cohen in 1987 [20]. A case study published by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services' Pathways to Work project reported data provided by Greyston for 2019: 67 employees, 40 new Open Hire employees hired that year, an average Open Hire tenure of 3.6 years, and $2.3 million in wages paid to Open Hire employees; the study noted that no formal evaluation of the model had been conducted [19].

Greyston's figures are its own, not TTI's, and its model reflects a specific mission, history, and anchor customer. The lesson for urban entrepreneurs is that deep roots in a neighborhood can be a strategy, not just a value. A long-term partner (Ben & Jerry's) provided steady demand, and a distinctive local commitment gave the business an identity customers and partners recognized.
:::

:::example Illustrative: value of a channel partner
Without partners, Maya acquires customers by visiting businesses and handing out cards: about 2 new customers per 10 hours of outreach. With one print shop mentioning her service at checkout, she receives about 3 referred customers a week with no outreach time.

If each customer orders about once a month and stays about 6 months, each is worth 6 × $10.84 contribution = about $65. Three referred customers a week over 4 weeks is 12 customers, worth about $780 in contribution over their lifetime. If she offers the shop $1 per delivered referred order as a thank-you, the cost is 12 × 6 × $1 = $72, leaving about $708. The partnership pays for itself many times over, if the referrals materialize. Track them to find out.
:::

# Chapter 10: Local Marketing That Builds Trust

:::objectives
- Choose marketing channels suited to a dense neighborhood.
- Write clear, truthful messages.
- Comply with basic rules on email and reviews.
- Track which channels produce customers.
:::

In a city corridor, marketing is often personal and physical: conversations, visible signage, word of mouth, and partner referrals. Digital channels support these but rarely replace them.

## Channels for neighborhood businesses

| Channel | Strengths | Watch-outs |
|---|---|---|
| In-person visits | Builds relationships; immediate feedback | Time-intensive; respect business hours |
| Partner referrals | Trusted; low cost | Depends on partner effort |
| Printed cards and flyers | Tangible; local | Follow posting rules; avoid litter |
| Map listings and simple website | Discoverable when people search | Keep hours and area accurate |
| Email updates | Direct to interested customers | Must comply with CAN-SPAM |
| Social media | Shows the work and the neighborhood | Consistency takes time |
| Merchant association events | Concentrated audience | May have membership costs |

## Truthful messages

State what you do, where, when, and for how much. Avoid claims you cannot support, such as "fastest in the city." Do not use a partner's name or logo without permission.

## Accessible and inclusive marketing

Neighborhoods are diverse. Make information easy to read: plain language, legible type, and good contrast on printed materials. Offer more than one way to reach you, such as text, phone, and a simple web form, so customers can use what works for them. Where many residents speak another language, consider translated materials reviewed by a fluent speaker. Describe your service area and hours precisely so no one is surprised. Inclusive marketing is not only fair; it widens the customer base in a dense, varied market.

## Email and reviews

Commercial email must meet the CAN-SPAM Act's requirements, including accurate header and subject lines, a valid physical postal address, and a clear way to opt out, honored within 10 business days [21]. The FTC's 2024 rule on consumer reviews prohibits fake reviews, buying positive reviews, and certain review-suppression practices [22]. Ask satisfied customers for honest reviews, and never offer rewards conditioned on positive sentiment.

:::example Illustrative: channel tracking
Maya asks every new customer, "How did you hear about us?" After eight weeks:

| Channel | Hours spent | New customers | Customers per hour |
|---|---|---|---|
| In-person visits | 20 | 8 | 0.40 |
| Print shop referrals | 2 | 11 | 5.50 |
| Flyers | 6 | 2 | 0.33 |
| Map listing | 1 | 3 | 3.00 |

Partner referrals and the map listing produce the most customers per hour. Maya shifts time from flyers to strengthening partner relationships and keeping her listing accurate. Small numbers can mislead, so she continues tracking for another eight weeks before making firm conclusions.
:::

:::checklist Local marketing
- My message states what, where, when, and price.
- I have permission to use any partner names.
- My listing shows accurate hours and service area.
- My emails include a postal address and opt-out.
- I request honest reviews and never buy or fake them.
- I ask every new customer how they heard about me.
:::

:::summary
Neighborhood marketing relies on personal visits, partner referrals, accurate listings, and word of mouth, supported by email and social media. Messages should be specific and truthful. Comply with CAN-SPAM and the FTC's rule on reviews. Track customers per hour by channel and shift effort to what works.
:::

# Chapter 11: Records, Cash, and Metrics

:::objectives
- Keep the records the IRS expects of a small business.
- Separate business and personal finances.
- Run a weekly cash review.
- Choose a few metrics that guide decisions.
:::

Good records are required by law and are the foundation of good decisions. The IRS explains that small businesses should keep records that identify the source of receipts, support items reported on tax returns, and document expenses, and that the business should choose a recordkeeping system that clearly shows income and expenses [23].

## Record basics

- Open a separate bank account for the business.
- Record every sale and expense, with receipts or invoices.
- Keep a mileage or equipment log if relevant.
- Store records securely and back them up.
- Keep records for as long as the IRS requires for your situation.

## Weekly cash review

Fifteen minutes a week answers three questions: How much cash do I have? What is coming in and going out in the next four weeks? Am I on track with my plan?

| Item | This week | Next 4 weeks (expected) |
|---|---|---|
| Starting cash | | |
| Cash in (sales) | | |
| Cash out (expenses) | | |
| Loan payments | | |
| Tax set-aside | | |
| Ending cash | | |

## Metrics that matter

Choose a handful of metrics tied to your decisions:

- Orders per week (demand).
- Contribution margin per order (unit economics).
- On-time rate and problem rate (reliability).
- Repeat customer rate (value).
- Customers per hour by channel (marketing efficiency).
- Revenue per working hour (owner economics).

## Reviewing price

Prices set at launch are guesses. After several weeks of data, review them. Signals that a price may be too low include near-universal acceptance without hesitation, demand exceeding capacity, and earnings per hour well below target despite efficient operations. Signals that a price may be too high include frequent objections, low repeat use, and customers choosing alternatives. When raising prices, give existing customers notice, explain what they receive, and consider keeping a lower rate for early customers for a period. A small price change can matter a great deal: at Maya's volume, raising the price from $12 to $14 adds $2 of contribution per order, an 18 percent increase in contribution margin, with little change in her costs.

:::example Illustrative: revenue per working hour
In week 6, Maya completes 22 orders at $12 (revenue $264) and works 14 hours including pickups, deliveries, and administration. Revenue per hour: $264 ÷ 14 ≈ $18.86. Contribution per hour: (22 × $10.84) ÷ 14 ≈ $17.03. After allocating weekly fixed costs of about $64 ($275 ÷ 4.3 weeks), her earnings are about ($238.48 − $64) ÷ 14 ≈ $12.46 an hour before taxes. This is below her $20 target. The metric tells her what must change: more orders per hour (denser routes, batching pickups), a higher price, or additional services with better margins.
:::

# Chapter 12: Test, Decide, and Grow

:::objectives
- Design a time-limited test with decision criteria set in advance.
- Make a stop, change, or grow decision using evidence.
- Document processes so others can help.
- Plan the next stage responsibly.
:::

A **test** is a deliberate, time-limited trial of your minimum testable offer, with success criteria written before it begins. Setting criteria in advance protects you from two common errors: quitting too early because of a slow week, and continuing too long because you are emotionally invested.

## Designing the test

1. **Duration**: long enough to see patterns, typically 4–8 weeks.
2. **Offer**: your minimum testable offer, unchanged during the test except for clear process fixes.
3. **Criteria**: specific thresholds for continuing, changing, or stopping.
4. **Budget**: the maximum money and time you will spend.
5. **Review date**: when you will decide.

| Outcome | Example criterion | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Strong | ≥ 25 orders/week by week 6, on-time ≥ 95%, repeat ≥ 40% | Grow: add hours, partners, or services |
| Mixed | 10–24 orders/week, or reliability below target | Change: adjust offer, price, or area; retest |
| Weak | < 10 orders/week with little repeat use | Stop or pivot to a different promise |

:::example Illustrative: Maya's decision
After eight weeks, Maya averages 18 orders a week, with a 96 percent on-time rate and 45 percent repeat customers. Demand is in the "mixed" range, but reliability and repeat use are strong. Her earnings per hour are below target.

Her decision: change, not stop. She adds a second service, same-day document delivery for three law offices and two real estate offices in the corridor, at $18 per delivery, and tests it for six weeks. If documents add 12 orders a week, her total becomes 30 orders, with weekly revenue of 18 × $12 + 12 × $18 = $432, within the "strong" range. The numbers are illustrative; the discipline of deciding by pre-set criteria is the point.
:::

## Documenting to grow

When a business grows, the founder becomes a bottleneck. Entrepreneur and author Dan Martell, in Buy Back Your Time, describes a "Camcorder Method": record yourself doing a task, turn the recording into a short checklist, and hand both to the person taking it over [24][25]. He also suggests a "Buyback Rate," calculated as annual income divided by 2,000 hours and then by 4, as a guide to which tasks to delegate [25]. These are Martell's frameworks, and his results are his own, not TTI's. For Maya, recording her pickup and handoff routine would let a second rider follow the same standards on busy days.

:::case Starting small and expanding with data
The New York City Department of Transportation began its cargo bike program as a small pilot with three participants and 100 bikes, collected trip data from participants, and expanded into Brooklyn and then made the program permanent once evaluation showed results [1][2]. In 2025 it added curbside microhubs, where goods move from trucks to handcarts and cargo bikes; in the first year the city reported that the microhubs helped replace more than 3,000 truck trips [26]. These are the city's results, not TTI's. For a small business, the same sequence (pilot, measure, expand where evidence is strongest) is a practical growth model.
:::

## Paths to growth

If the test succeeds, there are several ways to grow, each with different demands:

| Growth path | Example for Maya | Main requirement |
|---|---|---|
| More volume in the same corridor | More businesses ordering more often | Marketing and partner depth |
| Adjacent services | Document delivery, small parcel delivery | New terms, insurance check |
| Adjacent corridors | A second 20-block area | A second rider or longer hours |
| Added capacity | Second bike and part-time rider | Hiring, training, documented processes |
| Partnerships | Formal agreement with print shops | Written terms, shared standards |
| Physical presence | A shared micro-depot or storefront | Lease, zoning, higher fixed costs |

Grow one dimension at a time and re-run a test for each. Adding services, area, and staff all at once makes it impossible to know what is working.

## A 90-day plan

| Days | Focus | Milestones |
|---|---|---|
| 1–30 | Prepare | Promise written; research done; permits and insurance confirmed; costs funded; process and safety routine documented |
| 31–60 | Test | Minimum testable offer live; weekly metrics and cash review; problems logged and fixed |
| 61–90 | Decide | Results compared with criteria; stop, change, or grow decision made; next test designed |

# Glossary

**CDFI**: Community development financial institution; a mission-driven lender certified by the Treasury's CDFI Fund.

**Chain of custody**: The documented record of who held an item from pickup to delivery.

**Contribution margin**: Price minus variable costs per unit.

**Corridor**: A street or connected set of blocks with mixed activity.

**Density**: The number of people, households, or businesses in an area.

**Microloan**: A small loan, such as SBA microloans of up to $50,000 made through intermediaries.

**Minimum testable offer**: The smallest complete version of a service real customers can buy.

**Primary research**: Information you collect yourself through observation, interviews, or tests.

**Process map**: An ordered list of steps and owners in a workflow.

**Promise**: The specific result a customer can expect, including who, what, where, when, and terms.

**Proof of delivery**: Evidence that an item reached the correct person or place.

**SBDC**: Small Business Development Center, an SBA resource partner offering counseling and training.

**Secondary research**: Information collected by others, such as census or directory data.

**Self-employment tax**: Social Security and Medicare tax for self-employed individuals, 15.3 percent.

**Test**: A time-limited trial with success criteria set in advance.
