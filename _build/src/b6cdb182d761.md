# How to Use This Book

Real estate investing has always rewarded people who know more about a property than the person across the table. Property technology, usually shortened to "PropTech," promises to close that gap: instant valuations, rent estimates, neighborhood scores, risk maps, automated tenant screening, pricing software, and online platforms that let small investors buy fractions of large buildings. Some of these tools are genuinely useful. All of them are models of reality, not reality itself, and every model has error.

This book teaches you to use PropTech the way a careful analyst does: as a starting point that must be checked against primary records, professional inspection, and your own arithmetic. It is written for new and early-stage residential investors, small landlords evaluating software, and professionals such as agents, lenders, and property managers who want a structured way to explain these tools to clients.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Write a property brief with explicit stop rules before you look at any listing.
- Build a source hierarchy that ranks public records above platform data, and reconcile conflicting facts.
- Interpret automated valuation models (AVMs) using their published error rates, and check them against comparable sales.
- Explain why algorithmic home-buying businesses such as Zillow Offers struggled, and apply those lessons to your own pricing.
- Model rental cash flow with visible arithmetic, then stress-test it for vacancy, repairs, interest rates, and insurance.
- Evaluate tenant screening and rent-pricing software for fair housing and antitrust risk.
- Protect a transaction against wire fraud.
- Read the disclosures on real estate crowdfunding and REIT platforms.
- Assemble a due diligence packet that an independent professional can review.

# Chapter 1: The Investor's Evidence Mindset and the Property Brief

:::objectives
- Define PropTech and identify the main categories of tools an investor encounters.
- Explain the difference between data, estimates, and evidence.
- Write a property brief that states purpose, non-negotiables, evidence rules, and stop rules.
- Recognize common cognitive traps in property evaluation.
:::

## What PropTech Is

PropTech is software and data used to find, value, finance, buy, manage, or sell real estate. For a residential investor, the categories that matter most are:

| Category | Examples of what it does | Primary risk |
|---|---|---|
| Listing and search portals | Aggregate listings, photos, price history | Incomplete or stale data |
| Automated valuation models | Estimate market value from data | Estimate error, especially off-market |
| Rent estimators | Estimate market rent | Thin comparables, unit differences |
| Mapping and risk overlays | Flood zones, schools, crime, climate | Map is not a forecast |
| Underwriting calculators | Cash flow, returns, loan sizing | Garbage-in, garbage-out |
| Property management software | Leasing, rent collection, maintenance | Privacy, fair housing |
| Tenant screening | Credit, eviction, criminal checks, scores | Discrimination, inaccurate records |
| Pricing software | Recommends rents | Antitrust risk if using competitors' data |
| Transaction platforms | E-signature, wire transfers, closing | Wire fraud |
| Investment platforms | Crowdfunding, REITs, fractional shares | Illiquidity, fees, sponsor risk |

## Data, Estimates, and Evidence

Three words are often confused.

**Data** are recorded facts, such as a recorded sale price or the square footage on a tax roll. Data can still be wrong; tax rolls contain errors.

**Estimates** are outputs of models, such as an automated valuation or a rent estimate. An estimate is a probabilistic statement with a margin of error, even when displayed as a single number.

**Evidence** is information you have verified from a source appropriate to the decision: a recorded deed, a permit file, an appraisal, a professional inspection report, a signed lease, a written repair quote.

The disciplined investor uses estimates to decide what to investigate, uses data to narrow the field, and relies on evidence to commit money.

## The Property Brief

A property brief is a one-page document written before you look at listings. It protects you from the most expensive mistake in investing: falling in love with a property and then rationalizing the numbers. It has five parts.

1. **Purpose.** One sentence describing what this property must do, for example: "Provide positive monthly cash flow after all expenses and reserves, as a long-term rental, within 30 minutes of my home."
2. **Non-negotiables.** Measurable requirements, such as a confirmed legal unit count, a maximum purchase price, a maximum renovation budget you can fund from cash, and acceptable flood zones.
3. **Evidence rules.** Which facts require which proof. For example: unit count requires the assessor record plus the permit history; rent requires three comparable signed leases or listings within one mile; condition requires a licensed inspection.
4. **Pause rules.** Findings that require a named check before continuing, such as an unpermitted addition or a flood zone change.
5. **Stop rules.** Findings that end consideration, such as negative cash flow under the base case, title problems you cannot resolve, or a seller who refuses inspection.

Date the brief. Change it only when your goals change, not because a particular property tempts you.

## Cognitive Traps

| Trap | What it looks like | Defense |
|---|---|---|
| Anchoring | The first number you see (list price or AVM) shapes every later judgment | Build your own value estimate before looking at the AVM |
| Confirmation bias | Seeking data that support a property you like | Write disconfirming questions in the brief |
| Precision illusion | Treating a single-number estimate as exact | Always ask for the error range |
| Sunk cost | Continuing because you already paid for an inspection | Stop rules decide, not money spent |
| Recency bias | Assuming recent price trends continue | Stress-test with flat and falling prices |

:::example Writing a Property Brief (illustrative)
Leah, an invented nurse with $70,000 saved, wants her first rental. Her brief reads:

Purpose: a single-family or duplex long-term rental producing at least $150 per month after all expenses and reserves under the base case, within 25 minutes of her home.

Non-negotiables: purchase price no more than $285,000; renovation budget no more than $15,000 from cash; legal unit count confirmed by the county; not in a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area unless flood insurance is priced into the base case.

Evidence rules: value from at least four comparable closed sales within 12 months and one mile; rent from at least three comparable leases or listings; condition from a licensed inspection plus a sewer-line scope for homes built before 1980.

Pause rules: any unpermitted space; any roof older than 15 years; HOA with pending special assessment.

Stop rules: negative cash flow in the base case; title issues not curable before closing; seller refuses inspection.

With this brief, Leah can screen dozens of listings quickly and without emotion.
:::

# Chapter 2: Public Records and the Source Hierarchy

:::objectives
- Identify the public offices that hold authoritative property records.
- Rank sources by reliability for different facts.
- Reconcile conflicts between platform data and official records.
- Build a property fact sheet that cites the source and date of every fact.
:::

Listing portals present property facts with great confidence, but those facts are usually copied from public records, multiple listing services, or user submissions, and errors propagate. The investor's job is to go upstream.

## The Main Record Holders

| Office | What it holds | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| County assessor | Parcel number, assessed value, land and building characteristics, owner of record | Parcel identity, tax basis, square footage (with caution) |
| County recorder or register of deeds | Deeds, mortgages, liens, easements | Ownership chain, liens, recorded sale prices |
| Tax collector or treasurer | Property tax bills and delinquencies | Exact tax amount, delinquency risk |
| Building or permit department | Permits, inspections, certificates of occupancy | Legal unit count, permitted additions |
| Planning or zoning department | Zoning designation, allowed uses, variances | Whether intended use is legal |
| Courts | Lawsuits, foreclosures, evictions, probate | Litigation risk |
| FEMA Flood Map Service Center | Flood Insurance Rate Maps | Flood zone designation [1] |
| HOA or condo association | Budgets, reserve studies, rules, special assessments | Fees, rental restrictions |

Most counties offer online search portals; where they do not, a phone call or visit is usually enough.

## The Source Hierarchy

Rank each fact by where it came from:

1. **Primary official records** (recorded deed, permit file, court docket).
2. **Professional reports** (appraisal, survey, inspection, title commitment).
3. **Contracted documents** (signed leases, HOA estoppel letters, rent rolls with bank deposits).
4. **Industry databases** (MLS records entered by licensed agents).
5. **Aggregator platforms** (consumer portals, data resellers).
6. **Seller or agent statements** without documents.

When sources conflict, the higher-ranked source generally governs, but a conflict is itself information. A listing that advertises three bedrooms when the assessor shows two may indicate an unpermitted conversion, which can affect insurance, financing, legal rentability, and value.

## Step-by-Step: The Property Fact Sheet

1. Copy the parcel number exactly, including punctuation, and note the county.
2. Pull the assessor record; note the date it was last updated.
3. Pull recorded documents for at least the last two transfers.
4. Request or search permit history; list every permit with status.
5. Confirm zoning and allowed uses for your intended strategy.
6. Check the flood zone on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center [1].
7. Search court records for the address and owner names.
8. Record every fact with its source and date; flag conflicts.

:::example Reconciling a Conflict (illustrative)
A listing for an invented duplex at 418 Elm Street advertises "2 units, 2,100 sq ft, 4 bed / 2 bath." The assessor shows a single-family residence of 1,450 square feet with three bedrooms and one bathroom. The permit file shows a 2009 permit for a bathroom addition, finaled, and no permit for a second unit.

Interpretation: the second "unit" is probably an unpermitted conversion of a garage or basement. The 650-square-foot difference likely reflects that space. The investor's brief requires a legal unit count confirmed by the county, so this triggers a pause rule: before proceeding, she must ask the planning department whether the unit can be legalized and at what cost. If not, she must underwrite the property as a single-family home with one rent, which may change the decision entirely.
:::

Platforms republish records; investors should go upstream to the assessor, recorder, tax collector, permit and planning offices, courts, FEMA, and the HOA. Rank sources by reliability and treat conflicts as signals. A property fact sheet that cites the source and date of every fact becomes the foundation of your due diligence packet.

# Chapter 3: Automated Valuation Models and Comparable Sales

:::objectives
- Explain what an automated valuation model is and how its accuracy is reported.
- Interpret median error rates correctly and apply them to a specific property.
- Build a comparable-sales analysis with explicit adjustments.
- Combine AVMs and comparable sales into a value range rather than a point estimate.
:::

## What an AVM Is

An automated valuation model estimates a property's market value using statistical or machine-learning methods applied to public records, listing data, and recent sales. Consumer AVMs include Zillow's Zestimate and similar tools from other portals and data firms. Lenders use professional AVMs for some decisions, but they remain estimates.

## Reading Accuracy Statistics

Zillow publishes accuracy figures for the Zestimate. It reports a nationwide median error rate of 1.80% for homes on the market and 7.21% for off-market homes, explaining that for listed homes the Zestimate is within 1.80% of the final sale price half the time, and within 7.21% half the time for off-market homes; it also notes that accuracy varies by location and property type [2].

Two lessons follow. First, a median error means half of estimates are further off than that figure. Second, the on-market figure benefits from the list price and listing details; for an off-market property, which is exactly where investors look for deals, the typical error is several times larger.

| Home value | 7.21% median off-market error | Range containing about half of estimates |
|---|---|---|
| $200,000 | about $14,400 | roughly $185,600 to $214,400 |
| $300,000 | about $21,600 | roughly $278,400 to $321,600 |
| $500,000 | about $36,050 | roughly $463,950 to $536,050 |

The other half of estimates fall outside those ranges, sometimes far outside. Figures are illustrative applications of the published median [2].

## Comparable Sales Analysis

Appraisers estimate value primarily by comparing the subject property with recent sales of similar properties and adjusting for differences. You can apply a simplified version.

1. **Select comparables.** Closed sales, ideally within the last six months and within half a mile to one mile, of similar type, size, age, and condition. Use at least four.
2. **Verify each sale.** Confirm the recorded price and date; exclude non-arm's-length transfers such as family sales and foreclosure auctions unless the subject is similar.
3. **Adjust for differences.** Add value to a comparable that is inferior to the subject, subtract value from one that is superior. Typical adjustments cover size, bedrooms and baths, condition, garage, lot, and location within the neighborhood.
4. **Reconcile.** Weight the comparables most similar to the subject most heavily.
5. **State a range.** Report a low, most likely, and high value.

:::example A Four-Comparable Analysis (illustrative)
Subject: invented 3-bed, 2-bath, 1,500 sq ft ranch, average condition, one-car garage. Local per-square-foot adjustment assumed at $80; second bathroom $8,000; condition difference $15,000; garage $6,000. (Adjustment amounts are illustrative; an appraiser derives them from market data.)

| Comp | Sale price | Differences vs. subject | Net adjustment | Adjusted price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | $268,000 | 100 sq ft larger | -$8,000 | $260,000 |
| B | $255,000 | 1 bath only | +$8,000 | $263,000 |
| C | $281,000 | Renovated condition | -$15,000 | $266,000 |
| D | $249,000 | No garage | +$6,000 | $255,000 |

Adjusted range: $255,000 to $266,000. Comps A and B need the smallest adjustments, so they carry the most weight; the investor sets a most-likely value of $261,000. The AVM shows $289,000. Because the subject is off-market, a difference of about 10.7% is not surprising given the published median error, but the investor relies on the comparable analysis and, before purchase, an appraisal.
:::

:::checklist AVM and Comparable Discipline
- I wrote my own comparable-based range before looking at the AVM.
- I know whether the property is on or off the market and the AVM's published error for that case.
- Each comparable is a verified, arm's-length, closed sale.
- I documented every adjustment and its basis.
- My conclusion is a range, not a single number.
- I will obtain a professional appraisal or broker opinion before committing.
:::

:::summary
AVMs are useful screening tools with published, meaningful error. Zillow reports median errors of 1.80% on-market and 7.21% off-market, so off-market estimates in particular should be treated as rough. Comparable sales analysis, with verified sales and explicit adjustments, produces a defensible value range that you then confirm through an appraisal.
:::

# Chapter 4: When Algorithms Buy Houses: Lessons From iBuying

:::objectives
- Describe the iBuying business model and why it depends on pricing accuracy.
- Analyze Zillow's 2021 wind-down of Zillow Offers using its own disclosures.
- Explain how small forecasting errors become large losses with leverage and holding time.
- Apply iBuying lessons to an individual investor's pricing and margin of safety.
:::

"iBuying" refers to companies that make fast, data-driven cash offers on homes, renovate lightly, and resell them, earning money from fees and resale spreads. The model is a live experiment in whether algorithms can price houses precisely enough to trade them profitably at scale. Its results are instructive for every investor who uses valuation software.

## How the Model Works

An iBuyer's economics depend on four numbers: the purchase price, the cost of repairs and holding (taxes, insurance, utilities, financing), selling costs, and the eventual resale price. If the algorithm overestimates future resale value by even a few percent, the thin margin disappears. The longer the holding period and the faster the market turns, the larger the exposure.

:::case Zillow Offers (2021)
On November 2, 2021, Zillow Group announced its third-quarter results and its plan to wind down Zillow Offers, the iBuying service in which it acted as the primary purchaser and seller of homes. Co-founder and CEO Rich Barton stated that the company had determined "the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated" and that continuing to scale would produce too much earnings and balance-sheet volatility. The wind-down was expected to include a workforce reduction of approximately 25% [3].

Zillow's release disclosed that third-quarter results included an inventory write-down of approximately $304 million in the Homes segment "as a result of purchasing homes in Q3 at higher prices than the company's current estimates of future selling prices," and that it expected an additional $240 million to $265 million of losses in the fourth quarter, primarily on homes it expected to purchase that quarter. The Homes segment reported a pre-tax loss of $422 million for the quarter [3]. In its related filing, Zillow cited home pricing unpredictability, capacity constraints, and operational challenges exacerbated by an unprecedented housing market, a global pandemic, and labor and supply-chain difficulties [4].

Lesson for the reader: Zillow had one of the largest housing datasets in the country and still found that forecasting resale prices months ahead was too uncertain for its model. An individual investor with less data should build wider margins of safety, not narrower ones.
:::

:::case Opendoor in the 2022 Market Transition
Opendoor, an iBuying company, reported a net loss of $928 million in the third quarter of 2022, compared with $57 million a year earlier, while its revenue rose 48% year over year, according to industry publication Inman's report on its earnings [5]. Inman also reported the example of a home Opendoor bought for $678,000 in August 2021 that eventually sold for $570,000, a 16% loss [6].

Lesson for the reader: revenue growth does not protect against pricing error when market direction changes. Inventory bought near a peak and sold after a turn produces losses regardless of operational skill.
:::

## Why Small Errors Become Large Losses

Consider the arithmetic of a thin-margin trade. Suppose a buyer targets a 5% gross spread between purchase and resale, and transaction plus holding costs total 4% of value. A resale estimate that is 3% too optimistic turns a 1% expected profit into a 2% loss. With borrowed money, the loss as a share of the investor's own cash is multiplied.

| Scenario | Resale vs. forecast | Gross spread | Costs | Net on value | Net on 20% equity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Forecast correct | 0% | 5% | 4% | +1% | +5% |
| 3% optimistic | -3% | 2% | 4% | -2% | -10% |
| 7% optimistic | -7% | -2% | 4% | -6% | -30% |

Figures are illustrative and simplified (ignoring financing costs within the equity column).

## Lessons for Individual Investors

1. **Use a margin of safety tied to model error.** If your value estimate could reasonably be off by 7% or more, your offer should leave room for that.
2. **Shorten exposure to price forecasts.** Strategies that depend on resale in a specific future window, such as flips, carry more forecast risk than long-term holds that cash flow from rent.
3. **Respect capacity constraints.** Zillow cited renovation and resale capacity [3]; individual investors face the same constraint with contractors and their own time.
4. **Watch market direction, not just level.** Rising inventory and price cuts are early signals.
5. **Avoid scaling a strategy before it is proven.** Test with one property, measure actual versus forecast, then decide.

:::example Building a Margin of Safety Into an Offer (illustrative)
An investor's comparable analysis suggests an after-repair value (ARV) of $320,000 for an invented property. Repairs are quoted at $35,000; holding costs for five months are estimated at $9,000; selling costs at 7% of ARV, $22,400. The investor wants a $25,000 profit target and a margin of safety equal to 7% of ARV ($22,400) to reflect valuation uncertainty.

Maximum offer = $320,000 - $35,000 - $9,000 - $22,400 - $25,000 - $22,400 = $206,200.

If the seller will not accept roughly $206,000, the investor passes. Without the safety margin, the investor might have offered $228,600 and been exposed to the kind of forecast error that hurt far larger buyers.
:::

# Chapter 5: Rent Estimates and Market Research

:::objectives
- Evaluate rent estimates from platforms against direct market evidence.
- Use HUD Fair Market Rent data appropriately as a reference point.
- Build a rent comparable analysis with unit-level adjustments.
- Estimate vacancy and turnover realistically.
:::

Rent is the engine of a buy-and-hold investment, and it is often estimated more casually than price. Many platforms produce rent estimates from listing data; their accuracy depends on how many truly comparable units exist nearby.

## Sources of Rent Information

| Source | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Platform rent estimates | Fast; broad coverage | Asking rents, not signed rents; unit features often missing |
| Active rental listings | Current market asking prices | May be overpriced; days-on-market matters |
| Signed leases from local property managers | Actual achieved rents | Requires relationships |
| HUD Fair Market Rents | Official, public, consistent methodology | Set at the 40th percentile; designed for voucher programs, not as a market forecast [7] |
| HUD Small Area Fair Market Rents | ZIP-code level detail [8] | Same methodological purpose |

HUD describes its Fair Market Rents as 40th-percentile rents, publishes them annually, and offers them through an API [7]. Small Area Fair Market Rents are calculated for ZIP codes and are required for setting voucher payment standards in designated areas [8]. For an investor, these figures are a useful sanity check and essential information if you plan to accept Housing Choice Vouchers, but they are not a substitute for local comparables.

## Step-by-Step: Rent Comparable Analysis

1. Identify at least three, ideally five, comparable units within one mile with the same bedroom count.
2. Record asking rent, days on market, square footage, condition, parking, laundry, utilities included, and pet policy.
3. Call or email listing agents to ask whether units rented and at what price.
4. Adjust for differences (for example, in-unit laundry or included utilities).
5. Set a conservative base-case rent near the middle of the adjusted range, not the top.

## Vacancy and Turnover

Vacancy is not just empty days; it includes turnover costs such as cleaning, painting, minor repairs, and leasing fees. Investors commonly underwrite a vacancy and credit-loss allowance as a percentage of gross rent, adjusted for local conditions and their own management capacity. A property that turns over every year will lose more to vacancy than one with long-term tenants.

:::example Rent Comparables (illustrative)
Subject: invented 2-bed, 1-bath, 900 sq ft apartment, shared laundry, tenant pays utilities.

| Comp | Asking rent | Days listed | Key difference | Adjustment | Adjusted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | $1,450 | 12 | In-unit laundry | -$50 | $1,400 |
| B | $1,395 | 30 | Same | $0 | $1,395 |
| C | $1,525 | 45 (still listed) | Renovated; likely overpriced | -$100 | $1,425 |
| D | $1,350 | 8 | Water included | +$40 | $1,390 |

Adjusted range: $1,390 to $1,425. Base-case rent: $1,400. The platform estimate shows $1,520, which appears to reflect asking rents of renovated units. The investor also checks the ZIP code's Small Area Fair Market Rent for two bedrooms as a reference.
:::

:::checklist Rent Evidence Checklist
- At least three comparable units with the same bedroom count.
- Days on market noted; stale listings discounted.
- At least one achieved (signed) rent confirmed by a manager or agent.
- HUD FMR or SAFMR checked as a reference.
- Utilities and amenities adjusted explicitly.
- Base-case rent set at the middle of the adjusted range.
- Local rent-control or registration rules checked.
:::

:::summary
Rent estimates from platforms typically reflect asking rents and may miss unit features. Use active listings, achieved rents from local managers, and HUD's 40th-percentile Fair Market Rents as cross-checks, and build an adjusted comparable range. Underwrite vacancy as an allowance that includes turnover costs.
:::

# Chapter 6: Underwriting Rental Cash Flow With Visible Arithmetic

:::objectives
- Build a rental pro forma from gross rent to cash flow.
- Calculate net operating income, capitalization rate, cash-on-cash return, and debt service coverage ratio.
- Identify commonly omitted expenses.
- Understand basic depreciation rules for residential rental property.
:::

Underwriting calculators make it easy to produce attractive numbers. The defense is to show every line of arithmetic so that you, a lender, or a partner can check it.

## The Pro Forma

| Line | Definition |
|---|---|
| Gross scheduled rent | Monthly market rent times 12 for all units |
| Less vacancy and credit loss | Allowance for empty months and unpaid rent |
| Plus other income | Laundry, parking, pet fees |
| Effective gross income (EGI) | Result of the above |
| Less operating expenses | Taxes, insurance, repairs, maintenance, management, utilities paid by owner, HOA, landscaping, legal and accounting, capital reserves |
| Net operating income (NOI) | EGI minus operating expenses (excludes mortgage payments) |
| Less debt service | Principal and interest |
| Cash flow before tax | NOI minus debt service |

## Key Ratios

**Capitalization rate** = NOI / purchase price. A property-level yield, independent of financing.

**Cash-on-cash return** = annual cash flow before tax / total cash invested (down payment, closing costs, initial repairs).

**Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR)** = NOI / annual debt service. Fannie Mae's multifamily guide, for example, defines DSCR as the ratio of net cash flow to principal, interest, and certain other required payments [9]. Lenders set minimum DSCRs; a ratio below 1.0 means the property's income does not cover its debt.

## Commonly Omitted Expenses

- **Capital reserves** for roofs, HVAC, water heaters, and appliances.
- **Property management**, even if you self-manage, because your time has value and you may later hire help.
- **Tax reassessment** after purchase, which can raise property taxes above the seller's bill.
- **Insurance increases**, including flood or wind coverage.
- **Turnover costs** beyond simple vacancy.

## Depreciation Basics

For U.S. federal tax purposes, IRS Publication 527 explains that residential rental property placed in service after 1986 is generally depreciated using the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System, and that under the General Depreciation System residential rental property is depreciated over 27.5 years [10]. Land is not depreciable. Depreciation can reduce taxable income but may be recaptured on sale; tax treatment depends on your situation, so consult a CPA.

:::example A Complete Pro Forma (illustrative)
Invented duplex, purchase price $300,000, two units at $1,400 each. Down payment 25% ($75,000), closing costs $9,000, initial repairs $6,000, total cash invested $90,000. Loan $225,000 at 7.0% for 30 years: monthly principal and interest about $1,497, annual $17,964.

| Line | Annual |
|---|---|
| Gross scheduled rent (2 x $1,400 x 12) | $33,600 |
| Vacancy and credit loss (6%) | -$2,016 |
| Effective gross income | $31,584 |
| Property taxes | -$3,900 |
| Insurance | -$1,800 |
| Repairs and maintenance (6% of rent) | -$2,016 |
| Capital reserves (5% of rent) | -$1,680 |
| Management (8% of EGI) | -$2,527 |
| Owner-paid water and trash | -$1,200 |
| Net operating income | $18,461 |
| Debt service | -$17,964 |
| Cash flow before tax | $497 |

Cap rate = $18,461 / $300,000 = 6.15%. Cash-on-cash = $497 / $90,000 = 0.55%. DSCR = $18,461 / $17,964 = 1.03. The property barely covers its debt. If the investor's brief requires $150 per month ($1,800 per year), this property fails the base case at this price.
:::

# Chapter 7: Stress Testing, Insurance, and Climate Risk

:::objectives
- Design stress tests for vacancy, repairs, interest rates, rent, and insurance.
- Read FEMA flood maps correctly and understand their limits.
- Understand flood insurance waiting periods.
- Set reserves that match the property's risk profile.
:::

A base case describes what you expect. A stress test describes what you can survive. Lenders, institutional investors, and careful individuals all stress-test because averages hide the bad years.

## Five Standard Stresses

| Stress | Typical test | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| Vacancy | Add two months of vacancy in a year | Whether reserves cover a long turnover |
| Major repair | One unplanned repair equal to 5% to 10% of price | Cash needed for a roof, sewer line, or HVAC |
| Rent decline | Reduce rent 5% to 10% | Sensitivity to local market softening |
| Interest rate | For adjustable or future refinance: add 2 percentage points | Refinance and payment shock |
| Insurance and taxes | Increase both 20% to 30% | Exposure to reassessment and insurance repricing |

Run each stress separately, then a combined "bad year" with two or three at once.

## Flood Risk and Maps

FEMA's Flood Map Service Center lets anyone search an address to view the effective Flood Insurance Rate Map for that location [1]. FEMA explains that flood zones are geographic areas defined according to varying levels of flood risk and type of flooding [11]. A map designation is a regulatory classification at a point in time, not a forecast of whether a particular house will flood. Properties outside high-risk zones can and do flood.

Two practical facts matter for investors. First, if a property is in a Special Flood Hazard Area and financed with a federally backed mortgage, flood insurance is generally required. Second, FloodSmart, the National Flood Insurance Program's consumer site, states that NFIP coverage generally takes effect 30 days after purchase, with exceptions, including when the purchase is tied to making, increasing, extending, or renewing a loan [12]. FEMA's myth-and-fact sheet confirms the same rule and exceptions [13]. Price flood insurance before you make an offer.

## Other Overlays

Platforms now display wildfire, heat, and climate-risk scores from private vendors. Treat them as prompts for questions: What does my insurance agent say? What do local building codes require? What did the inspection find about drainage, roofing, and defensible space? Scores are proprietary models; ask what data and methods underlie them.

## Setting Reserves

A common practitioner approach is to hold a dedicated cash reserve equal to several months of total expenses plus debt service, increased for older buildings, single-tenant properties, and properties with high insurance exposure. Write your reserve rule into your property brief.

:::example A Combined Stress Test (illustrative)
Using the Chapter 6 duplex: base NOI $18,461, debt service $17,964, base cash flow $497.

Bad year: two months of vacancy in one unit (-$2,800), a $9,000 sewer-line replacement, and a 25% insurance increase (-$450).

Stressed cash flow = $497 - $2,800 - $9,000 - $450 = -$11,753.

The investor would need at least about $11,800 in reserves after closing to survive this year without new borrowing. With $90,000 already invested and $20,000 in reserves, the property survives; with $5,000 in reserves, it does not. This result, not the base case, determines whether the deal fits the investor's finances.
:::

# Chapter 8: Tenant Screening Technology and Fair Housing

:::objectives
- Summarize the Fair Housing Act's application to screening technology.
- Describe HUD's 2024 guidance on tenant screening and AI.
- Learn from the SafeRent settlement.
- Build a written, consistent, and lawful screening policy.
:::

Tenant screening software bundles credit reports, eviction records, criminal history, income verification, and sometimes proprietary "scores." These tools can speed decisions, but the landlord remains responsible for compliance with fair housing law and with the Fair Credit Reporting Act when using consumer reports.

## HUD Guidance

On May 2, 2024, HUD announced Fair Housing Act guidance on the application of artificial intelligence, recommending best practices to prevent discriminatory uses of AI in tenant screening and in advertising of housing [14]. HUD's screening guidance explains how the Fair Housing Act protects rental applicants and how housing providers and screening companies can screen in a nondiscriminatory way, noting that an increasing number of screening companies claim to use machine learning and other AI [15]. Among its themes: screening criteria should be relevant to tenancy, applied consistently, transparent to applicants, and based on accurate records, and applicants should have a chance to dispute negative information.

:::case SafeRent Solutions Settlement (2024)
Rental applicants in Massachusetts reached a settlement of about $2.28 million with SafeRent Solutions, a tenant screening company formerly known as CoreLogic Rental Property Solutions, after alleging that its algorithmic screening disproportionately harmed Black and Hispanic applicants using federally funded housing vouchers, according to the National Law Journal [16]. The Verge reported that U.S. District Judge Angel Kelley gave final approval in November 2024 and that, under the settlement, SafeRent would no longer use AI-powered scores to evaluate applicants using housing vouchers [17].

Lesson for the reader: a landlord who relies on a vendor's score still makes the housing decision. Understand what a score measures, whether it is relevant to the applicant's ability to pay their share of rent, and whether it may screen out protected groups.
:::

## Building a Screening Policy

1. **Write criteria before advertising.** Income, rental history, and other requirements in writing.
2. **Make criteria relevant.** For voucher holders, consider the tenant's portion of rent, not the full rent, when assessing income.
3. **Apply them consistently.** Same criteria, same order, every applicant.
4. **Use individualized assessment.** Especially for criminal history, consider nature, recency, and relevance rather than blanket bans; check state and local rules.
5. **Give notice and a chance to respond.** If you deny based on a consumer report, provide the adverse action notice the FCRA requires and let applicants correct errors.
6. **Audit vendors.** Ask what data a score uses and whether it has been tested for disparate impact.
7. **Keep records.** Applications, criteria, decisions, and reasons.

| Practice | Lower risk | Higher risk |
|---|---|---|
| Income rule | Ratio based on tenant's share of rent | Fixed multiple of full rent for voucher holders |
| Criminal history | Individualized, recent, relevant | Blanket ban on any record |
| Eviction records | Verify outcome; ignore dismissed cases | Deny on any filing |
| Scores | Understand inputs; use as one factor | Automatic deny below cutoff |
| Communication | Written criteria provided upfront | Unstated or changing criteria |

:::example Applying a Written Policy (illustrative)
An invented landlord lists a unit at $1,400. Policy: tenant's portion of rent must not exceed 40% of verified gross income; no evictions resulting in judgment in the past three years; individualized review of criminal history within the past five years.

Applicant 1, a voucher holder, pays $420 of the rent; her income is $2,100 per month. Her share is 20% of income, so she meets the income rule. A vendor score labels her "high risk," largely because of thin credit. Because the policy uses the score only as one factor, and her rental references and payment history are good, the landlord approves. Applicant 2 has a dismissed eviction filing from 2023; the policy counts only judgments, so the filing is not disqualifying.
:::

:::checklist Screening Compliance Checklist
- Written criteria available to every applicant before they pay a fee.
- Income criteria account for voucher holders' share of rent.
- Criminal history reviewed individually and consistent with state and local law.
- Vendor asked about score inputs and disparate-impact testing.
- Adverse action notices prepared for decisions based on consumer reports.
- Decision records kept for every applicant.
:::

:::summary
Screening technology does not shift fair housing responsibility away from the landlord. HUD's 2024 guidance urges relevant, consistent, transparent, accurate screening, and the SafeRent settlement shows the legal risk of opaque scores. Write criteria first, apply them consistently, review individually, and keep records.
:::

# Chapter 9: Algorithmic Rent Pricing and Antitrust Risk

:::objectives
- Describe how revenue management software recommends rents.
- Summarize the Department of Justice lawsuit against RealPage.
- Distinguish lawful use of market data from risky information sharing.
- Set rents defensibly as a small landlord.
:::

Large apartment operators have long used revenue management software that recommends rents based on demand, supply, and lease expirations. The legal question is not whether software can help price units; it is whether competitors share confidential information through a common algorithm in a way that reduces competition.

:::case United States v. RealPage
The Justice Department, together with eight state attorneys general, filed a civil antitrust lawsuit against RealPage Inc., alleging an unlawful scheme to decrease competition among landlords in apartment pricing and to monopolize the market for commercial revenue management software. The Department alleged that RealPage's algorithm enabled landlords to share confidential, competitively sensitive information and align their rents [18]. The Department later filed an amended complaint adding six large landlords as defendants, and the number of state co-plaintiffs grew to ten [19]. These are allegations; readers should check the Department's pages for the current status.

Lesson for the reader: using public market information to set rents is ordinary business. Feeding non-public data into a shared tool that also serves competitors, and deferring to its recommendations, can create antitrust risk.
:::

## Defensible Rent Setting for Small Landlords

1. Use public information: listings, your own leasing history, HUD FMRs, and conversations with tenants and agents.
2. Do not exchange non-public rent, occupancy, or lease data with competing landlords.
3. If you use pricing software, ask whether it pools competitors' confidential data and whether it pressures users to accept recommendations.
4. Document the reasoning behind each rent decision.
5. Check local rent-control, rent-stabilization, and notice rules before any increase.

| Practice | Generally lower risk | Higher risk |
|---|---|---|
| Data source | Public listings, own history | Competitors' non-public rents |
| Decision | Landlord decides, documents reasons | Automatic acceptance of shared algorithm |
| Communication | No rent discussions with competitors | Coordinating rents or concessions |

:::example Setting a Renewal Rent (illustrative)
An invented landlord's tenant pays $1,350 with a lease expiring in 60 days. Public listings for comparable units range from $1,390 to $1,425. Turnover would cost about $2,600 in vacancy and make-ready. A $50 increase to $1,400 yields $600 more per year, while losing the tenant could cost $2,600. The landlord offers $1,385, documents the comparables and turnover analysis, confirms local notice rules, and sends written notice within the required period.
:::

:::checklist Rent Decision Record
- Comparables (public sources) listed with dates.
- Turnover cost estimate recorded.
- Local rent rules and notice periods checked.
- No non-public data from competitors used.
- Final rent and reasoning documented.
:::

:::summary
Revenue management software is lawful in many uses, but the RealPage litigation alleges that shared algorithms using confidential competitor data can harm competition. Small landlords should set rents from public information and their own data, document reasons, and follow local rules.
:::

# Chapter 10: Transaction Technology and Wire Fraud

:::objectives
- Explain how real estate wire fraud works.
- Use FBI IC3 data to understand the scale of the risk.
- Apply a verification protocol for every wire.
- Respond quickly if a fraudulent wire is sent.
:::

Digital closings, e-signatures, and emailed instructions make transactions faster, and they also give criminals an opening. Real estate wire fraud is a subtype of business email compromise in which criminals impersonate a title company, attorney, or agent and send altered wiring instructions.

## The Scale of the Problem

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) reported 12,368 real estate fraud complaints in 2025 with reported losses of about $275 million, up from about $174 million in 2024, and business email compromise losses of about $3.05 billion [20]. The report describes a 2025 case in which a senior citizen closing on a property received a compromised email from the purported "title company" with wire instructions for over $1.3 million, and another in which buyers received an email impersonating their attorneys and wired over $449,000 [20]. An FBI congressional report describes real estate wire fraud as a subcategory of business email compromise targeting people executing large real estate wires [21].

## The Verification Protocol

1. At the start of the transaction, obtain the title or escrow company's phone number from an independent source (its official website typed directly, or a prior contract), not from an email.
2. Agree in writing that wiring instructions will never change by email.
3. Before sending any wire, call the known number and verbally confirm the account and routing numbers.
4. Send a small test amount only if the escrow officer confirms receipt by phone.
5. After wiring, call again to confirm receipt.
6. Never act on urgent last-minute changes without a phone verification.

## If You Are Defrauded

Contact your bank immediately and ask it to initiate a recall, then file a complaint at ic3.gov. The IC3 report describes its Recovery Asset Team working with banks to freeze funds when complaints arrive quickly [20]. Speed matters.

:::example A Near Miss (illustrative)
Two days before closing, an invented buyer receives an email that appears to come from her escrow officer: "Our bank changed; please use the new account below." The domain differs by one letter. Following her protocol, she calls the escrow company's number from its website. The officer confirms that no change was made. The buyer forwards the email to the escrow company and her agent and reports it to IC3. No money is lost.
:::

:::checklist Wire Safety Checklist
- Escrow phone number obtained from an independent source.
- Written agreement that instructions never change by email.
- Verbal confirmation of account details before wiring.
- Receipt confirmed by phone after wiring.
- Bank and IC3 contacts saved in case of fraud.
:::

:::summary
Real estate wire fraud uses impersonation and altered instructions. IC3 reported about $275 million in real estate fraud losses in 2025. A simple protocol, verifying every wire by phone through an independently obtained number, prevents most losses. If fraud occurs, contact your bank and IC3 immediately.
:::

# Chapter 11: Investment Platforms, Crowdfunding, REITs, and Coaching Offers

:::objectives
- Distinguish publicly traded REITs, non-traded REITs, and crowdfunding offerings.
- Read key disclosures: fees, liquidity, sponsor track record, and investment limits.
- Recognize warning signs in real estate "education" and coaching sales.
- Match a platform investment to your goals and risk capacity.
:::

Platforms let small investors own slices of large properties without being landlords. These are securities, not real estate you control, and they require a different kind of due diligence.

## Types of Investment

**REITs.** Investor.gov explains that real estate investment trusts allow individuals to invest in large-scale, income-producing real estate, and that a REIT owns and typically operates income-producing real estate or related assets [22]. Publicly traded REITs trade on exchanges and can be sold daily.

**Non-traded REITs.** The SEC's investor bulletin on non-traded REITs explains their structure and risks; such investments are not traded on an exchange, which can limit liquidity [23].

**Regulation Crowdfunding.** The SEC's bulletin explains that anyone can invest in a Regulation Crowdfunding offering but that, because of the risks involved, non-accredited investors may be limited in how much they can invest in any 12-month period [24]. Limits were adjusted for inflation in 2022 [25].

| Feature | Public REIT | Non-traded REIT | Crowdfunding offering |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquidity | Daily on exchange | Limited | Often very limited |
| Price discovery | Market price | Periodic valuations | Sponsor-determined |
| Diversification | Often broad | Varies | Often single project |
| Key risks | Market volatility | Fees, illiquidity | Sponsor and project risk, illiquidity |

## Due Diligence Questions

1. What exactly do I own (shares, membership units, debt)?
2. What are all fees, including acquisition, asset management, and disposition fees?
3. How and when can I get my money out?
4. What is the sponsor's track record, verified through filings?
5. How much of my net worth would this represent, and could I lose it all?

:::case Zurixx Real Estate Coaching (FTC and Utah)
The FTC and the Utah Division of Consumer Protection sued Zurixx, LLC and its owners, alleging they ran a real estate investment coaching scheme promoted by television house-flipping personalities that made empty promises about profits. The operators agreed to permanent bans and roughly $12 million for consumer redress [26], and in 2024 the FTC announced more than $12 million in refunds to consumers [27].

Lesson for the reader: expensive seminars that promise easy profits deserve skepticism. Public records, inspections, and arithmetic, the subjects of this book, are freely available; no secret system replaces them.
:::

:::example Sizing a Platform Investment (illustrative)
An invented investor has $40,000 in liquid savings, $15,000 of which is an emergency fund. She is considering a $10,000 crowdfunding investment in a single apartment renovation project with a five-year expected hold and no redemption option. Applying her rule of never investing more than 10% of investable assets in a single illiquid offering, she limits the investment to $2,500 ($25,000 x 10%) and keeps the remainder in diversified, liquid holdings.
:::

:::checklist Platform Disclosure Review
- Security type and rights identified.
- All fees listed and totaled.
- Liquidity and redemption terms understood.
- Sponsor track record verified through filings.
- Investment within my personal concentration limit and any legal limit.
:::

:::summary
REITs, non-traded REITs, and crowdfunding offerings are securities with different liquidity, fees, and risks. Read disclosures carefully and size positions to your risk capacity. Real estate coaching offers that promise easy profits have drawn enforcement action; evidence and arithmetic remain the investor's best tools.
:::

- Decision memo with recommendation, maximum price, and open items.
:::

# Appendix F: Questions for Professionals

**For an inspector:** What are the three most expensive likely repairs in the next five years? Are there signs of water intrusion, foundation movement, or unpermitted work? What specialist inspections do you recommend?

**For an insurance agent:** What will insurance cost, including flood or wind? Are there features that would make the property hard to insure? How have premiums changed in this area?

**For a CPA:** How should I allocate purchase price between land and building? What records do I need for depreciation and expenses? How would a sale be taxed?

**For an attorney:** Are there title exceptions I should worry about? What landlord-tenant rules apply locally, including rent control and notice periods? Should I hold the property in an entity?

**For a lender:** What DSCR and reserves do you require? How do you treat projected rents? What happens if rates change before closing?

# Glossary

**After-repair value (ARV).** Estimated market value after planned renovations.

**Automated valuation model (AVM).** Software that estimates property value from data.

**Capitalization rate.** Net operating income divided by purchase price.

**Cash-on-cash return.** Annual pre-tax cash flow divided by total cash invested.

**Comparable sale (comp).** A recent, similar, arm's-length sale used to estimate value.

**Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR).** Net operating income divided by annual debt service.

**Effective gross income (EGI).** Gross rent minus vacancy and credit loss plus other income.

**Fair Market Rent (FMR).** HUD's estimate of 40th-percentile rents used for voucher programs.

**Flood Insurance Rate Map (FIRM).** FEMA's official map of flood zones.

**iBuying.** A business model of algorithm-driven instant offers to buy homes for resale.

**Margin of safety.** A cushion in price or assumptions to absorb estimation error.

**Median error rate.** The error that half of estimates are within and half exceed.

**Net operating income (NOI).** Effective gross income minus operating expenses, before debt service.

**Pro forma.** A projected income and expense statement.

**REIT.** Real estate investment trust; a company owning income-producing real estate in which investors buy shares.

**Revenue management software.** Software that recommends rents based on market data.

**Special Flood Hazard Area.** A high-risk flood zone on FEMA maps.

**Stress test.** Analysis of results under adverse assumptions.

**Wire fraud (real estate).** Theft through falsified wiring instructions, a form of business email compromise.
