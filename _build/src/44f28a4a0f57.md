# How to Use This Book

This book treats house flipping as what it actually is: a short-duration, capital-intensive construction and sales project with a narrow margin for error. It is written for three readers. The first is the newcomer who has watched renovation television and wants to know what the work looks like when the cameras are off. The second is the small contractor, real estate agent, or landlord who has done one or two projects informally and wants a repeatable system. The third is the private lender or partner who will put money into someone else's project and needs a vocabulary for asking hard questions.

By the end of the book you should be able to do six things. You should be able to write a buy box that rejects most properties quickly; estimate an after-repair value from comparable sales rather than hope; build a full-cost budget that includes the costs beginners routinely forget; structure financing and a cash reserve so a delay does not become a crisis; manage contractors, permits, draws, and change orders with written controls; and decide on an exit, including the moment to cut a loss.

## How the chapters are built

Every chapter follows the same pattern so that you can use the book as a reference after the first reading. Each opens with learning objectives. The body defines terms in plain language and then lays out a step-by-step framework. A worked example applies the framework with numbers. Those examples are illustrative: every company, property, address, and dollar figure inside an example box is invented to show arithmetic, not to predict what any project will earn. Where a real organization's experience teaches something important, it appears in a case-study box with a citation to a public source. Each chapter closes with a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions you can use to test yourself or to train a partner.

The appendices contain tools meant to be used, not admired: a deal analyzer, a scope-of-work template, a bid-leveling sheet, a draw schedule, a lender package checklist, and a project closeout review. Copy them freely for your own projects.

## A note on numbers and risk

House flipping is regularly marketed with stories of large, fast profits. The national data are more sobering. ATTOM, a property-data firm that analyzes deed records, reported that the typical home flipped in 2025 was bought for about $259,000 and resold for $325,000, a gross profit of roughly $66,000 and a gross return of 25.5 percent, the lowest annual rate it had recorded since 2008 [1]. Crucially, ATTOM's gross figure is the resale price minus the purchase price only. It excludes renovation, financing, holding, and selling costs, which ATTOM notes that experienced flippers estimate typically run between 20 and 33 percent of the after-repair value [1]. Apply that range to a $325,000 resale and the "gross profit" can shrink to a small net figure or disappear entirely. Keep that arithmetic in mind whenever someone shows you a headline number.

# Chapter 1: The Economics of a Flip

:::objectives
- Define a flip in operational and financial terms.
- Distinguish gross profit from net profit and explain why the difference matters.
- Identify the five cost families that determine whether a flip makes money.
- Explain why time is the hidden variable in every flip.
:::

A house flip is the purchase of a residential property with the intention of improving it and reselling it within a short period, usually less than twelve months. ATTOM's research definition is mechanical: a flip is an arms-length sale of a home that had also changed hands in an arms-length sale within the previous twelve months [1]. Practitioners use a narrower, intention-based definition. A flip is a project in which value is created by changing the property, by renovation, by curing a legal or title problem, or by repositioning it for a different buyer, and then captured by sale.

That distinction matters because it tells you where profit comes from. There are only three sources. The first is buying below market value, usually because the seller needs speed, certainty, or relief from a problem. The second is forcing appreciation through improvements that buyers value more than they cost. The third is market appreciation during the holding period. Professionals plan only on the first two. The third is a bonus when it appears and a threat when it reverses.

## Gross profit versus net profit

Gross profit is the resale price minus the purchase price. Net profit is what remains after every cost of acquiring, financing, holding, improving, and selling the property, and after income tax. Beginners often compute something in between, subtracting the purchase price and the renovation budget but forgetting closing costs on both ends, interest, insurance, utilities, property taxes, permit fees, staging, and the sales commission. Those omitted items are not small. On a mid-priced property they commonly add up to ten percent or more of the resale price.

## The five cost families

Every dollar spent on a flip falls into one of five families. Learning to think in these families prevents omissions.

| Cost family | What it includes | Typical sources of error |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | Purchase price, buyer closing costs, title insurance, inspections, appraisal, survey | Skipping specialist inspections to save money |
| Financing | Points, origination fees, interest, draw fees, extension fees, private-lender returns | Assuming the project ends on schedule |
| Holding | Property tax, insurance, utilities, HOA dues, security, lawn and snow care | Ignoring cost of vacancy during listing period |
| Renovation | Labor, materials, permits, dumpsters, contingency, design | Scope creep and hidden conditions behind walls |
| Disposition | Agent commissions, seller closing costs, transfer taxes, staging, photography, buyer concessions | Assuming full list price with no concessions |

## Time is the hidden variable

Financing and holding costs accrue every day the project is open. A project budgeted at four months that takes seven does not merely delay profit; it consumes it. Time also exposes the project to market change. A flipper who buys in a rising market and sells in a flat one has to absorb the difference. This is why every chapter of this book returns to schedule control.

## A five-step economic screen

1. Estimate the after-repair value (ARV) from recent, comparable closed sales (Chapter 3).
2. Estimate the renovation cost from a written scope, not from a per-square-foot guess (Chapter 5).
3. Estimate financing, holding, and selling costs for a realistic schedule plus a delay buffer (Chapter 6).
4. Subtract all costs and a target profit from the ARV to find your maximum allowable offer (MAO).
5. Compare the MAO with what the seller will accept. If the gap cannot be closed, walk away.

:::example Illustrative: the full-cost view of a "$60,000 profit"
A hypothetical property is purchased for $240,000 and expected to resell for $320,000. The seller's agent describes this as an $80,000 spread. A full-cost screen shows otherwise. Renovation budget: $38,000. Contingency at 12 percent: $4,560. Buyer closing costs at 2 percent of price: $4,800. Financing (a 10 percent interest-only loan on $250,000 for six months plus 2 points): $12,500 + $5,000 = $17,500. Holding costs (taxes, insurance, utilities) for six months at $650 per month: $3,900. Selling costs at 7 percent of resale (commissions, seller closing costs, staging): $22,400. Total costs beyond the purchase price: $91,160. Net result before tax: $320,000 − $240,000 − $91,160 = −$11,160. The "spread" was a loss. Every figure here is invented; the lesson is the method.
:::

:::case Zillow Offers and the danger of forecasting resale prices
Zillow Group, the real estate listings company, launched an iBuying operation called Zillow Offers that bought homes, renovated them, and resold them. In its third-quarter 2021 report, Zillow announced it would wind the business down, recorded an inventory write-down of about $304 million because it had purchased homes at prices above its own estimates of future selling prices, and said it expected a further $240 to $265 million of losses in the next quarter [2]. Chief Executive Rich Barton stated that "the unpredictability in forecasting home prices far exceeds what we anticipated" [2]. CNBC reported that Zillow bought 9,680 homes in that quarter and sold 3,032, and that rising labor and materials costs eroded already thin margins [3]. The lesson for a small operator is direct: a company with vast data still could not reliably predict resale prices months ahead, so a single flipper should build margin and contingency into every offer rather than rely on a forecast.
:::

:::checklist Economic screen
- I can name the source of value in this deal (discount, forced appreciation, or both).
- My resale estimate is based on closed sales, not list prices or automated estimates.
- I have costed all five cost families.
- I have modeled at least one delay scenario.
- I know my maximum allowable offer before I talk price with a seller.
:::

:::summary
A flip is a construction and sales project whose profit depends on buying well and improving efficiently, not on market appreciation. Gross spread is not profit. Every flip carries acquisition, financing, holding, renovation, and disposition costs, and the last three grow with time. National data show that typical gross returns have narrowed sharply, and even a data-rich company like Zillow lost money when its resale forecasts were wrong.
:::

# Chapter 2: Writing Your Buy Box

:::objectives
- Explain what a buy box is and why it saves time and money.
- Choose a target market using observable data.
- Set property, condition, and financial limits that reflect your skills and capital.
- Create a stop rule before emotions enter the process.
:::

A buy box is a written set of criteria that a property must meet before you spend serious time on it. It functions like an admissions standard. Without one, every listing looks like a possibility and you spend evenings analyzing houses you should have rejected in thirty seconds. With one, you can screen dozens of properties quickly and reserve your analysis for the few that fit.

A good buy box reflects three realities: the market where you can realistically operate, the kind of work you can competently supervise, and the amount of capital you can afford to lose. It is not a wish list. It is a statement of limits.

## Choosing a market

Start geographically small. Most successful small operators work within a drive time that lets them visit a site several times a week. Within that radius, look for neighborhoods where three conditions hold: houses sell regularly, so you have comparable sales; there is visible variation in condition, so updated homes sell for meaningfully more than dated ones; and buyer demand comes from owner-occupants who will pay for finished work.

ATTOM's data illustrate how widely results vary by place. In the first quarter of 2025, its gross ROI figures ranged from more than 100 percent in Buffalo and Pittsburgh to about 19 percent in metro areas where flippers' typical purchase price exceeded $400,000 [4]. The same report found that homes originally bought for less than $50,000 in a later quarter produced a typical loss [5]. Cheap is not the same as profitable. Low-priced houses in weak markets can carry repair costs that resale prices never recover.

## Property and condition limits

Define the property type (detached single-family, townhouse, small multifamily), size range, bedroom and bathroom minimums, and age. Then define condition limits by category of work. A useful approach sorts work into three tiers.

| Tier | Typical work | Who should attempt it |
|---|---|---|
| Cosmetic | Paint, flooring, fixtures, landscaping, minor repairs | First-time flippers with a reliable general contractor |
| Moderate | Kitchen and bath remodels, roof, windows, HVAC replacement | Operators with at least one completed project |
| Heavy | Foundation, structural changes, full rewiring, additions, fire or flood damage | Experienced teams with engineers and deep reserves |

Your buy box should name the highest tier you will accept. A first project limited to cosmetic and light moderate work is far less likely to surprise you.

## Financial limits

State your maximum purchase price, maximum total project cost, minimum projected net profit in dollars, and minimum projected margin as a percentage of ARV. Add a liquidity rule: the cash you must hold in reserve after closing, expressed in months of carrying costs plus a percentage of the renovation budget.

## The stop rule

A stop rule is a pre-commitment. Write it down before you look at houses: "I will walk away if the projected net profit is below $X, if the renovation scope includes structural work, if I cannot get two written bids within the inspection period, or if my reserve would fall below $Y." Behavioral research and ordinary experience agree that people who have invested time in a deal tend to rationalize continuing. A written stop rule protects you from your own optimism.

## Building the buy box in six steps

1. Draw your operating radius on a map and list the neighborhoods inside it.
2. For each neighborhood, pull twelve months of closed sales and note the price spread between updated and dated homes.
3. Pick one or two neighborhoods with steady sales and a clear condition premium.
4. Set property-type, size, and condition-tier limits.
5. Set financial limits and a liquidity reserve.
6. Write your stop rule and share it with a partner or lender who will hold you to it.

:::example Illustrative: a first-project buy box
A hypothetical first-time flipper, working with a licensed general contractor, writes this buy box. Area: three named neighborhoods within 20 minutes of home. Property: detached three-bedroom, at least 1.5 baths, 1,100 to 1,700 square feet, built after 1950. Condition: cosmetic and light moderate only; no foundation, structural, or fire-damaged properties. Price: maximum purchase $230,000. Total project cost cap: $290,000. Minimum projected net profit: $25,000 and at least 8 percent of ARV. Reserve: six months of carrying costs plus 15 percent of the renovation budget, held in cash. Stop rule: walk away if any specialist inspection reveals structural or sewer-line failure, or if two written bids exceed the budget by more than 10 percent.
:::

:::worksheet My buy box
Operating radius and neighborhoods: ___
Property type and size range: ___
Highest condition tier I will accept: ___
Maximum purchase price: ___
Maximum total project cost: ___
Minimum projected net profit ($ and % of ARV): ___
Required cash reserve after closing: ___
My stop rule, in one sentence: ___
Person who will hold me to it: ___
:::

:::summary
A buy box turns a vague ambition into a filter. It should reflect your market, your skills, and your capital. Market data show that results vary enormously by location and that the cheapest houses can lose money. Condition tiers keep beginners away from structural risk, and a written stop rule guards against sunk-cost thinking.
:::

# Chapter 3: Valuing the Finished House

:::objectives
- Define after-repair value and explain why it anchors every other number.
- Select and adjust comparable sales using a consistent method.
- Recognize the limits of automated valuation estimates.
- Produce a valuation range rather than a single point estimate.
:::

After-repair value (ARV) is the price a typical informed buyer would pay for the property after your planned improvements are complete, in the market conditions you expect at the time of sale. Every subsequent number depends on it. If the ARV is overstated by ten percent, the error typically consumes most or all of a flip's profit.

## Comparable sales

A comparable sale, or comp, is a recent closed sale of a similar property in the same submarket. Closed sales are evidence; list prices and pending sales are not, because they may still change. Appraisers use comps to estimate value, and lenders will rely on an appraiser's opinion when they finance your buyer. Your ARV should therefore anticipate what an appraiser will conclude, not what you hope a buyer will pay.

Good comps share five characteristics with the subject property: location (ideally the same subdivision or within a short distance, without crossing major boundaries such as highways or school attendance lines); recency (ideally sold within the past three to six months); size (within about 15 to 20 percent of living area); configuration (similar bedrooms, bathrooms, lot, and garage); and condition (renovated to the standard you intend).

## Adjusting comps

No comp is identical. Adjustments correct for differences. If a comp has a two-car garage and your finished house will have none, you subtract the market value of that garage from the comp's price to estimate what the comp would have sold for without it. Adjustments should be based on evidence, such as paired sales of otherwise similar homes, and they should be few. A comp that needs large adjustments is a weak comp.

## Automated valuation estimates

Online estimates are convenient and often useful for screening, but they are produced by statistical models that cannot see the interior of the house, the quality of a renovation, or an unusual feature of the lot. The Zillow Offers experience described in Chapter 1 is a reminder that even sophisticated models can miss turning points [2]. Use automated estimates as a sanity check, never as your ARV.

## A seven-step valuation method

1. Define the finished product: bedrooms, bathrooms, square footage, finishes, and any added features.
2. Pull all closed sales in the submarket for the past six months. Extend to twelve months only if needed.
3. Keep the three to six sales most similar to the finished product.
4. Make a small number of evidence-based adjustments to each.
5. Weight the comps, giving most weight to those needing the least adjustment.
6. State a low, most-likely, and high ARV.
7. Use the low ARV for your offer math and the most-likely ARV for your listing plan.

:::example Illustrative: reconciling four comps
The finished subject will be a 1,450-square-foot, three-bedroom, two-bath ranch with a one-car garage. Four hypothetical comps: Comp A, 1,420 sq ft, 3/2, one-car garage, renovated, sold 6 weeks ago for $318,000; no adjustment, adjusted $318,000. Comp B, 1,520 sq ft, 3/2, two-car garage, renovated, sold 3 months ago for $331,000; subtract $8,000 for the extra garage bay and $4,000 for size, adjusted $319,000. Comp C, 1,380 sq ft, 3/1.5, one-car garage, renovated, sold 2 months ago for $305,000; add $7,000 for the half bath and $3,000 for size, adjusted $315,000. Comp D, 1,460 sq ft, 3/2, no garage, partially updated, sold 5 months ago for $296,000; add $9,000 for garage and $12,000 for condition, adjusted $317,000, but given low weight because of the large condition adjustment. Reconciled range: low $312,000, most likely $317,000, high $322,000. The offer is computed from $312,000.
:::

| Comp quality signal | Strong comp | Weak comp |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | Same subdivision | Across a major road or school boundary |
| Sale date | Under 3 months | Over 9 months |
| Size difference | Under 10 percent | Over 25 percent |
| Number of adjustments | 0 to 2 small | 4 or more, or one large |
| Condition | Matches planned finish | Very different finish level |

:::checklist ARV discipline
- All comps are closed sales with documented dates and prices.
- I have at least three comps that need only small adjustments.
- Each adjustment has a reason I can explain to a lender.
- I have stated a range, and my offer uses the low end.
- I checked my ARV against an automated estimate and can explain any large gap.
:::

:::summary
ARV anchors every flip calculation, so it must be grounded in recent, similar closed sales with few, evidence-based adjustments. Automated estimates are screening tools only. Expressing ARV as a range and building offers on the low end protects against the most common cause of failed flips: overestimating resale value.
:::

# Chapter 4: Due Diligence on an As-Is Property

:::objectives
- Explain what "as-is" means and what it does not mean.
- Build a due-diligence plan with specialist inspections matched to property risk.
- Recognize the federal lead-paint requirements that apply to flippers of pre-1978 homes.
- Investigate permits, title, and neighborhood factors before the contingency period ends.
:::

Many flip properties are sold "as-is." In most contracts, that phrase means the seller will not make repairs or give credits for condition. It does not mean the buyer gives up the right to inspect, and in many states it does not relieve the seller of statutory disclosure duties. Read your contract and ask a real estate attorney or your agent what rights you have during the inspection or due-diligence period. That period is the most valuable time in the entire project: it is the last moment when walking away costs little.

## The general inspection and its limits

A general home inspection is a visual, non-invasive survey of accessible systems. It is valuable, but it is limited. Inspectors do not open walls, do not test every circuit, and often recommend specialists when they see warning signs. Treat the general inspection as a map of where to look harder.

## Matching specialist inspections to risk

| Warning sign or property feature | Specialist to consider | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Older home, mature trees, slow drains | Sewer camera inspection | Main-line replacement can cost thousands and is invisible above ground |
| Cracks, sloping floors, sticking doors | Structural engineer | Foundation repair is the most common budget-breaker |
| Roof over ~15 years or visible damage | Roofer | Replacement is a major line item and affects insurance |
| Fuse panel, aluminum branch wiring, ungrounded outlets | Licensed electrician | May require full or partial rewiring and permits |
| Stains, odors, past flooding | Moisture and mold assessment | Hidden water damage expands scope |
| Built before 1978 | Lead-based paint inspection or risk assessment | Federal work-practice rules apply to paid renovators and flippers |
| Built before roughly 1980 with older insulation, tile, or pipe wrap | Asbestos survey | Disturbing asbestos may require licensed abatement |
| Well or septic | Well flow and water test; septic inspection | Failures are expensive and can block resale financing |

## Lead paint and the RRP Rule

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) Rule requires that anyone paid to perform work that disturbs painted surfaces in homes built before 1978 be certified, with workers trained in lead-safe work practices [6]. The EPA states that the rule generally does not apply to homeowners working on their own homes, but that it does apply to people who buy, renovate, and sell homes for profit, explicitly naming house flippers [6]. Several states run their own authorized programs. Before any paint-disturbing work on a pre-1978 property, confirm which program governs your location and use certified firms. The EPA also notes that about three-quarters of U.S. homes built before 1978 still contain some lead-based paint [6]. This is a legal, health, and budget issue at once.

## Permits, title, and the neighborhood

Visit or search the local building department's records for open permits, expired permits, and code violations on the property. Unpermitted additions or garage conversions can reduce value and may need to be legalized or removed. Order a preliminary title report early and ask your title company or attorney to explain any liens, easements, or judgments. Finally, walk the neighborhood at different times of day, check flood maps, and note anything a future buyer will notice.

## A due-diligence plan in six steps

1. On the day your offer is accepted, write a calendar of contingency deadlines.
2. Book the general inspection within the first few days.
3. Book every specialist the property's age and the inspector's notes call for.
4. Request permit history and order preliminary title.
5. Get at least two contractor walkthroughs to price the scope (Chapter 5).
6. Re-run your deal analysis with the new information before the deadline, then proceed, renegotiate, or cancel.

:::example Illustrative: a sewer camera changes the deal
A hypothetical 1958 house is under contract at $215,000. The general inspector notes slow drainage and large trees near the front yard and recommends a sewer scope, which costs $250. The scope shows root intrusion and a partially collapsed clay pipe between the house and the street. A plumber quotes $9,500 for replacement. The buyer re-runs the analysis: projected net profit falls from $27,000 to $17,500, below the buy box minimum of $25,000. The buyer asks for a $9,500 price reduction. The seller agrees to $6,000. Net profit is now projected at $23,500, still below the minimum, so under the stop rule the buyer cancels within the contingency period and loses only inspection fees.
:::

:::checklist Before your contingency deadline
- General inspection completed and report read in full.
- All recommended specialist inspections completed.
- Lead-paint status known for any pre-1978 property, with RRP compliance planned.
- Permit history and code-violation search completed.
- Preliminary title reviewed with title company or attorney.
- Two contractor walkthroughs completed.
- Deal analysis updated and decision documented.
:::

:::summary
"As-is" limits what the seller will fix, not what you are allowed to learn. Use the contingency period to inspect thoroughly, match specialists to risk, confirm lead-paint obligations under the EPA RRP Rule, and check permits and title. Then re-run the numbers and be willing to walk away.
:::

# Chapter 5: Scoping the Renovation

:::objectives
- Write a scope of work precise enough for contractors to bid on identically.
- Decide which improvements are worth making using a buyer-value test.
- Estimate costs from line items rather than rules of thumb.
- Set a contingency that reflects the property's risk.
:::

A scope of work is a written, room-by-room and system-by-system description of exactly what will be done, to what standard, with what materials. It is the bridge between your valuation and your budget. A vague scope ("update kitchen") invites contractors to bid different projects, makes bids impossible to compare, and guarantees disputes later. A precise scope ("remove existing cabinets and countertops; install 22 linear feet of shaker-style stock cabinets in white, quartz countertop in a stated color group with undermount sink, new faucet allowance $250, tile backsplash 30 square feet, allowance $6 per square foot") lets you compare bids line by line and hold contractors to a standard.

## The buyer-value test

Not every improvement earns its cost. The question for each line is: will the typical buyer at this price point pay more for the house because of this item than the item costs, or will its absence prevent a sale? Improvements fall into three groups. Required items are those needed for safety, code compliance, insurability, or lender approval: a failing roof, a non-functioning furnace, exposed wiring. Expected items are those buyers in your price range assume a renovated house will have, which in many markets include updated kitchens and bathrooms, fresh paint, and new flooring. Optional items are upgrades beyond the neighborhood norm. Do the required items always, the expected items to the neighborhood standard, and optional items only when your comps show buyers pay for them.

Over-improvement is a classic error. A luxury kitchen in a starter-home neighborhood rarely returns its cost, because buyers in that market are constrained by what lenders will finance and what appraisers will support from comps.

## Line-item estimating

Rules of thumb such as a cost per square foot are useful only for a first screen. Before you commit, build an estimate from line items: quantities (square feet of flooring, linear feet of cabinets, number of fixtures) multiplied by unit costs taken from written bids, supplier quotes, or your own completed projects. Add permits, dumpsters, temporary utilities, cleaning, and design fees, which are often left out.

## Setting contingency

Contingency is money set aside for conditions you cannot see yet. It is not a slush fund for upgrades. Scale it to risk: lower for a cosmetic project on a newer house with clean inspections, higher for an older house with original plumbing and wiring or any sign of water damage. Many practitioners use ranges of roughly 10 percent for low-risk cosmetic work and 15 to 20 percent or more for older homes or heavier scopes; whatever percentage you choose, write down why.

## Seven steps to a usable scope

1. Walk the house with your inspection reports and photograph every room and system.
2. List required items first, then expected items, then optional items.
3. Specify quantities, materials, and finish standards for each line.
4. Use allowances (fixed dollar amounts) for items where you will choose finishes later.
5. Note which items require permits and which require licensed trades.
6. Price each line from at least two sources.
7. Add a risk-based contingency and freeze the scope before bidding.

:::example Illustrative: scope summary for a 1,400-square-foot ranch
| Area | Work | Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Exterior | Repair fascia, paint trim, replace front door, landscape cleanup | $4,800 |
| Roof | Tear-off and replace architectural shingles, 18 squares | $11,200 |
| Kitchen | Stock cabinets, quartz top, appliances package, backsplash, lighting | $16,500 |
| Baths (2) | Retile shower/tub surround, new vanities, toilets, fixtures | $11,000 |
| Flooring | Luxury vinyl plank 1,150 sq ft; bedroom carpet 250 sq ft | $7,400 |
| Paint | Interior walls, ceilings, trim | $5,200 |
| Electrical | Replace outlets/switches, add GFCI, new light fixtures | $3,100 |
| Permits, dumpsters, cleaning | Building and electrical permits, two dumpsters, final clean | $2,600 |
| Subtotal | | $61,800 |
| Contingency | 15 percent (house built 1962) | $9,270 |
| Total renovation budget | | $71,070 |

All figures are invented for illustration; local costs vary widely.
:::

# Chapter 6: The Full-Cost Deal Analysis

:::objectives
- Build a complete deal analysis that includes all five cost families.
- Calculate a maximum allowable offer from the ARV backward.
- Understand the 70 percent heuristic and its limitations.
- Stress-test a deal against delay, cost overrun, and lower resale price.
:::

The deal analysis is the document that tells you whether to make an offer and at what price. It brings together the ARV from Chapter 3, the renovation budget from Chapter 5, and every other cost. It should be simple enough to fit on one page and honest enough that you would show it to a skeptical lender.

## Working backward from ARV

Most beginners start with the asking price and ask whether the deal works. Professionals start with the ARV and work backward to the most they can pay. The formula is:

Maximum allowable offer = low ARV − renovation budget (including contingency) − acquisition costs − financing costs − holding costs − selling costs − target profit.

Because financing and acquisition costs depend partly on the purchase price, you may need to iterate once or twice, or use a spreadsheet.

## The 70 percent heuristic

A widely repeated rule of thumb says a flipper should pay no more than 70 percent of ARV minus repair costs. It is a quick screen, not a law. It implicitly assumes that the remaining 30 percent of ARV covers all financing, holding, and selling costs plus profit. In high-priced markets, where commissions and transfer taxes scale with price, or in slow markets, where holding periods lengthen, 70 percent can be too generous. In low-priced markets with fast sales, investors sometimes pay more. Use it to discard obviously bad deals, then do the full analysis.

## Stress testing

A deal that works only if everything goes right is not a deal. Run at least three stress cases. Delay: add three months of financing and holding costs. Overrun: add 15 percent to the renovation budget beyond contingency. Price: reduce ARV by 5 percent. If the deal loses money in any single case, you are exposed. If it loses money in a plausible combination of two, consider whether your reserve can absorb that outcome.

| Scenario | ARV | Renovation | Months held | Net profit (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base case | $312,000 | $71,070 | 6 | $24,600 |
| Delay | $312,000 | $71,070 | 9 | $17,400 |
| Overrun | $312,000 | $81,730 | 6 | $13,900 |
| Price drop | $296,400 | $71,070 | 6 | $10,100 |
| Delay plus price drop | $296,400 | $71,070 | 9 | $2,900 |

## The analysis in eight steps

1. Enter low and most-likely ARV.
2. Enter the renovation budget with contingency.
3. Estimate acquisition costs from a title company or closing-cost estimate.
4. Enter financing terms: rate, points, fees, and loan amount.
5. Estimate holding costs per month and a realistic schedule.
6. Estimate selling costs, including commissions, transfer taxes, staging, and a concession allowance.
7. Choose a target profit in dollars and as a percentage of ARV.
8. Solve for maximum allowable offer, then run the stress cases.

:::example Illustrative: computing the maximum allowable offer
Low ARV: $312,000. Renovation with contingency: $71,070. Selling costs at 7 percent of ARV: $21,840. Holding costs for 6 months at $700: $4,200. Target profit: $25,000. Before acquisition and financing costs, the remaining amount is $312,000 − $71,070 − $21,840 − $4,200 − $25,000 = $189,890. Suppose a lender charges 2 points and 11 percent interest-only on 90 percent of purchase price plus 100 percent of renovation, drawn evenly, and buyer closing costs are 2 percent. Trying a purchase price of $165,000: loan on purchase $148,500 plus average renovation balance about $35,500 gives average balance about $184,000; interest for 6 months ≈ $10,120; points on total loan of $219,570 ≈ $4,390; closing costs $3,300. Total ≈ $17,810. $165,000 + $17,810 = $182,810, which is below $189,890, so the offer could rise slightly. A second iteration at $171,000 gives total costs near $18,500 and a sum of about $189,500. Maximum allowable offer ≈ $171,000. The 70 percent heuristic would have suggested 0.70 × $312,000 − $71,070 = $147,330, showing how rules of thumb and full analysis can diverge.
:::

:::checklist One-page deal analysis
- ARV range documented with comps attached.
- Renovation budget from line items with risk-based contingency.
- Acquisition, financing, holding, and selling costs all present.
- Target profit stated in dollars and percentage.
- Maximum allowable offer computed by iteration.
- Three stress cases run; outcome of worst case known.
:::

:::summary
The deal analysis works backward from a conservative ARV to the maximum you can pay while still earning your target profit. The 70 percent rule is only a screen. Stress tests for delay, overrun, and lower resale price reveal whether a deal is robust or fragile.

# Chapter 7: Financing the Project

:::objectives
- Compare the main financing options for flips and their cost structures.
- Read a term sheet and identify points, fees, draw terms, and extension costs.
- Size a cash reserve that survives a realistic delay.
- Understand how buyer financing rules, such as FHA's anti-flipping rule, affect your timeline.
:::

Financing determines both the cost and the fragility of a flip. Expensive, short-term money magnifies delay; patient money gives you room to solve problems. Before you look for a lender, understand the options and what each really costs.

## The main options

| Source | Typical structure | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal cash | No interest; opportunity cost | Fast closings, no lender approvals, no draw delays | Concentrates personal risk; limits number of projects |
| Hard-money or private-bridge lender | Short-term, interest-only, points at closing, renovation funds released in draws | Lends on the property and plan; fast | High cost; extension fees if late; draw inspections can slow work |
| Private individual lender | Negotiated note secured by the property | Flexible terms | Relationship risk; must be documented properly; securities rules may apply to pooled money |
| Home equity line on a primary residence | Variable-rate revolving credit | Relatively low rate | Puts your home at risk if the flip fails |
| Partner equity | Partner provides capital for a share of profit | No monthly payments | Gives up profit; requires a clear written agreement |

## Reading a term sheet

A term sheet from a short-term lender typically states the loan-to-cost and loan-to-ARV limits, the interest rate, points (each point is one percent of the loan amount, paid at closing), origination, underwriting, and draw fees, the term (often 6 to 12 months), the extension fee if you need more time, prepayment rules, and whether interest is charged on the full renovation commitment or only on funds drawn. That last item can make a large difference. Ask for an example payoff calculation in writing.

## Draws

Renovation funds are usually held back and released in draws after the lender inspects completed work. You pay contractors first and are reimbursed, or the lender pays them directly. Either way, you need working capital to bridge the gap between doing work and receiving funds. Draw delays are a common source of contractor frustration and schedule slippage, so learn the lender's process and turnaround time before closing.

## Sizing the reserve

A reserve is cash you do not plan to spend. A practical rule is to hold enough to cover at least three additional months of interest and holding costs plus the gap between the renovation contingency and your worst stress-case overrun. If losing that reserve would harm your household, the project is too large for you.

## How buyer financing shapes your timeline

Your eventual buyer's loan type matters. Under the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development's regulation at 24 CFR 203.37a, a property resold 90 days or less after the seller acquired it is not eligible for an FHA-insured mortgage, and resales between 91 and 180 days may require additional documentation, including a second appraisal, when the price increase exceeds the threshold set by HUD [7]. The regulation lists exceptions, for example sales of HUD-owned properties and inherited properties [7]. If many buyers in your price range use FHA loans, a very fast flip can shrink your buyer pool. Plan your listing date accordingly and discuss it with your agent.

## Six steps to a financing plan

1. Decide how much of your own capital you can afford to lose entirely.
2. Request term sheets from at least two lenders and convert each to a total-cost estimate for your base and delay schedules.
3. Confirm the draw process, inspection timing, and who pays contractors.
4. Calculate the reserve and keep it in a separate account.
5. Check the likely buyer loan types for your price point and plan the listing date.
6. Document any private loan or partnership in writing reviewed by an attorney.

:::example Illustrative: comparing two term sheets
Loan request: $150,000 purchase funds plus $70,000 renovation, six-month base schedule. Lender A: 10 percent interest on the full $220,000 commitment from day one, 2 points, $1,500 fees, extension 1 point per 3 months. Six-month cost: interest $11,000 + points $4,400 + fees $1,500 = $16,900. Nine-month cost: interest $16,500 + points $4,400 + fees $1,500 + extension $2,200 = $24,600. Lender B: 11.5 percent interest only on funds drawn, 2.5 points, $2,000 fees, extension 1.5 points per 3 months. With renovation drawn evenly, the average balance is about $185,000. Six-month cost: interest ≈ $10,640 + points $5,500 + fees $2,000 = $18,140. Nine-month cost: interest ≈ $17,700 + points $5,500 + fees $2,000 + extension $3,300 = $28,500. Lender A is cheaper in both scenarios even though its headline rate is lower by only 1.5 points, because its extension terms are gentler. Figures are hypothetical.
:::

:::checklist Financing readiness
- I know the maximum I can afford to lose.
- Two or more term sheets converted to total cost under base and delay schedules.
- Draw process and turnaround confirmed in writing.
- Reserve funded in a separate account.
- Buyer-financing timing rules, including FHA's resale rule, considered.
- Private loans or partner agreements reviewed by an attorney.
:::

:::summary
Financing choices change both the cost and the risk of a flip. Compare lenders on total cost under realistic and delayed schedules, understand draws, and hold a reserve you never plan to touch. Buyer financing rules, notably FHA's 90-day resale restriction, can shape the earliest practical sale date.
:::

# Chapter 8: Selecting and Contracting the Team

:::objectives
- Identify the roles a small flip requires and who should fill them.
- Vet contractors for license, insurance, references, and capacity.
- Level bids so they can be compared fairly.
- Write a contract with payment terms tied to completed work.
:::

A flip is a team effort even when one person owns it. The owner sets the plan, controls money, and makes decisions. Around the owner sit professionals with specific roles: a real estate agent or broker who knows the submarket, a general contractor who manages trades, licensed specialty trades (electrical, plumbing, HVAC, roofing), an inspector, a title or escrow company, an insurance agent, a lender, a tax professional, and, where needed, a real estate attorney and a structural engineer. Your first projects are as much about building this team as about the house.

## Vetting contractors

The Federal Trade Commission's guidance for hiring a contractor offers a sound baseline: get recommendations from people you trust, check online reputation, confirm the license with your state or local government, ask for proof of insurance, get written estimates from several firms, and do not automatically pick the lowest bid without understanding why it is low [8][9]. The FTC also warns that it is a red flag if a contractor asks you to pull the permits yourself, insists on cash, or demands full payment up front [9].

Go further than a homeowner would. Ask for three references from projects of similar size completed in the past year and call them. Visit a job in progress. Ask how many projects the contractor is running now and who will be on site daily. A contractor who is overcommitted will deliver your project late regardless of skill.

## Leveling bids

Bid leveling is the process of putting competing bids on the same basis. Because you wrote a precise scope in Chapter 5, every contractor should be pricing the same work. In practice, bids still differ: one excludes permits, another assumes you supply fixtures, a third uses a lower allowance for tile. Build a table with your scope lines down the side and each bidder across the top. Note inclusions, exclusions, and allowances. Then ask each bidder to fill the gaps before you compare totals.

| Scope line | Bidder 1 | Bidder 2 | Bidder 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen cabinets and install | $9,800 | $8,900 | $10,400 |
| Countertops | Included in cabinets | $3,100 | $2,800 |
| Permits | Excluded | Included | Included |
| Tile allowance | $4/sq ft | $6/sq ft | $6/sq ft |
| Dumpsters | Owner supplies | Included | Included |
| Start date and duration | 2 weeks out, 9 weeks | 5 weeks out, 7 weeks | 1 week out, 10 weeks |

## Contract essentials

The FTC recommends that a contract include the contractor's name, address, phone number, and license number; estimated start and completion dates; the payment schedule; the contractor's obligation to obtain permits; warranty terms; and any promises made orally [8][9]. For a flip, add: a detailed scope and allowance schedule attached as an exhibit; a change-order procedure requiring written, priced approval before extra work begins; lien waivers from the contractor and major subcontractors and suppliers with each payment; daily cleanup and site security responsibilities; and a clause about what happens if work stops.

## Payment terms

Tie payments to completed, inspected milestones rather than to the calendar. The FTC advises limiting the down payment, noting that some states cap it, and making payments contingent on completed work [8]. Retain a final payment until the work is complete, the final inspection has passed, and you hold lien waivers showing subcontractors and suppliers have been paid.

## Six steps to contracting

1. List required roles and fill each with a named person or firm.
2. Invite three qualified contractors to walk the property with your scope.
3. Verify licenses, insurance certificates, and references.
4. Level the bids and resolve exclusions.
5. Negotiate a contract with milestone payments, change-order rules, and lien waivers.
6. Hold a pre-construction meeting to agree on schedule, communication, and site rules.

:::example Illustrative: why the lowest bid was not the cheapest
Three hypothetical bids for the same scope arrive at $58,900, $63,400, and $66,200. The lowest bid excludes permits ($1,400), dumpsters ($1,200), and countertops ($3,100), and uses a $4 tile allowance where the scope assumes $6 across 180 square feet ($360 difference). Leveled, it becomes $64,960, higher than the middle bid. The middle bidder also has the shortest duration, saving roughly one month of holding and interest costs, which the owner estimates at $2,300. The middle bid is selected.
:::

:::checklist Contractor vetting
- State or local license verified and current.
- General liability and workers' compensation certificates on file.
- Three recent references called; one job site visited.
- Current workload and on-site supervisor identified.
- Bid leveled against the scope with all exclusions resolved.
- Contract includes milestone payments, change-order procedure, lien waivers, and permit responsibility.
:::

:::summary
Your team determines your outcome. Vet contractors as a professional would, following and extending the FTC's guidance: license, insurance, references, and capacity. Level bids before comparing them, and contract for milestone payments, written change orders, and lien waivers.
:::

# Chapter 9: Managing Construction

:::objectives
- Build a construction schedule that sequences trades correctly.
- Run weekly site meetings and keep a project log.
- Control change orders and protect the budget.
- Manage permits and inspections without stalling work.
:::

Once demolition begins, the project's financial clock speeds up. Every idle day costs interest and holding expense. The owner's job during construction is not to swing a hammer but to keep information moving, decisions timely, and money controlled.

## Sequencing

Renovation work follows a logic. Demolition and haul-off come first. Then come structural repairs and rough-in work for plumbing, electrical, and HVAC, which must be inspected before walls close. Insulation and drywall follow, then cabinets and built-ins, then flooring (sometimes before cabinets, depending on material), then paint, trim, fixtures, and appliances. Exterior work and landscaping run in parallel when weather allows. A final clean, punch list, and final inspection close the job. Disrupting this order wastes money; for example, painting before electrical trim-out often means repainting.

| Phase | Typical activities | Inspection or decision point |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Preparation | Permits issued, materials ordered, utilities confirmed | Long-lead items (windows, cabinets) ordered |
| 2. Demolition | Remove finishes, haul debris | Reassess hidden conditions; update contingency |
| 3. Structure and rough-in | Framing repairs, plumbing, electrical, HVAC rough | Rough inspections passed |
| 4. Close-up | Insulation, drywall, tape and finish | Insulation inspection where required |
| 5. Finishes | Cabinets, counters, flooring, tile, paint, trim | Owner selections confirmed on time |
| 6. Fixtures and exterior | Lighting, plumbing fixtures, appliances, exterior paint, landscaping | Utilities tested |
| 7. Closeout | Punch list, final clean, final inspection | Certificate or final approval; lien waivers collected |

## Weekly rhythm

Hold a site meeting at the same time every week with the general contractor. Walk the property, compare progress with the schedule, review upcoming decisions, and agree on actions. Keep a project log with dated photographs, decisions, deliveries, inspection results, and problems. The log protects you in disputes and becomes the raw material for your post-project review.

## Change orders

Change orders arise from three sources: hidden conditions discovered after demolition, owner-requested changes, and errors or omissions in the scope. Treat each differently. Hidden conditions are what contingency is for; price them, approve them in writing, and record the draw against contingency. Owner-requested changes should be rare and must pass the buyer-value test. Scope errors are lessons for the next project. Never let work proceed on a verbal change. Require a written description, price, and schedule impact signed by both parties.

## Permits and inspections

The FTC notes that most states and localities require permits for building projects and that a competent contractor will obtain them before starting work [8]. Unpermitted work can surface during the buyer's inspection or appraisal and derail the sale. Schedule inspections as soon as each phase is ready, be present or have the contractor present, and keep signed inspection cards in your project file.

## Seven steps to construction control

1. Build a phase-by-phase schedule with the contractor before demolition.
2. Order long-lead materials before or immediately after closing.
3. Hold a weekly site meeting and update the project log.
4. Re-forecast budget and schedule after demolition.
5. Approve change orders only in writing, recording contingency used.
6. Request inspections promptly and file the results.
7. Track a budget-versus-actual report weekly.

:::example Illustrative: a demolition surprise and a disciplined response
After demolition of a hypothetical bathroom, the contractor finds a rotted subfloor around the toilet flange and a leaking cast-iron drain section. The contractor prices the repair at $3,600 and two additional days. The owner photographs the condition, approves a written change order, and records a draw on contingency. Contingency had been $9,270; it is now $5,670. The owner updates the forecast and notes that 39 percent of contingency has been used with roughly 30 percent of work complete, a warning sign that triggers a review of remaining risk items and a decision to defer an optional upgrade worth $1,800.
:::

:::worksheet Weekly site meeting record
Date and attendees: ___
Phase and percent complete: ___
Schedule status (ahead / on / behind, days): ___
Inspections scheduled or passed: ___
Decisions needed from owner this week: ___
Change orders proposed (description, price, days): ___
Contingency remaining ($ and %): ___
Actions, owner, and due date: ___
:::

:::summary
Construction management is information and money control. Sequence trades correctly, hold weekly site meetings, keep a dated log, and re-forecast after demolition. Approve change orders only in writing, track contingency burn against progress, and keep permits and inspections current so they do not threaten the sale.
:::

# Chapter 10: Holding, Insurance, Entities, and Taxes

:::objectives
- Identify the insurance coverages a vacant renovation property needs.
- Understand the basic purposes of a business entity for flipping.
- Explain the dealer-versus-investor distinction in federal income tax.
- Set up records that make tax preparation and lender reporting straightforward.
:::

This chapter covers matters that are easy to postpone and expensive to get wrong. None of it replaces advice from a licensed insurance agent, attorney, or tax professional, but you should understand the issues well enough to ask good questions.

## Insurance

A standard homeowner's policy is designed for an owner-occupied home. A vacant house under renovation presents different risks: theft of materials, vandalism, fire during construction, water damage from open plumbing, and injury to workers or visitors. Many standard policies limit or exclude coverage when a property is vacant for an extended period. Ask your agent about builder's risk or vacant-dwelling coverage and general liability, and confirm that your contractors carry their own liability and workers' compensation coverage, as the FTC recommends verifying [8]. Your lender will usually require proof of coverage and will want to be named on the policy.

## Entities

Many flippers hold projects in a limited liability company or another entity. The usual goals are to separate business finances from personal ones, to present a professional face to lenders and partners, and to limit personal liability for business obligations. An entity does not eliminate risk; lenders frequently require personal guarantees, and courts can disregard an entity that is not run as a separate business. Formation and ongoing requirements differ by state. Discuss structure with an attorney and a tax professional before your first purchase.

## Dealer versus investor

The tax treatment of flipping profit is one of the most commonly misunderstood topics in the field. The IRS explains in Publication 544 that property held mainly for sale to customers in a trade or business is not a capital asset [10]. Courts weigh facts such as the purpose of acquisition, the frequency and continuity of sales, the extent of improvements, and selling efforts to decide whether a taxpayer is a dealer holding inventory or an investor holding a capital asset. Professional commentary from tax practitioners generally treats repeated fix-and-flip activity as dealer activity, which means the profit is typically taxed as ordinary income and may be subject to self-employment tax regardless of how long the property was held [11]. Even for an investor, a gain on property held one year or less is short-term and taxed at ordinary rates. The practical lesson is to budget for income tax on flip profit at your ordinary rate and to talk with a tax professional before your first sale.

## Records

Keep a separate bank account and card for each project or for the business. Record every expense with a receipt and a project code. Track renovation costs separately from repairs and from holding costs, because your tax professional may treat them differently. Keep closing statements from both purchase and sale, lender statements, contractor invoices, lien waivers, permits, and the project log in one folder per property.

| Record | Why you need it | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase closing statement | Establishes basis and acquisition costs | Title or escrow company |
| Contractor invoices and lien waivers | Supports renovation costs; protects against liens | Contractor and subcontractors |
| Loan statements | Documents interest and fees | Lender |
| Insurance declarations | Shows coverage and cost | Insurance agent |
| Utility and tax bills | Documents holding costs | Utilities, county |
| Sale closing statement | Establishes sale price and selling costs | Title or escrow company |

## Six steps to administrative readiness

1. Meet an insurance agent before closing and bind appropriate coverage effective at closing.
2. Decide on entity structure with an attorney and tax professional.
3. Open dedicated accounts.
4. Create a folder structure and expense codes for the project.
5. Estimate tax on projected profit and hold that amount aside when the property sells.
6. Calendar property-tax and insurance renewal dates.

:::example Illustrative: setting aside tax from a flip
A hypothetical sole proprietor expects a net profit of $30,000 from a flip. Based on a conversation with a tax professional, the owner assumes the profit will be treated as ordinary business income. The owner estimates a combined federal and state marginal income-tax rate of 30 percent and self-employment tax of roughly 14 percent of net profit, for a provisional reserve of about $13,200. The owner transfers that amount into a tax savings account at closing and asks the tax professional whether estimated tax payments are due. The percentages are placeholders, not tax advice; actual liability depends on the owner's full situation.
:::

:::checklist Administrative readiness
- Vacant or builder's risk coverage bound, with lender named.
- Contractors' liability and workers' compensation certificates on file.
- Entity decision documented with professional input.
- Dedicated bank account and card opened.
- Expense codes and project folder created.
- Tax reserve rule written down.
:::

:::summary
Vacant renovation properties need insurance designed for that risk. Entities can separate finances and limit some liability but are not a shield against poor practice. Under IRS rules, property held mainly for sale is not a capital asset, so repeat flippers should expect ordinary-income treatment and plan for it. Good records make every one of these issues easier.
:::

# Chapter 11: Marketing and Selling the Property

:::objectives
- Price the finished property using current comps and market absorption.
- Prepare the property and listing to compete for buyers online.
- Understand disclosure obligations and the paperwork buyers will request.
- Negotiate offers and manage the transaction to closing.
:::

The sale is where the plan is tested. A property priced and presented correctly typically attracts showings quickly. One that is overpriced or poorly presented sits, and every week on the market adds cost and invites lower offers.

## Pricing

Return to your comps, update them with sales that closed during construction, and look at current competition: active listings and pending sales at your price point. Market absorption, the number of months it would take to sell current inventory at the recent sales pace, tells you whether buyers or sellers have the upper hand. Price for the market you are in, not the one you hoped for when you bought.

## Presentation

Most buyers first encounter a property online, so photography and presentation matter. The National Association of Realtors' 2025 Profile of Home Staging found that 83 percent of buyers' agents said staging made it easier for buyers to visualize a property as a future home, that the living room, primary bedroom, and kitchen were the rooms most important to stage, and that 29 percent of agents reported staging was associated with a 1 to 10 percent increase in the dollar value offered [12][13]. These are survey perceptions, not guarantees, but they support investing in professional photography and at least partial staging of key rooms.

## Disclosure and documentation

Many states require sellers to disclose known material defects, and federal law requires lead-based paint disclosures for most housing built before 1978 [14]. As a flipper, you know more about the property than a typical owner, because you opened the walls. Disclose honestly and completely, and consult your agent or attorney about the forms required in your state. Prepare a buyer packet: permits and final inspections, a summary of work performed with dates, warranties on roof, HVAC, appliances, and workmanship, and receipts for major systems. A packet like this answers questions before they become objections.

## Negotiating offers

Compare offers on net proceeds and certainty, not price alone. An offer with a higher price but a large concession request, a low down payment, an appraisal gap, or a long closing period may be worse than a slightly lower clean offer. Remember FHA's resale timing rule from Chapter 7: if your buyer uses an FHA-insured loan, the contract date relative to your acquisition date matters [7].

## Seven steps to a clean sale

1. Update comps and study active competition.
2. Complete the punch list and a professional clean.
3. Stage key rooms and commission professional photographs.
4. Assemble the buyer packet and complete disclosures.
5. Set a list price and a review date for price adjustment.
6. Evaluate offers on net proceeds, financing strength, and contingencies.
7. Manage inspection requests and appraisal promptly through closing.

:::example Illustrative: comparing two offers
Offer A: $318,000, conventional loan with 5 percent down, buyer asks for $8,000 in closing-cost credits, 45-day close, inspection and appraisal contingencies. Offer B: $311,000, conventional loan with 20 percent down, no credits, 30-day close, inspection contingency only with appraisal-gap coverage up to $5,000. Net price before other costs: A is $310,000 after credits; B is $311,000. B also closes 15 days sooner, saving about $1,700 in interest and holding costs, and its larger down payment and appraisal-gap coverage reduce the risk of a failed closing. The owner accepts B.
:::

| Listing period | Indicator | Action to consider |
|---|---|---|
| First weekend | Few showings | Review photos, online description, and price |
| Weeks 1–2 | Showings but no offers | Collect feedback; address objections |
| Weeks 3–4 | No offers, competitors selling | Adjust price to the market |
| After an offer | Inspection requests | Respond quickly with documentation and fair credits |

:::checklist Ready to list
- Final inspection passed and permits closed.
- Punch list completed; professional clean done.
- Key rooms staged; professional photographs taken.
- Disclosures, including lead-based paint disclosure for pre-1978 homes, completed.
- Buyer packet prepared.
- List price and price-review date agreed with agent.
:::

:::summary
Price from current comps and market absorption, present the property well online, and disclose honestly. NAR's 2025 survey found most buyers' agents believe staging helps buyers visualize a home. Evaluate offers on net proceeds and certainty, not headline price, and manage the transaction actively to closing.

# Chapter 12: When Projects Go Wrong, and How to Improve

:::objectives
- Recognize early warning signs that a project is in trouble.
- Choose among exit alternatives when the original plan fails.
- Conduct a structured post-project review.
- Decide whether and how to take on the next project.
:::

Every experienced flipper has had a project that went wrong. The difference between a setback and a disaster is usually how early the owner recognized the problem and how decisively they acted.

## Early warning signs

| Signal | What it may mean |
|---|---|
| Contingency use outpacing progress | Scope underestimated or hidden conditions widespread |
| Contractor missing site meetings | Overcommitment or financial trouble |
| Repeated inspection failures | Quality or competence problem |
| Comparable listings cutting prices | Market softening |
| Your reserve below one month of costs | Insufficient liquidity to absorb further delay |

## Exit alternatives

When the original plan no longer works, there are usually several options. You can finish and sell at a lower price, accepting a smaller profit or a loss. You can sell the property unfinished to another investor, cutting further spending. You can refinance and rent the property if the numbers support it, converting a flip into a rental. Or you can pause work, which rarely helps because holding costs continue. Model each option's net result, including the cost of further time, and choose the one that minimizes loss or maximizes outcome given your capital. The Zillow case in Chapter 1 shows a large firm choosing to exit rather than continue a strategy whose risk it could not control [2].

## The post-project review

After every project, compare planned with actual results for each cost family, schedule, and sale price. Identify the three biggest variances and their root causes. Update your buy box, cost database, contingency rules, and team list. This review is how one project becomes a system.

## Scaling responsibly

Do not take on a second simultaneous project until you have completed at least one with a documented review and until your reserve can carry both through a delay scenario. Scaling multiplies both returns and mistakes.

## Five steps for a troubled project

1. Name the problem precisely and quantify its cost and schedule impact.
2. Re-run the deal analysis under each exit alternative.
3. Consult your lender early; surprises damage relationships.
4. Choose and commit to an exit, with dates.
5. Record lessons immediately while they are fresh.

:::example Illustrative: choosing an exit after a market shift
Midway through a hypothetical project, three comparable listings cut their prices and the owner's updated low ARV falls from $312,000 to $294,000. Remaining renovation spending is $28,000. Option 1, finish and sell: projected net −$2,500 after all costs. Option 2, sell unfinished to an investor at $238,000: projected net −$9,800. Option 3, finish, refinance, and rent: rent estimate $2,150 per month against a new loan payment, taxes, insurance, and maintenance of about $2,300, a negative monthly cash flow. The owner chooses Option 1, reduces optional finishes by $3,000, and lists two weeks earlier than planned.
:::

# Glossary

**After-repair value (ARV).** The estimated market value of a property after planned improvements, based on comparable sales.

**Allowance.** A fixed dollar amount in a scope or contract for an item whose specific selection will be made later.

**As-is.** A contract term under which the seller does not agree to make repairs; it does not by itself waive the buyer's right to inspect.

**Bid leveling.** Adjusting competing bids to a common scope so they can be compared fairly.

**Change order.** A written, priced amendment to a construction contract that changes scope, cost, or schedule.

**Comparable sale (comp).** A recent closed sale of a similar property used as evidence of value.

**Contingency.** Budgeted funds reserved for unforeseen conditions; also a contract condition allowing a party to cancel.

**Dealer.** For federal income tax purposes, a taxpayer who holds property primarily for sale to customers in a trade or business.

**Draw.** A disbursement of renovation loan funds after the lender verifies completed work.

**Gross profit (flipping).** Resale price minus purchase price, before renovation, financing, holding, and selling costs.

**Hard-money lender.** A short-term lender that underwrites primarily on the property and project, usually at higher cost than bank financing.

**Holding costs.** Ongoing costs of owning the property during the project, such as taxes, insurance, and utilities.

**Lien waiver.** A document in which a contractor, subcontractor, or supplier waives the right to file a lien for amounts paid.

**Market absorption.** The time it would take to sell current inventory at the recent pace of sales.

**Maximum allowable offer (MAO).** The highest purchase price at which a deal still meets the target profit after all costs.

**Point.** A loan fee equal to one percent of the loan amount.

**RRP Rule.** The EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule governing paid work that disturbs paint in pre-1978 homes.

**Scope of work.** A detailed written description of the renovation work, materials, and standards.

**Stop rule.** A pre-committed condition under which you will walk away from a deal.
