# How to Use This Book

The United States government is the largest single buyer of goods and services in the world, and Congress has directed that a meaningful share of its contract dollars go to small businesses. For a small firm, that creates a real but demanding opportunity. Government buyers follow written rules, publish many of their needs, and pay reliably, yet they also impose registration requirements, detailed proposal instructions, compliance obligations, and long sales cycles that can exhaust an unprepared company.

This book is written for owners and managers of small businesses who are considering federal contracting, and for staff responsible for building a government sales function. It treats the subject the way a university course in public procurement would: it explains the institutional logic first, then the procedures, and then the practical decisions a firm must make. It also draws on the practitioner's experience that most early failures come not from lack of opportunity but from poor fit, weak research, and cash-flow surprises.

## What you will be able to do

After working through this book you should be able to:

- Explain how federal agencies buy, including the main dollar thresholds and procurement methods.
- Assess whether your business is ready, in capability and cash, to pursue government work.
- Complete the core registrations: Unique Entity ID, SAM.gov, NAICS codes, and size determination.
- Determine which socioeconomic programs and set-asides, if any, you may be eligible for.
- Research agency buying history and forecasts to target realistic opportunities.
- Read a solicitation, build a compliance matrix, and decide whether to bid, partner, or pass.
- Price work with attention to cost, risk, and cash flow.
- Write a compliant, evaluator-friendly proposal and perform in a way that builds past performance.
- Understand how the SBIR and STTR programs fund innovative small firms.

## How each chapter is built

Each chapter opens with learning objectives, explains its concepts in plain language with definitions, and then gives a step-by-step framework. A worked example applies the framework with numbers; all worked examples are illustrative and hypothetical, and the firms in them are invented. Case studies describe real, publicly documented programs or companies, with sources in the References chapter; where a real company is discussed, its results are its own, not TTI's. Each chapter includes a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Appendices provide usable templates.

:::warning Scope and limits
This book is education only. It is not legal, accounting, or tax advice. Federal acquisition rules, dollar thresholds, and program requirements change; the Federal Acquisition Regulation itself is undergoing a substantial rewrite. Always read the current solicitation, consult SBA and agency guidance, and seek advice from an attorney, an accountant, or a no-cost APEX Accelerator counselor before you commit. No contract award, revenue, or result is promised.
:::

# Chapter 1: How the Federal Government Buys

:::objectives
- Describe the statutory small business contracting goals and recent results.
- Identify the main procurement methods and the dollar thresholds that govern them.
- Explain the roles of the contracting officer, program office, and small business specialists.
- Recognize where small firms most commonly enter the market.
:::

## A rules-based buyer

Unlike a private customer, a federal agency cannot simply choose a vendor it likes. Its purchasing is governed by statutes, the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), agency supplements, and the specific terms of each solicitation. The Congressional Research Service summarizes the basic process: acquisition personnel determine the agency's requirements, post solicitations on SAM.gov, receive offers, and evaluate them according to the FAR [6]. The rules exist to promote competition, fairness, and accountability for public money. For a small business, they mean that success depends heavily on following instructions exactly.

## Small business goals

By statute, the government-wide goal is that small businesses receive at least 23 percent of the dollar value of prime contract awards each fiscal year [2]. Additional goals cover small disadvantaged businesses (5 percent), women-owned small businesses (5 percent), service-disabled veteran-owned small businesses (5 percent), and HUBZone small businesses (3 percent) [2]. The SBA publishes an annual scorecard grading agencies on these goals [10].

:::case FY2025 small business procurement scorecard
In June 2026 the SBA announced that in fiscal year 2025 federal agencies awarded nearly 28 percent of prime contract dollars to small businesses, about $179 billion, exceeding the 23 percent goal; including subcontracts, small businesses received nearly $273 billion [1]. The Congressional Research Service reported the detailed FY2025 figures: 27.58 percent to small businesses, 11.60 percent to small disadvantaged businesses, 4.52 percent to women-owned small businesses, 5.01 percent to service-disabled veteran-owned small businesses, and 2.66 percent to HUBZone firms, meaning three of five prime goals were met or exceeded [2]. These are aggregate national figures; they say nothing about the odds that any particular firm will win.
:::

## Procurement methods and thresholds

The method an agency uses depends largely on the dollar value and nature of the purchase. A FAR inflation-adjustment rule effective October 1, 2025, raised two of the most important thresholds [7]:

- The **micro-purchase threshold** rose from $10,000 to $15,000 (with lower figures for certain construction and service contracts subject to labor statutes) [7].
- The **simplified acquisition threshold** rose from $250,000 to $350,000 [7].

| Method | Typical dollar range | How it works | Small business relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Micro-purchase (often by government purchase card) | Up to $15,000 generally [7] | Authorized cardholders buy directly with minimal competition | Entry point for local suppliers and service firms |
| Simplified acquisition procedures | Above micro-purchase up to $350,000 [7] | Streamlined quotes and evaluations | Frequently reserved for small businesses |
| Sealed bidding | Any amount | Award to lowest-priced responsive, responsible bidder | Common in construction |
| Negotiated contracting | Any amount | Evaluation of technical, past performance, and price factors | Most service contracts above the simplified threshold |
| Federal Supply Schedules (GSA MAS) | Any amount | Pre-negotiated contracts agencies order from | Requires obtaining a schedule contract |
| Task and delivery orders under existing contracts | Any amount | Orders placed against IDIQ contracts | Must first hold the parent contract |

CRS notes that agencies may also acquire goods without a new contract, for example by purchase card, by placing orders against existing contracts, or by ordering from a GSA schedule [6]. The SBA advises that agencies use SAM.gov to list contracts over $25,000 [5].

## The people involved

- **Contracting officer (CO).** The only official with authority to bind the government to a contract. Questions about a solicitation go to the CO or the designated contract specialist, in the manner the solicitation specifies.
- **Program or requiring office.** The people who need the product or service. They write requirements and often participate in evaluation.
- **Small business specialists.** Agencies have Offices of Small and Disadvantaged Business Utilization (OSDBU) or equivalent offices that advocate for small business participation.
- **Prime contractors.** Large firms holding major contracts, many of which must meet small business subcontracting goals.

## Where small firms usually enter

Most first federal revenue for small firms comes from one of four doors: micro-purchases and small simplified acquisitions; subcontracts under a prime contractor; set-aside competitions for which the firm qualifies; or, for research-intensive firms, SBIR and STTR awards. Large, complex, multi-year contracts are rarely a realistic first target.

:::example Illustrative: matching method to opportunity
A hypothetical janitorial company, Brightline Facility Services, has three leads: (a) a one-time $9,500 carpet cleaning at a federal building; (b) a $180,000 annual janitorial contract at a regional office; (c) a $4.2 million, five-year multi-site contract.

- Lead (a) falls under the $15,000 micro-purchase threshold [7], though services covered by the Service Contract Labor Standards statute may use a lower $2,500 threshold [7], so Brightline asks the contracting office which rules apply.
- Lead (b) is within simplified acquisition procedures and may be set aside for small businesses.
- Lead (c) is a full negotiated procurement; Brightline lacks past performance at that scale and decides to seek a subcontracting role with a prime instead.

The firm's first-year plan targets (a)-type and (b)-type work. All details are hypothetical.
:::

:::checklist Market orientation
- I can state the 23 percent small business goal and the four socioeconomic goals.
- I know the current micro-purchase and simplified acquisition thresholds and that they change.
- I understand that only a contracting officer can bind the government.
- I have identified which entry door fits my business today.
:::

:::summary
Federal buying is rules-based and goal-driven. Agencies collectively exceeded the 23 percent small business goal in FY2025, but goals do not guarantee awards to any firm. Procurement methods depend on dollar thresholds, which rose in October 2025 to $15,000 for micro-purchases and $350,000 for simplified acquisitions. Small firms commonly enter through micro-purchases, small set-asides, subcontracts, or SBIR/STTR.
:::

# Chapter 2: Readiness: Fit, Capacity, and Cash

:::objectives
- Evaluate whether the government buys what your business sells.
- Assess operational capacity to perform under federal terms.
- Model the cash-flow effect of government payment timing.
- Make a reasoned decision to proceed, prepare further, or wait.
:::

## Readiness before registration

The SBA's guidance begins with a simple premise: to bid on and win government contracts, you must sell products or services that the government buys, at a competitive price [3]. Registration is free and relatively quick, but pursuing contracts consumes significant time and money. A readiness assessment prevents a firm from spending months on proposals it cannot win or contracts it cannot afford to perform.

## Three dimensions of readiness

**Fit.** Does the government buy your product or service in meaningful quantities? Is it bought by agencies near you, or in a way you can serve remotely? Market research (Chapter 5) answers this with data rather than assumptions.

**Capacity.** Can you perform at the scale and standard a government buyer requires? Consider staff, equipment, quality control, insurance, bonding (for construction), security clearances (for some defense work), cybersecurity requirements, and the ability to produce documentation and reports.

**Cash.** Government customers typically pay after performance and after a properly submitted invoice is accepted. Meanwhile, you pay staff, suppliers, and overhead. A firm that is profitable on paper can fail if it cannot finance the gap.

## The readiness framework

**Step 1: Define your offering in government terms.** Write one paragraph describing what you sell, to whom, and with what differentiation. Identify candidate NAICS codes (Chapter 3).

**Step 2: Check demand.** Use public award data to see whether agencies have bought similar work in the last three years and at what volume (Chapter 5).

**Step 3: Inventory capacity.** List staff, certifications, insurance, equipment, and systems. Identify gaps that a typical solicitation would expose.

**Step 4: Model cash flow.** Estimate the working capital required to perform a representative contract for 60 to 90 days before receiving payment.

**Step 5: Decide.** Proceed, prepare (close specific gaps first), or wait.

## Capacity checklist by industry

| Industry | Common capacity requirements |
|---|---|
| Construction | Bonding capacity, licensing, safety program, prevailing-wage payroll compliance |
| Professional services | Qualified personnel with résumés, timekeeping system, conflict-of-interest procedures |
| IT and cyber | Security controls, possible certification requirements, cleared personnel for some work |
| Facilities services | Staffing depth, supervision, wage-determination compliance, insurance |
| Products and supplies | Supply chain reliability, country-of-origin compliance, delivery logistics |

## Cash-flow modeling

Working capital need can be estimated as monthly direct costs plus overhead, multiplied by the number of months between starting work and receiving payment, plus a buffer.

:::example Illustrative: the cash gap
A hypothetical IT support firm wins a $240,000 one-year task order billed monthly at $20,000. Its monthly costs for the work are $15,500 in labor and $1,500 in allocated overhead, total $17,000. The firm invoices at the end of each month, and payment arrives about 30 days after invoice acceptance.

- Month 1: spends $17,000; receives $0.
- Month 2: spends $17,000; receives $0 (Month 1 invoice still processing).
- Month 3: spends $17,000; receives $20,000.

Cumulative cash out by the time the first payment arrives: about $51,000 spent against $20,000 received, a gap of roughly $31,000. If the invoice is rejected for a formatting error and resubmitted, the gap widens. The firm arranges a line of credit of $50,000 before accepting the order. All figures are illustrative.
:::

:::worksheet Readiness assessment
Offering in one paragraph: ___
Candidate NAICS codes: ___
Evidence of government demand (agencies, amounts, years): ___
Capacity gaps identified: ___
Insurance and bonding status: ___
Estimated working capital for a representative contract: ___
Financing available (source, amount): ___
Decision (proceed / prepare / wait) and reason: ___
:::

:::summary
Readiness has three dimensions: fit with government demand, capacity to perform under federal terms, and cash to finance the gap between performance and payment. A structured assessment, including a cash-flow model, protects a small firm from expensive false starts.
:::

# Chapter 3: Registration and Identity

:::objectives
- Obtain a Unique Entity ID and complete SAM.gov registration.
- Select accurate NAICS codes for your offerings.
- Determine small business status using SBA size standards, including affiliation.
- Maintain registrations and avoid paid "registration" scams.
:::

## The identity layer

Before a firm can compete as a prime contractor, it must establish an official identity with the government. CRS notes that, with few exceptions, a firm that wants to compete for federal contracts must obtain a Unique Entity ID (UEI) and register with SAM [6]. Since April 4, 2022, the government has used the UEI generated in SAM.gov instead of the former DUNS number [6].

## Unique Entity ID

The SBA describes the UEI as a unique 12-character alphanumeric value issued when you register with SAM.gov [3]. GSA explains that some entities, such as certain subcontractors, may obtain a UEI without full registration, but those entities cannot bid as prime contractors [4].

## SAM.gov registration

GSA lists the main steps of SAM registration: create a user account; provide business details such as legal name and address and obtain a UEI; select NAICS codes; confirm size information; enter points of contact and payment information; and complete representations and certifications [4]. GSA advises starting early because validation can take several days, keeping registration active through final payment of any contract, and ensuring the business name, address, and ownership details match across IRS, SAM.gov, and SBA records [4]. CRS notes that registrations must be renewed annually [6].

:::warning Registration is free
GSA states plainly that you do not need to pay a third party to complete your SAM registration [4]. State APEX Accelerator offices regularly warn businesses about firms that solicit fees for SAM registration or imply official status. If you want help, no-cost assistance is available from APEX Accelerators [8].
:::

## NAICS codes

The SBA explains that NAICS codes classify businesses by the product or service they supply; a business generally has a primary code and may list several [3]. Each solicitation designates one NAICS code, which determines the size standard for that procurement [10]. Choose codes that accurately describe what you actually do, since contracting officers and primes search by code.

## Size standards and affiliation

The SBA assigns a size standard to each NAICS code. It notes that most manufacturers with 500 or fewer employees and most non-manufacturers with average annual receipts under $7.5 million qualify as small, with many industry exceptions [3]. Crucially, when calculating size, you must include the receipts and employees of affiliates [3]. CRS quotes the regulation: SBA counts the size of the concern and "all of its domestic and foreign affiliates" [6]. Affiliation can arise from common ownership, common management, or certain contractual relationships, so seek advice if your ownership is complex.

| Registration element | What it is | Source of truth | Renewal |
|---|---|---|---|
| UEI | 12-character entity identifier | SAM.gov [3] | Tied to SAM record |
| SAM registration | Entity profile, reps and certs, payment info | SAM.gov [4] | Annually [6] |
| CAGE code | Commercial and Government Entity code, assigned with registration | SAM.gov [4] | With SAM |
| NAICS codes | Industry classification | U.S. Census NAICS list; SBA table [3] | Update as offerings change |
| Size status | Small or other-than-small per NAICS | SBA size standards [3] | Recalculate as revenue grows |

## The registration framework

**Step 1: Gather documents.** EIN, legal name exactly as on IRS records, physical address, banking details for electronic payment, and ownership information [4].

**Step 2: Create the SAM account and request a UEI.**

**Step 3: Choose NAICS codes.** Primary plus any secondary codes you can truthfully perform.

**Step 4: Check size for each code** using the SBA size standards tool, including affiliates [3].

**Step 5: Complete representations and certifications** carefully; they are legal statements.

**Step 6: Complete your Small Business Search profile.** The SBA notes that information in SAM populates the Small Business Search database that contracting officers and primes use, and recommends a comprehensive profile with capabilities narrative, keywords, NAICS codes, and performance history [5][9].

**Step 7: Calendar renewal** at least 60 days before expiration.

:::example Illustrative: size and growth
A hypothetical engineering services firm lists NAICS 541330 (engineering services). Suppose the size standard for that code is an average annual receipts figure the firm currently falls below, using SBA's averaging method. The firm also owns 60 percent of a separate surveying company. Under affiliation rules, the firm must add the surveying company's receipts when computing size. Combined, the firms remain small for 541330 but would exceed a lower standard for a different code they had planned to list. They remove the code they cannot truthfully claim as small and seek advice before bidding set-asides under it. The figures and codes are for illustration; always check the current SBA table.
:::

:::checklist Registration complete
- Legal name and address match IRS records exactly.
- UEI obtained and SAM registration active.
- NAICS codes reflect real capabilities.
- Size determined for each code, including affiliates.
- Representations and certifications reviewed line by line.
- Small Business Search profile completed with narrative and keywords.
- Renewal date on calendar.
- No fees paid to third-party "registration" services.
:::

:::summary
Federal identity rests on the UEI, SAM.gov registration, NAICS codes, and size status. Registration is free, must be kept active and consistent with IRS records, and must be renewed annually. Size includes affiliates. A complete Small Business Search profile makes the firm discoverable to contracting officers and primes.
:::

# Chapter 4: Socioeconomic Programs and Set-Asides

:::objectives
- Explain what a set-aside is and why it reduces competition.
- Describe the SBA's main contracting assistance programs: 8(a), WOSB, HUBZone, and SDVOSB.
- Distinguish self-certification from formal certification.
- Evaluate whether pursuing a certification is worth the effort for your firm.
:::

## Set-asides

The SBA explains that some contracts are reserved for small businesses; these "small business set-asides" help small firms compete for and win federal contracts [5]. Certain contracts are further reserved for participants in specific SBA programs, and in some circumstances contracts may be awarded on a sole-source basis [5]. The SBA notes that participating in one of these programs means fewer businesses to compete with, but that a firm must meet eligibility requirements and certify its status before bidding on such set-asides [5].

## The main programs

| Program | Basic idea | Statutory goal (share of dollars) [2] | Certification path |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small business set-aside | Competition limited to firms small under the solicitation's NAICS code | 23% (prime) | Self-represent size in SAM [10] |
| 8(a) Business Development | Multi-year program for firms owned by socially and economically disadvantaged individuals | Small disadvantaged business goal: 5% | Formal SBA application |
| Women-Owned Small Business (WOSB/EDWOSB) | Set-asides in industries where women-owned firms are underrepresented | 5% | Formal SBA certification |
| HUBZone | Firms located in, and employing residents of, Historically Underutilized Business Zones | 3% | Formal SBA certification |
| Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned (SDVOSB) | Firms owned and controlled by service-disabled veterans | 5% | SBA certification |

The SBA notes that for some programs you can self-certify by updating your SAM profile, while for others you must apply, answer questions about ownership and control, and upload supporting documents [5]. Program rules on ownership, control, economic disadvantage, location, and employee residency are detailed and change; consult SBA's current guidance and the certification portal before applying.

## The certification decision framework

**Step 1: Screen eligibility honestly.** Ownership and control must be genuine. Arrangements designed to appear eligible when the real control lies elsewhere can lead to serious penalties.

**Step 2: Check market relevance.** Use award data to see how much your target agencies spend through each set-aside type in your NAICS codes (Chapter 5).

**Step 3: Estimate effort.** Count the time to assemble documents, respond to SBA questions, and maintain eligibility.

**Step 4: Decide and sequence.** Pursue the certification that opens the most relevant spending first.

:::example Illustrative: is HUBZone worth it?
A hypothetical landscaping firm sits in a census tract it believes is a HUBZone. It finds that its target agency, a regional military installation, awarded a meaningful share of grounds maintenance contracts as HUBZone set-asides over the last three years. HUBZone status requires not only a principal office in a HUBZone but also a share of employees living in HUBZones, so the firm reviews where its 14 employees live and finds it would need to adjust hiring to qualify reliably. The owner decides to pursue the certification while documenting residency carefully. All details are illustrative; the firm would verify current HUBZone rules with SBA.
:::

:::case FY2025 goal performance as a signal
CRS reported that in FY2025 the government met or exceeded the small business, small disadvantaged business, and SDVOSB prime goals but fell short on the women-owned (4.52 percent versus 5 percent) and HUBZone (2.66 percent versus 3 percent) goals [2]. Agencies are graded on these results [10]. Shortfalls can motivate agency small business offices to seek capable firms in under-achieved categories. This is a reason to understand agency goals, not a promise that a certified firm will win work.
:::

:::checklist Certification readiness
- I have read SBA's current eligibility rules for each program I am considering.
- Ownership and control of my firm genuinely satisfy those rules.
- I have evidence that my target agencies use the relevant set-aside in my NAICS codes.
- I have assembled ownership documents, tax returns, and other required records.
- I understand ongoing eligibility obligations.
:::

:::summary
Set-asides reserve competitions for small businesses or for participants in SBA programs such as 8(a), WOSB, HUBZone, and SDVOSB. They narrow competition but require genuine eligibility and, for most programs, formal certification. Whether to pursue a certification depends on eligibility, market relevance, and effort.
:::

# Chapter 5: Market Research: Finding Your Buyers

:::objectives
- Use SAM.gov, USAspending.gov, and procurement forecasts to research demand.
- Identify which agencies, offices, and prime contractors buy what you sell.
- Analyze incumbents, contract vehicles, and recompete timing.
- Build a target list of realistic opportunities.
:::

## Research replaces guessing

The SBA encourages prospective contractors to use resources such as USAspending.gov and agency procurement forecasts to determine whether the government is buying their products or services, and to research the Federal Procurement Data System and USAspending.gov to understand the needs of agencies and primes [5]. Historical award information is available on SAM.gov and USAspending.gov [9]. The goal of research is a short, prioritized list of buyers and opportunities, not a long list of every posting.

## Sources

| Source | What it shows | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| SAM.gov Contract Opportunities | Current solicitations, sources sought, presolicitation notices | Find active and upcoming opportunities [5] |
| USAspending.gov | Historical awards by agency, recipient, NAICS, location | Size demand; identify incumbents |
| Federal Procurement Data System (FPDS) | Detailed contract action data | Analyze set-aside use and vehicles [6] |
| Agency procurement forecasts | Planned acquisitions | Prepare months in advance |
| Small Business Search (SBS) | Profiles of small firms | See competitors; ensure your profile is complete [9] |
| SBA SUBNet | Subcontracting opportunities posted by primes | Find subcontract leads [9] |
| GSA Schedules | Pre-negotiated vehicles | Understand how agencies buy your category [9] |

## Notice types worth understanding

- **Sources sought / request for information (RFI).** The agency is gathering market information. Responding is one of the most influential low-cost actions a small firm can take, because responses can help an agency decide whether to set aside a procurement.
- **Presolicitation notice.** Announces a forthcoming solicitation.
- **Solicitation (RFQ, RFP, IFB).** The formal request for quotes, proposals, or bids.
- **Award notice.** Identifies the winner and often the amount; useful for competitive intelligence.

## The market research framework

**Step 1: Define search terms.** NAICS codes, product service codes, and keywords.

**Step 2: Pull three to five years of awards** in your codes and geography.

**Step 3: Rank agencies and offices** by dollars and number of awards.

**Step 4: Identify incumbents and contract end dates.** Expiring contracts signal recompetes.

**Step 5: Note set-aside patterns** and contract vehicles used.

**Step 6: Build a target list** of five to ten offices and three to five primes.

**Step 7: Track in a pipeline** with dates for forecasted releases.

:::example Illustrative: from data to a target list
A hypothetical commercial printing firm searches award data for its NAICS code within 150 miles. It finds 212 awards over four years totaling about $9.8 million, but 61 percent of dollars went through a single GSA schedule category, and the firm holds no schedule. Of the remaining awards, 84 were under $25,000, mostly from two military installations and a regional VA medical center. Twelve awards between $50,000 and $200,000 were small business set-asides, with three incumbents.

The firm's target list: (1) the two installations' purchasing offices for small orders, (2) the VA center, (3) responses to any sources-sought notices in its code, and (4) a longer-term evaluation of obtaining a GSA schedule. All figures are illustrative.
:::

Market research uses public award data, forecasts, and notices to identify where demand exists, who the incumbents are, which vehicles are used, and when recompetes occur. # Chapter 6: Capability Statements and Relationships

:::objectives
- Write a one-page capability statement tailored to government buyers.
- Engage agency small business offices and APEX Accelerators.
- Prepare for industry days and matchmaking events.
- Follow communication rules during active procurements.
:::

## Why relationships matter in a rules-based system

Formal competitions are decided on the written record, but relationships still matter before a solicitation is released. Agency small business specialists can explain how their agency buys, program offices can learn what capabilities exist in the market, and primes can identify subcontractors. Once a solicitation is released, communication typically must go through the designated contracting official, and informal contact with evaluators is inappropriate.

## The capability statement

A capability statement is a one-page summary of what your firm does for government customers. It should include:

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Header | Company name, logo, contact, website |
| Core competencies | Three to five specific capabilities, in the buyer's language |
| Differentiators | What makes you distinct: method, speed, certification, location |
| Past performance | Recent relevant projects, government or commercial, with measurable scope |
| Company data | UEI, CAGE code, NAICS codes, socioeconomic status, GSA schedule if any |
| Call to action | How to reach you |

Avoid generic claims such as "quality service" or "customer focus." Use specifics: the number of sites served, response times achieved, certifications held.

## Engaging the right people

- **APEX Accelerators.** The SBA describes these, formerly called Procurement Technical Assistance Centers, as offering local counseling and training at no cost or nominal cost, administered by the Department of Defense; they can help assess readiness, register, identify certifications, research past contracts, and support bidding and performance [8].
- **OSDBU offices.** Agency small business offices host outreach events and can explain buying patterns.
- **Prime contractors' small business liaisons.** Primes post subcontracting opportunities on SUBNet and search Small Business Search to find partners [9].
- **SBA resources.** The SBA's counseling page points to APEX Accelerators and other partners, and offers email support for prime and subcontracting questions [8].

## The outreach framework

**Step 1:** Finalize your capability statement and SBS profile.

**Step 2:** Schedule an APEX Accelerator consultation.

**Step 3:** Request brief introductory meetings with OSDBU staff at target agencies.

**Step 4:** Attend industry days and matchmaking events with specific goals.

**Step 5:** Follow up within 48 hours with a tailored note and your capability statement.

**Step 6:** Log every contact in your pipeline.

:::example Illustrative: a tailored versus generic statement
Generic: "We provide high-quality cybersecurity services with a customer-first approach."

Tailored: "We perform vulnerability assessments and remediation for small and mid-sized federal offices. In the past 24 months we completed 31 assessments for commercial health clinics averaging 45 endpoints each, with a median remediation plan delivered in 6 business days. Two staff hold [named industry certification]."

The tailored version gives an evaluator measurable facts. All details are hypothetical.
:::

:::checklist Outreach readiness
- Capability statement fits on one page and uses specific facts.
- UEI, CAGE, NAICS, and socioeconomic status are listed.
- APEX Accelerator appointment scheduled.

# Chapter 7: Reading a Solicitation

:::objectives
- Identify the main parts of a federal solicitation.
- Extract instructions, evaluation criteria, and requirements accurately.
- Build a compliance matrix.
- Submit questions properly and track amendments.
:::

## The solicitation is the rulebook

Every solicitation tells offerors exactly what to submit, how it will be evaluated, and what the contract will require. CRS explains that agencies evaluate offers in accordance with the FAR, using either sealed bidding or negotiated contracting, and that for negotiated procurements each agency chooses its own non-price evaluation factors [6]. Reading the solicitation carefully, more than once, is the highest-return activity in the entire process.

## Typical structure

Many solicitations follow the Uniform Contract Format or a commercial-item format. Common elements include:

| Element | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Cover form and schedule of items | What is being bought, quantities, contract line items |
| Statement of work / performance work statement | The work to be performed and standards |
| Contract clauses | Legal terms, including FAR and agency clauses |
| Instructions to offerors | Format, page limits, volumes, deadlines, delivery method |
| Evaluation criteria | Factors, their relative importance, and the basis for award |
| Attachments | Wage determinations, pricing sheets, forms |
| Amendments | Changes issued after release |

## Basis for award

Two common bases for negotiated procurements are:

- **Lowest price technically acceptable (LPTA).** Award goes to the lowest-priced offer that meets minimum technical requirements.
- **Best value tradeoff.** The agency may award to a higher-priced offer if its technical merit or past performance justifies the price difference, as described in the evaluation criteria.

Your proposal strategy depends heavily on which basis applies.

## Building a compliance matrix

A compliance matrix lists every requirement and instruction, where it appears, where your proposal addresses it, and who is responsible. It prevents the most common cause of losing: being found non-compliant.

:::example Illustrative: compliance matrix excerpt
| Solicitation ref. | Requirement | Proposal section | Owner | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Instr. 3.1 | Technical volume not to exceed 15 pages, 12-pt font | Vol. I | Lead writer | Drafting |
| Instr. 3.2 | Include résumés for key personnel (2) | Vol. I, App. A | HR | Collected |
| PWS 4.2 | Respond to service calls within 4 hours | Vol. I, Sec. 2.3 | Ops lead | Drafted |
| Eval. Factor 2 | Three past performance references within 5 years | Vol. II | BD lead | 2 of 3 |
| Attachment 3 | Complete pricing sheet, all CLINs | Vol. III | Finance | Not started |
| Amend. 0001 | Revised due date and Q&A responses | All | BD lead | Reviewed |

This hypothetical matrix shows a missing reference and an unstarted pricing sheet, both of which must be resolved before submission.
:::

## The solicitation reading framework

**Step 1: First read for go/no-go.** Note scope, basis for award, set-aside status, NAICS, due date, and place of performance.

**Step 2: Second read for requirements.** Highlight every "shall," "must," and instruction.

**Step 3: Build the compliance matrix.**

**Step 4: Submit questions** by the deadline in the manner instructed.

**Step 5: Monitor for amendments** and acknowledge them as required.

:::checklist Solicitation review
- I identified the basis for award and evaluation factors.
- I recorded page limits, formatting, volumes, and delivery method.
- I listed every requirement in a compliance matrix.
- I noted the question deadline and submission deadline in my calendar.
- I checked for wage determinations and special clauses.
- I set up monitoring for amendments.
:::

:::summary
The solicitation defines what to submit, how it will be judged, and what performance requires. Understanding its structure, the basis for award, and every instruction, captured in a compliance matrix, is the foundation of a competitive and compliant proposal.

# Chapter 8: Bid, No-Bid, and Teaming

:::objectives
- Apply a structured bid/no-bid scorecard.
- Evaluate subcontracting and teaming as alternatives to prime bids.
- Understand the basics of teaming agreements and joint ventures.
- Allocate proposal resources to the best opportunities.
:::

## The discipline of saying no

Proposals are expensive. A small firm that bids on everything spreads its limited capacity thin and produces weaker proposals. A disciplined bid/no-bid process concentrates effort where the firm has a realistic chance and the work fits its strategy.

## Bid/no-bid criteria

| Criterion | Questions | Weight (example) |
|---|---|---|
| Customer knowledge | Do we know the agency and its needs? Did we respond to the RFI? | 20% |
| Technical fit | Can we perform every requirement well? | 20% |
| Past performance | Do we have relevant, recent references? | 20% |
| Competitive position | Who is the incumbent? Is it a set-aside we qualify for? | 15% |
| Price competitiveness | Can we price to win and still profit? | 15% |
| Strategic value | Does it build capability or relationships we want? | 10% |

Score each criterion from 1 to 5, multiply by weight, and set a threshold (for example, 3.5) below which you do not bid unless there is an explicit strategic reason.

## Subcontracting and teaming

The SBA notes that large primes post subcontracting opportunities on SUBNet and use Small Business Search to find small business partners [9]. Subcontracting lets a small firm gain federal past performance, learn compliance requirements, and build relationships with less risk. Teaming can take several forms:

- **Prime-subcontractor teaming agreement.** The prime submits the offer and subcontracts part of the work to team members.
- **Joint venture.** Two or more firms form a venture to pursue a contract; special SBA rules govern joint ventures for set-asides and size.
- **Mentor-protégé arrangements.** SBA and some agencies operate programs in which experienced firms assist small firms; specific eligibility rules apply.

Teaming and joint ventures raise affiliation and limitations-on-subcontracting questions; consult an attorney before signing.

:::example Illustrative: scoring two opportunities
A hypothetical environmental consulting firm scores two opportunities:

| Criterion (weight) | Opportunity A score | Opportunity B score |
|---|---|---|
| Customer knowledge (20%) | 4 | 2 |
| Technical fit (20%) | 5 | 4 |
| Past performance (20%) | 3 | 2 |
| Competitive position (15%) | 4 | 2 |
| Price competitiveness (15%) | 3 | 3 |
| Strategic value (10%) | 3 | 5 |
| **Weighted score** | **3.75** | **2.85** |

Opportunity A scores 0.8 + 1.0 + 0.6 + 0.6 + 0.45 + 0.3 = 3.75. Opportunity B scores 0.4 + 0.8 + 0.4 + 0.3 + 0.45 + 0.5 = 2.85. The firm bids A as a prime and approaches the likely primes for B to propose a subcontract role, since B has high strategic value but a weak competitive position. All scores are illustrative.
:::

# Chapter 9: Pricing and Cash Flow

:::objectives
- Build a price from direct costs, indirect costs, and profit.
- Understand common contract types and their risk to the contractor.
- Account for wage determinations and other mandated costs.
- Protect cash flow through invoicing discipline and financing.
:::

## Price is evaluated, but cost is what you live with

Evaluators look at price; your firm lives with cost. Underpricing to win can lock a small firm into a loss for years. Overpricing loses the competition. Disciplined pricing starts from a realistic cost build-up and then tests competitiveness against market information.

## Cost elements

| Element | Examples |
|---|---|
| Direct labor | Wages of staff performing the work |
| Fringe benefits | Payroll taxes, insurance, paid leave, retirement |
| Other direct costs | Materials, travel, equipment specific to the contract |
| Subcontracts | Work performed by team members |
| Overhead | Facilities, supervision, and support costs tied to operations |
| General and administrative (G&A) | Company-wide management, accounting, business development |
| Profit or fee | Return for risk and capital |

## Contract types and risk

| Contract type | How payment works | Contractor risk |
|---|---|---|
| Firm-fixed-price | Fixed amount for defined deliverables | High: cost overruns are yours |
| Time-and-materials / labor-hour | Fixed hourly rates times hours, plus materials | Moderate: rates must cover all costs |
| Cost-reimbursement | Allowable costs plus a fee | Lower cost risk, but requires an adequate accounting system |
| Indefinite-delivery/indefinite-quantity (IDIQ) | Orders placed over time within limits | Volume uncertainty |

Most small firms begin with firm-fixed-price or time-and-materials work. Cost-reimbursement contracts typically require accounting systems capable of segregating allowable and unallowable costs.

## Mandated costs

Service contracts may include a wage determination under the Service Contract Labor Standards statute, and construction contracts may include prevailing-wage requirements. These set minimum wages and fringe benefits for covered workers. The FAR thresholds for these labor statutes are not adjusted with other inflation adjustments [7]. Read the wage determination attached to the solicitation and build it into labor costs.

## The pricing framework

**Step 1: Define scope and staffing** from the statement of work.

**Step 2: Build direct costs** using wage determinations where applicable.

**Step 3: Apply indirect rates** from your accounting records.

**Step 4: Add profit** appropriate to risk.

**Step 5: Compare with market data**, such as prior award amounts and published schedule rates.

**Step 6: Decide** whether the competitive price is acceptable; if not, consider a no-bid.

:::example Illustrative: building a fixed price
A hypothetical firm prices a one-year help desk contract requiring two full-time technicians.

| Line | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Direct labor | 2 × 2,080 hrs × $28.00 | $116,480 |
| Fringe (32% of labor) | $116,480 × 0.32 | $37,274 |
| Other direct costs | Software licenses, phones | $6,000 |
| Overhead (12% of labor + fringe) | $153,754 × 0.12 | $18,450 |
| Subtotal | | $178,204 |
| G&A (10% of subtotal) | $178,204 × 0.10 | $17,820 |
| Total cost | | $196,024 |
| Profit (8%) | $196,024 × 0.08 | $15,682 |
| **Price** | | **$211,706** |

Prior awards for similar work ranged from $195,000 to $225,000, so the price appears competitive. If the attached wage determination required a higher wage, the firm would rebuild the estimate rather than absorb the difference. All figures are illustrative.
:::

## Cash discipline

- Submit invoices exactly as the contract requires, through the designated system, with all required data.
- Track invoice status weekly.
- Arrange financing, such as a business line of credit, before performance begins (Chapter 2).
- Keep subcontractor payment terms aligned with your own receipt of payment where lawful and agreed.

:::checklist Pricing review
- Staffing matches the statement of work.
- Wage determination applied where required.
- Indirect rates come from actual records, not guesses.
- Profit reflects contract-type risk.
- Price compared with available market data.
- Invoicing requirements and financing are in place.
:::

:::summary
Sound pricing builds from direct labor, fringe, other direct costs, subcontracts, overhead, G&A, and profit, adjusted for contract-type risk and mandated wages. Market comparison tests competitiveness. Invoicing discipline and pre-arranged financing protect cash flow.
:::

# Chapter 10: Writing the Proposal

:::objectives
- Organize a proposal around the solicitation's instructions and evaluation factors.
- Write technical content that is specific, verifiable, and easy to evaluate.
- Present past performance persuasively.
- Run a review and quality-control process before submission.
:::

## Write for the evaluator

Evaluators often read many proposals against a scoring guide derived from the evaluation criteria. A proposal that mirrors the solicitation's structure, uses its terminology, and makes compliance easy to verify is easier to score well. Clever marketing language that obscures whether a requirement is met can hurt.

## Principles of proposal writing

1. **Follow the instructions literally.** Use the required volumes, headings, page limits, and formats.
2. **Map to evaluation factors.** Each factor and subfactor should have a clearly labelled response.
3. **Be specific.** State how you will perform, with whom, using what methods, and how you will measure quality.
4. **Prove claims.** Support assertions with data, past performance, or credentials.
5. **Highlight benefits to the agency.** Explain why your approach reduces risk or improves outcomes for the mission.
6. **Make it scannable.** Use headings, short paragraphs, tables, and graphics within the rules.

## Technical approach structure

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Understanding of the requirement | Restate the agency's objectives and challenges in your own words |
| Approach and methodology | Step-by-step performance, tools, schedules |
| Staffing and key personnel | Roles, qualifications, availability |
| Quality control | Inspection, metrics, corrective action |
| Transition plan | How you will start smoothly, especially when replacing an incumbent |
| Risk management | Identified risks and mitigations |

## Past performance

Choose references that are recent, relevant in scope and size, and from customers who will respond. If you lack federal past performance, many solicitations allow commercial or state and local references, and some allow past performance of key personnel or team members; follow the solicitation's rules.

## The proposal process

**Step 1: Kickoff** with the compliance matrix and an outline matching the instructions.

**Step 2: Storyboard** key themes and proof points for each factor.

**Step 3: Draft** each section.

**Step 4: Review** against the evaluation criteria (often called a "red team" review).

**Step 5: Revise and finalize pricing.**

**Step 6: Compliance check** against the matrix.

**Step 7: Submit early** through the required method and retain proof of timely submission.

:::example Illustrative: rewriting a weak technical paragraph
Weak: "Our experienced team will provide excellent maintenance services using industry best practices to ensure customer satisfaction."

Strong: "We will perform preventive maintenance on all 46 HVAC units listed in Attachment 2 on the quarterly schedule in PWS 3.1. A dedicated lead technician, certified as required by PWS 5.2, will schedule work through a computerized maintenance management system, which logs each task and generates the monthly report required by CDRL A002. Emergency calls will receive on-site response within 4 hours, as required by PWS 4.2, supported by two on-call technicians within 25 miles of the site."

The strong version cites the requirements, explains the method, and makes compliance easy to verify. Details are hypothetical.
:::

:::checklist Pre-submission quality control
- Every instruction and requirement in the matrix is addressed.
- Page limits, fonts, and file formats are compliant.
- Each evaluation factor has a clearly labelled response.
- Claims are supported by specific evidence.
- Pricing matches the technical approach and staffing.
- All amendments are acknowledged.
- Submission method tested; proof of submission retained.
:::

:::summary
A strong proposal follows instructions literally, maps to evaluation factors, makes specific and verifiable claims, and presents relevant past performance. A structured process with a review stage and a final compliance check reduces the risk of avoidable loss.
:::

An award starts a relationship governed by the contract. Performance quality determines payment, future past-performance ratings, and reputation within the agency. A small firm's first contract is especially important because it becomes the reference for many future bids.

# Chapter 12: The Innovation Path: SBIR and STTR

:::objectives
- Describe the purpose and phases of the SBIR and STTR programs.
- Analyze how Qualcomm and iRobot used early SBIR funding.
- Decide whether your firm's research fits these programs.
- Plan for commercialization beyond the award.
:::

## What SBIR and STTR are

The Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) and Small Business Technology Transfer (STTR) programs fund research and development by small firms through participating federal agencies. They generally proceed in phases: a feasibility phase, a development phase, and commercialization, which is funded from sources other than the SBIR/STTR set-aside. Because the program funds work at the feasibility stage, it can reach ideas earlier than most private investors are willing to. Agencies publish topics describing the problems they want solved, and proposals respond to those topics.

:::case Qualcomm
According to SBIR.gov, Qualcomm in 1985 was a small company providing research and development to the government, and SBIR awards were the young company's first funding, enabling it to hire engineers and begin developing chips; participating agencies including the National Science Foundation and the Defense Department awarded the company $1.5 million [11]. A National Academies study reports that Qualcomm received eight Phase I and four Phase II awards in its first five years, and quotes co-founder Irwin Jacobs describing SBIR as one of the critical "stamps of approval" that helped the company pursue private capital [12]. Qualcomm's results are its own and reflect many factors beyond SBIR funding.
:::

:::case iRobot
The National Academies study describes how SBIR funding in the mid-to-late 1990s helped iRobot build expertise across several technologies, and how a 1998 DARPA research contract helped fund development of the PackBot, its first commercially successful product; the study notes that only one of the proposed products became a commercial success [12]. An SBIR.gov success story reports total Defense Department SBIR investment in iRobot of $10.2 million, and notes that in 2016 the company sold its defense division for $45 million to focus on consumer robots such as the Roomba [13]. Early public funding opened a market; it did not guarantee any particular long-term outcome. iRobot's results are its own.
:::

## Lessons from the cases

| Lesson | Qualcomm | iRobot |
|---|---|---|
| SBIR funded risky early R&D | First funding for chip development [11] | Multiple early technologies [12] |
| Not every project succeeded | Program portfolio evolved [12] | Only one of several proposed products succeeded [12] |
| Commercialization required other capital and markets | Private capital followed [12] | Military demand, then consumer products [12] |
| Government customer as first market | Government R&D contracts [11] | Defense robots [12] |

## Is SBIR right for you?

SBIR and STTR fit firms performing genuine research and development with a plausible path to a product or service that government or commercial customers will buy. They do not fit firms seeking general operating funds or offering established services. Proposals are competitive and technical; the effort is substantial.

## The SBIR evaluation framework

**Step 1:** Review agency topics in your technical area.

**Step 2:** Assess whether your innovation directly addresses a topic.

**Step 3:** Evaluate team qualifications and research facilities.

**Step 4:** Draft a commercialization hypothesis: who buys the result, and how.

**Step 5:** Consult agency program managers when the topic rules allow pre-release questions.

**Step 6:** Decide and plan the proposal schedule.

:::example Illustrative: topic fit screening
A hypothetical sensor startup reviews 40 topics in a recent solicitation cycle. Three match its core technology. For each, it scores technical fit (1–5), team expertise (1–5), and commercialization path (1–5). Topic 1 scores 5, 4, 4; Topic 2 scores 3, 3, 2; Topic 3 scores 4, 5, 3. The firm writes proposals for Topics 1 and 3 and passes on Topic 2. Scores are illustrative.
:::

:::checklist SBIR readiness
- My work is genuine research and development, not routine services.
- I have identified specific agency topics that match.
- My team has qualified technical personnel.
- I have a written commercialization hypothesis.
- I understand eligibility rules and registration requirements for SBIR.
:::

:::summary
SBIR and STTR fund early-stage research by small firms in response to agency topics. Qualcomm and iRobot illustrate how early awards can support risky R&D, but both cases show that commercialization required other capital, markets, and persistence, and that not every project succeeds.
:::

# Chapter 13: A Twelve-Month Entry Plan

:::objectives
- Sequence the activities in this book into a realistic first-year plan.
- Define metrics for pipeline health and capture effectiveness.

## Why twelve months

Government sales cycles are long. Forecasted opportunities may be released months after they first appear, evaluations take time, and first awards often come from small purchases or subcontracts. A twelve-month horizon allows a fair test.

## The plan

| Quarter | Focus | Key outputs |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | Readiness and registration | Readiness assessment, UEI and SAM, NAICS and size, SBS profile, capability statement, APEX consultation |
| Q2 | Research and relationships | Market research log, target list, OSDBU meetings, two to four sources-sought responses, prime outreach |
| Q3 | Bidding and teaming | Bid/no-bid scorecards, two to four proposals or quotes, one or more teaming agreements |
| Q4 | Performance and review | Perform any awards, debriefings, lessons learned, certification decisions, year-two plan |

## Metrics

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| Qualified opportunities | Opportunities scoring above your bid threshold |
| Sources-sought responses | Count submitted |
| Proposals submitted | Count and total value |
| Win rate | Awards ÷ proposals (by count and value) |
| Pipeline value | Sum of qualified opportunity values weighted by probability |
| Cost of pursuit | Hours and dollars spent per proposal |
| Revenue from government work | Invoiced and collected |

:::example Illustrative: first-year scorecard
A hypothetical firm's first year: 14 qualified opportunities, 6 sources-sought responses, 5 proposals worth a combined $1.1 million, 1 award worth $92,000, plus 2 micro-purchase orders totaling $21,000 and one subcontract worth $60,000. Win rate by count: 1 ÷ 5 = 20 percent. Cost of pursuit: about 320 hours. The firm concludes its subcontract and small-purchase channels are working and its prime proposals need stronger past performance; it plans year two accordingly. Figures are illustrative.
:::

## Decision points

- **Continue** if the pipeline is growing, at least some revenue has been earned, and costs of pursuit are sustainable.
- **Refocus** if effort is spread across too many agencies or codes; narrow to the strongest two or three.
- **Pause** if the firm lacks capacity or cash to perform or pursue work responsibly.

- Metrics reviewed monthly.
:::

:::summary
A twelve-month plan sequences readiness, registration, research, relationships, bidding, teaming, and performance. Monthly metrics and explicit decision points keep the effort disciplined and allow the firm to continue, refocus, or pause on evidence.

# Appendix E: Sources-Sought Response Outline

A sources-sought response is usually short. Follow any format the notice specifies; otherwise use this outline.

1. **Company information.** Name, UEI, CAGE, address, point of contact, size status under the notice's NAICS code, and any socioeconomic certifications.
2. **Statement of interest.** One sentence confirming interest in the requirement as a prime or subcontractor.
3. **Capability narrative.** Two to four paragraphs addressing each capability the notice asks about, with specific evidence.
4. **Relevant experience.** Two or three brief project summaries with scope, dates, and customer type.
5. **Answers to specific questions.** Respond to every question in the notice, in order.
6. **Contract vehicle information.** Any GSA schedule or other vehicle held.
7. **Recommendations (if invited).** Constructive suggestions about requirements or acquisition strategy that would increase small business participation.

# Glossary

**8(a) Business Development Program.** SBA program for small businesses owned by socially and economically disadvantaged individuals.

**APEX Accelerator.** Locally operated, Department of Defense-administered assistance centers that help businesses pursue government contracts, formerly called PTACs.

**CAGE code.** Commercial and Government Entity code assigned to registered entities.

**Contracting officer.** Government official with authority to enter into, administer, and terminate contracts.

**Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR).** The primary regulation governing federal acquisition.

**HUBZone.** Historically Underutilized Business Zone program for firms located in and employing residents of designated areas.

**IDIQ.** Indefinite-delivery/indefinite-quantity contract under which orders are placed over time.

**LPTA.** Lowest price technically acceptable basis for award.

**Micro-purchase threshold.** Dollar level below which purchases may be made with minimal competition; generally $15,000 after October 1, 2025.

**NAICS code.** North American Industry Classification System code classifying a business's products or services.

**Set-aside.** A competition reserved for small businesses or a specific category of small business.

**Simplified acquisition threshold.** Dollar level below which simplified procedures may be used; generally $350,000 after October 1, 2025.

**SBIR/STTR.** Federal programs funding research and development by small businesses.

**Sources sought.** A market research notice seeking information about potential offerors.

**UEI.** Unique Entity ID, a 12-character identifier issued through SAM.gov.
