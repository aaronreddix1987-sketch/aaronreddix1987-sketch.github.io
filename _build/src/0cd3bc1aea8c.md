# How to Use This Book

This book is for people considering starting or joining an insurance agency in the United States: career changers, employees of existing agencies who want to own a book of business, and small-business owners who want to add insurance services. It explains how the industry distributes insurance, how producer licensing works, how agencies obtain the right to sell carriers' products, how agency revenue and costs behave, and how to build intake, compliance, and service processes that protect customers and the business.

It is deliberately cautious. Insurance is regulated state by state, and the details that matter most (license requirements, fees, appointment rules, advertising rules, and what unlicensed staff may do) vary by jurisdiction and change over time. This book teaches the structure so you know which questions to ask; your state department of insurance, the carriers you work with, and qualified advisers supply the answers that apply to you.

Each chapter includes learning objectives, plain-language definitions, a step-by-step framework, a worked example labelled "Illustrative" with numbers you can check, a real case where relevant, a table, a checklist or worksheet, a summary, and review questions. Case studies describe what organizations have publicly reported or regulators have published. Those results are their own, not TTI's, and they are not predictions of yours.

## What you will be able to do

- Explain the main insurance distribution channels and agency business models.
- Map the steps to obtain and maintain a producer license in your state.
- Describe carrier appointments and the contracts behind them.
- Build a revenue model and calculate how much runway you need.
- Design intake and data-handling processes that respect customer privacy.
- Separate tasks that require a license from those that do not, and build a clean handoff.
- Market lawfully, handle complaints professionally, and rehearse operations before launch.

# Chapter 1: How Insurance Reaches Customers

:::objectives
- Define insurer, producer, agent, and broker.
- Describe the captive, independent, and direct distribution channels.
- Interpret market-share data for independent agencies.
- Identify where a new entrant might fit.
:::

An **insurer** (also called a carrier) is the company that issues policies, collects premiums, and pays claims. A **producer** is the general legal term for a person licensed to sell, solicit, or negotiate insurance. Producers who represent an insurer are often called **agents**; producers who act on behalf of the customer are sometimes called **brokers**. The terms are used loosely in everyday speech, but they can carry legal meaning in a given state.

## Three channels

**Captive (exclusive) agents** represent one insurer or one insurer group. The carrier often provides brand, leads, training, and systems; in return, the agent usually sells only that carrier's products and may not own the customer relationships outright.

**Independent agents and brokers** hold appointments with several carriers and can compare options for each customer. They typically own their book of business, meaning the right to the renewal commissions and customer relationships, subject to their contracts.

**Direct writers** sell to customers without an outside agent, through call centers, websites, and apps. Some insurers use more than one channel.

## How large is the independent channel?

The Independent Insurance Agents & Brokers of America (often called the Big "I") publishes two relevant studies. Its 2026 Market Share Report, based on 2025 AM Best data, found that the independent agency channel placed 62 percent of all U.S. property-casualty premium, including 87.7 percent of commercial lines and 39.5 percent of personal lines premium [1]. Its 2026 Agency Universe Study estimated about 37,000 independent property-casualty agencies, a slight decrease from 2024 amid active mergers and acquisitions, and found that fewer agencies reported revenue declines in 2025 than in 2024 (8 percent versus 12 percent), with about 7 in 10 reporting increases in personal lines revenue [2][3]. An earlier edition reported that agencies were appointed with an average of 17 carriers [4].

| Channel | Products offered | Who owns relationships (typically) | Startup support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Captive | One carrier group | Often the carrier, per contract | Often substantial |
| Independent | Many carriers | Usually the agency, per contract | Agency must build or join a network |
| Direct | One carrier | Carrier | Not applicable to agents |

These figures describe property-casualty insurance. Life and health insurance distribution has its own channels and rules, including federal rules for Medicare and Affordable Care Act marketplace products discussed in Chapter 9.

## Lines of authority

Insurance is divided into **lines of authority**, categories a producer is licensed to sell. The NAIC's Uniform Licensing Standards list six major lines: life; accident and health or sickness; property; casualty; variable life and variable annuity products; and personal lines [5]. Choosing which lines to pursue is one of the first strategic decisions a new agency makes, because it determines your exams, your carriers, and your customers.

:::example Illustrative: sizing a local opportunity
A prospective agency owner in a county with 40,000 households estimates that each household spends about $3,000 a year on auto and home insurance combined, for a market of about $120 million. If independent agencies place roughly 40 percent of personal lines nationally (the 2025 figure was 39.5 percent [1]), and local conditions are similar, about $48 million might flow through independents. If the county already has 25 independent agencies, the average would be about $1.9 million in premium each. Capturing even 1 percent of the county's personal lines premium ($1.2 million) would be a substantial book for a new agency, and would take years. This is a rough sizing, not a forecast; local carrier presence, competition, and growth rates all matter.
:::

# Chapter 2: Choosing a Business Model

:::objectives
- Compare five common paths into agency ownership.
- Evaluate franchise and network offers critically.
- Use public company filings to understand a model's economics.
- Select a model that fits your capital, experience, and goals.
:::

There is no single way to start an insurance business. The five paths below, plus buying an existing book, cover most entrants. The right model depends on your experience, capital, appetite for risk, and the lines you want to sell.

## Five paths

1. **Join an existing agency as a producer.** Lowest risk; you learn the trade, typically on salary, commission, or both. You may or may not own any book.
2. **Become a captive agent.** The carrier supplies brand and support; you accept its product limits and contract terms.
3. **Start an independent agency from scratch.** Maximum control and ownership; you must secure carrier access yourself, which is difficult without a track record.
4. **Join an agency network or aggregator.** Networks (sometimes called clusters or aggregators) give small agencies access to carrier appointments and support in exchange for fees or a share of commissions.
5. **Buy a franchise.** A franchisor licenses its brand, systems, and often carrier access, in exchange for an initial fee and ongoing royalties.

:::case Goosehead Insurance: a public franchise model
Goosehead Insurance, a publicly traded independent agency, describes in its annual reports a model that combines corporate-owned offices with franchises. Its Form 10-K filed in February 2026 states that franchisees use the company's processes, carrier appointments, systems, and back-office support, and that the company in exchange receives an initial franchise fee and ongoing royalty fees [6]. The company separates sales from service: franchise owners rely on Goosehead's service centers for service requests and renewals so they can focus on new business [6]. Its franchise website lists an initial franchise fee of $50,000 and royalty fees of 20 to 50 percent, noting that franchises are offered only through its Franchise Disclosure Document [7].

The filings also show that franchise systems can contract as well as grow. Goosehead's 10-K for 2022 reported 2,125 total franchises (1,413 operating), while its 10-K for 2023 reported 1,415 total franchises (1,226 operating), a 33 percent decrease in total franchises [8][9]. Its 10-K for 2025 reported that operating franchises fell 9 percent, from 1,103 to 1,009, after a 10 percent decline in 2024 from 1,226, even as franchise premiums grew [10]. These are Goosehead's figures, not TTI's. The lesson for a prospective franchisee is to read the Franchise Disclosure Document closely, including the data on franchise closures and transfers, and to talk with current and former franchisees.
:::


## A sixth path: buying a book of business

Some entrants buy an existing agency or a portion of a retiring agent's book. Perpetuation is a live issue in the industry; the Big "I" reports that 87 percent of independent agencies have some type of perpetuation tool or plan in place [3]. Buying a book provides immediate renewal revenue and carrier relationships, but it requires capital or seller financing, careful due diligence, and carrier consent to transfer appointments.

Due diligence on a book should cover at least the following:

- **Premium and commission history** for three to five years, reconciled to carrier statements rather than the seller's summaries.
- **Retention by carrier and line**, since a book concentrated in one carrier that is raising rates or withdrawing from the state may shrink quickly after purchase.
- **Customer concentration**, especially in commercial lines where a few accounts can represent much of the revenue.
- **Contract terms**, including whether the seller owns the expirations and whether carriers will appoint the buyer.
- **Complaint and E&O history**, because a book with unresolved problems can transfer them to you.
- **The seller's role after closing**, such as introductions and a transition period, and any non-solicitation agreement.

:::example Illustrative: a simple book valuation check
A retiring agent offers a personal lines book producing $150,000 a year in commission. The asking price is 2.0 times annual commission, or $300,000, with 30 percent down and the balance paid over five years. Down payment: $90,000. Remaining $210,000 over 60 months is $3,500 a month before interest.

If retention after transfer is 85 percent, year-one commission is about $127,500, or $10,625 a month. After the $3,500 payment, about $7,125 a month remains to cover servicing costs and owner income. If retention falls to 75 percent, monthly commission drops to about $9,375 and the margin narrows to about $5,875. A buyer should model the low-retention case and consider tying part of the price to retained revenue. Valuation multiples vary widely by market, line, and profitability; consult an adviser who values agencies.
:::

The FTC's Franchise Rule requires franchisors to provide a Franchise Disclosure Document before a sale, which includes information on fees, litigation, and outlet openings and closings. Read it with an attorney who knows franchising.

Path A: join a network that charges $300 a month and keeps 20 percent of commissions. Path B: a franchise with a $50,000 initial fee and a 30 percent royalty (within the published range), plus $500 a month in technology fees.

Path B costs $84,200 more over three years under these assumptions. It would make sense only if its brand, systems, or service centers produce substantially more commission than Path A. That is the question to investigate. These numbers are invented for illustration; actual terms vary widely.

# Chapter 3: Producer Licensing

:::objectives
- Describe the steps to obtain a resident producer license.
- Explain resident and nonresident licensing and reciprocity.
- Identify continuing education and renewal obligations.
- Build a licensing inquiry sheet for your state.
:::

No one may sell, solicit, or negotiate insurance without a license for the relevant line of authority in the relevant state. Licensing is the legal foundation of the business, and it is administered by each state's department of insurance (DOI).

## The uniform framework

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) has worked for two decades to make licensing more uniform across states through its Producer Licensing Model Act and Uniform Licensing Standards (ULS). Among the standards: applicants must be at least 18; no high school diploma is required; states that require prelicensing education should require 20 hours per major line of authority; individual licenses renew every two years; and continuing education (CE) is 24 hours per renewal period, including 3 hours of ethics [5]. States adopt these standards to varying degrees, so always confirm your state's rules.

## Typical steps

1. **Choose lines of authority** (Chapter 1).
2. **Complete prelicensing education** if your state requires it. For example, Oregon's instructions call for 20 hours of prelicense training per line of authority [11], while Delaware states that it requires no prelicensing education [12].
3. **Pass the state exam** for each line, administered by a testing vendor.
4. **Submit fingerprints and a background check** where required.
5. **Apply for the license**, commonly through the National Insurance Producer Registry (NIPR) or a state system, and pay the fee. Fees vary by state; Delaware, for example, lists a $100 license fee [12].
6. **Obtain appointments** with carriers if your state and role require them (Chapter 4).
7. **Maintain the license**: complete CE, renew on time, and report changes of address or legal actions as required.

## Resident and nonresident licenses

Your **resident license** is issued by your home state. To sell to customers in other states, you generally need a **nonresident license** in each, which states commonly grant based on your resident license in good standing, without re-examination, through reciprocity. The NAIC's Producer Database (PDB), maintained with NIPR, lets states verify licensing status electronically [13].

## Business entity licenses

If you operate through an LLC or corporation that receives commissions, many states require the entity itself to hold a license and to designate a licensed responsible producer. Plan the entity structure and licensing together.

:::example Illustrative: licensing timeline and cost
A candidate in a state requiring prelicensing education seeks property and casualty lines.

| Step | Time | Cost (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Prelicensing course, 2 lines × 20 hours | 2–3 weeks part-time | $250 |
| Exam (property and casualty, combined or separate) | 1 day; schedule 1–2 weeks out | $150 |
| Fingerprints and background check | 1 visit | $60 |
| License application and transaction fee | Processing 1–3 weeks | $200 |
| Total | About 5–8 weeks | About $660 |

Then add CE: 24 hours every two years at, say, $150 per cycle. If the candidate fails one exam and retakes it, add one to two weeks and another exam fee. Actual times and costs vary by state and vendor; check your DOI.

# Chapter 4: Carrier Appointments and Agency Arrangements

:::objectives
- Define an appointment and explain how it differs from a license.
- Describe what carriers look for in a new agency.
- Read the key terms of an agency agreement.
- Plan a realistic carrier-access strategy.
:::

A license allows you to sell a line of insurance in a state. An **appointment** is a carrier's authorization for you to represent it. In states that require appointments, a producer may not act as an agent of a particular insurer without one.

## How appointments work

States handle appointments differently. Delaware, for example, says an insurer must appoint a producer within 15 days of executing an agency contract or receiving the producer's first application, and lists a $50 appointment fee [12]. Some states have shifted to having insurers maintain registers rather than file each appointment. Appointments are commonly filed electronically through NIPR. Your carriers handle the filing; you need to know whether you are appointed before you write business.

## What carriers look for

Carriers invest in each agency relationship: training, systems access, underwriting time, and commissions. They want evidence that an agency will produce profitable business. Common criteria include:

- Licensing and experience of the principal.
- A business plan with target markets and premium projections.
- Minimum premium commitments within a set period.
- Errors and omissions (E&O) insurance at specified limits.
- Financial stability and clean regulatory history.
- Location and market fit with the carrier's appetite.

New agencies without a track record often struggle to obtain direct appointments, which is why networks, aggregators, and franchises (Chapter 2) exist.

## The agency agreement

The **agency agreement** is the contract between carrier and agency. Read every clause; key terms include:

| Term | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Authority | What you may bind, quote, or collect |
| Commission schedule | New and renewal rates by line; whether the carrier may change them |
| Contingent or profit-sharing commissions | Bonuses based on volume, growth, or loss ratio |
| Premium handling | Whether you collect premium; fiduciary and trust account duties |
| Book ownership and expirations | Who owns renewal rights if the contract ends |
| Termination | Notice periods; what happens to in-force policies |
| Production requirements | Minimum premium or policy counts |
| Indemnification | Who bears losses from errors |
| E&O | Required coverage limits |

:::callout Fiduciary responsibility
Where an agency collects premium on behalf of a carrier, that money generally is not the agency's to spend. Many states require such funds to be held in a fiduciary or trust account and remitted on time. Mixing premium with operating funds is a common cause of regulatory action. Learn your state's rules before collecting a single payment.
:::

:::example Illustrative: building a carrier panel over 18 months
A new independent agency targets personal auto and home in one state.

- Months 0–3: joins a network that provides immediate access to 8 carriers through the network's appointments, at 80 percent of commissions to the agency.
- Months 4–12: writes $600,000 in new premium; 60 percent through two carriers whose pricing fits the local market.
- Month 12: applies directly to those two carriers, showing its production record, loss ratio, and growth plan. One grants a direct appointment requiring $250,000 in annual new premium; the other declines for now.
- Months 13–18: moves new business for the first carrier to the direct appointment, raising the agency's share on that business from 80 percent to 100 percent of commission, while keeping network access for the others.

If the direct carrier pays 12 percent new-business commission and the agency writes $300,000 a year there, the move adds about 0.20 × 0.12 × 300,000 = $7,200 a year in commission. The agency weighs that against the loss of network services for that carrier and any contractual restrictions on moving business.
:::

:::checklist Carrier readiness
- I know whether my state requires appointments and how they are filed.
- I have a written business plan with target markets and premium projections.
- I have E&O coverage quotes at limits carriers commonly require.
- I understand fiduciary rules for any premium I collect.
- I have read each agency agreement, including commission changes, book ownership, and termination.
:::

:::summary
A license authorizes you to sell a line; an appointment authorizes you to represent a carrier. Carriers look for experience, a plan, production commitments, E&O coverage, and fit with their appetite. Agency agreements govern authority, commissions, premium handling, book ownership, and termination. Many new agencies start through networks and add direct appointments as production grows.
:::

# Chapter 5: Revenue, Costs, and Runway

:::objectives
- Explain how agencies earn revenue from new and renewal commissions.
- Build a simple book-of-business projection.
- Estimate startup and monthly operating costs.
- Calculate the runway a new agency needs before it covers its costs.
:::

Agency economics differ from most small businesses in one important way: revenue compounds. A policy written this year typically pays a commission when written and again each time it renews, as long as the customer stays and the agency remains the agent of record. That makes early years lean and later years more stable, but only if retention is strong.

## Revenue sources

- **New-business commission**: a percentage of premium on newly written policies.
- **Renewal commission**: a percentage of premium on renewals, sometimes lower than the new-business rate, depending on the carrier and line.
- **Contingent or profit-sharing commission**: bonuses tied to volume, growth, or underwriting profitability. Treat these as uncertain, not as budget.
- **Fees**: some states permit certain broker or service fees under defined conditions; others restrict them. Confirm before charging any fee.

Commission rates vary by carrier, line, and state. Personal lines rates often fall in the low teens or below as a percentage of premium, while commercial and life products follow different structures. Use the actual schedules in your agency agreements, not industry averages, when you plan.

## Retention: the compounding engine

**Retention rate** is the share of policies (or premium) still in force a year later. If you write $500,000 of new premium a year and retain 85 percent, your book grows each year but at a decreasing rate, approaching a ceiling where new business equals losses. At 85 percent retention the long-run ceiling is about new premium ÷ (1 − retention) = $500,000 ÷ 0.15 ≈ $3.33 million. At 90 percent it is $5 million. Small changes in retention make large differences over time.

:::example Illustrative: a three-year book projection
Assumptions: $400,000 new premium in year 1, $600,000 in year 2, $700,000 in year 3; 85 percent retention; 12 percent commission on both new and renewal premium.

| Year | Renewed premium | New premium | Total book | Commission (12%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $0 | $400,000 | $400,000 | $48,000 |
| 2 | $340,000 | $600,000 | $940,000 | $112,800 |
| 3 | $799,000 | $700,000 | $1,499,000 | $179,880 |

Year 2 renewed premium is 0.85 × $400,000 = $340,000. Year 3 renewed premium is 0.85 × $940,000 = $799,000. This simplified model ignores rate changes, mid-term cancellations, and differing commission rates. It is a planning tool, not a promise.
:::

## Costs

| Category | Startup (one-time) | Monthly (ongoing) |
|---|---|---|
| Licensing, exams, entity formation | Yes | Renewals periodically |
| E&O insurance | Deposit | Premium installments |
| Agency management system and comparative rater | Setup | Subscription |
| Phones, website, email, security tools | Setup | Subscription |
| Office (home, shared, or leased) | Deposit, furniture | Rent, utilities |
| Network or franchise fees | Initial fee | Monthly fee or royalty |
| Marketing | Launch | Ongoing |
| Staff | Hiring | Payroll and taxes |
| Owner's living costs | — | Personal draw |

The last line is the one most often omitted. If the owner needs $4,000 a month to live, the agency must either earn that or the owner must fund it from savings while the book grows.

## Runway

**Runway** is the number of months your cash can cover the gap between expenses and revenue. A practical method: project monthly revenue and expenses, compute the cumulative shortfall until monthly revenue exceeds expenses, and hold that amount plus a margin of safety.

:::example Illustrative: runway calculation
Monthly costs: business $3,500 plus owner draw $4,000 = $7,500. Commission revenue starts at zero and rises by about $500 a month as the book grows (month 1: $500; month 12: $6,000; month 15: $7,500).

The monthly gap is $7,000 in month 1, $6,500 in month 2, and so on, down to zero in month 15. The cumulative gap is the sum of an arithmetic series: 15 terms averaging ($7,000 + $0) ÷ 2 = $3,500, so about $52,500. Adding a 30 percent margin for delays and slower production gives roughly $68,000 of runway, plus startup costs. If production grows half as fast, the break-even point moves to month 30 and the cumulative gap roughly doubles. Test the slow case before committing.
:::

# Chapter 6: The Operating Foundation

:::objectives
- Choose an entity structure in consultation with professionals.
- Explain why errors and omissions insurance is essential.
- Set up premium handling, records, and an agency management system.
- Design a sales and service workflow that separates responsibilities.
:::

Before the first customer, build the infrastructure that keeps the business organized, compliant, and insurable.

## Entity and banking

Most agencies operate as limited liability companies or corporations. The choice affects taxes, liability, and licensing (many states require the entity to be licensed). Make the choice with an attorney and accountant. Open separate operating accounts and, where you will collect premium, a separate fiduciary or trust account as your state requires.

## Errors and omissions insurance

**Errors and omissions (E&O) insurance** protects the agency against claims that it made a professional mistake: failing to bind coverage, recommending inadequate limits, or missing a renewal. Carriers commonly require E&O as a condition of appointment, and it is a basic safeguard for the owner. Ask about limits, deductibles, and whether coverage includes cyber incidents or whether a separate cyber policy is needed.

## The agency management system

An **agency management system (AMS)** stores customers, policies, documents, activities, and commission records. A **comparative rater** quotes multiple carriers at once for personal lines. Choose systems that support:

- A complete record of every contact, quote, and recommendation.
- Role-based access, so staff see only what they need.
- Secure document storage with retention settings.
- Commission reconciliation against carrier statements.
- Audit trails showing who changed what and when.

## Separating sales from service

Goosehead's public filings describe a deliberate separation of sales and service, with service handled by dedicated centers so producers can focus on new business [6]. You do not need a service center to apply the principle. Even a two-person agency benefits from defining which tasks belong to new business, which to account service, and which require a licensed person.

:::example Illustrative: time allocation for a solo owner
A solo licensed owner logs time for two weeks: 40 percent on service requests (certificates, ID cards, billing questions), 25 percent on quoting, 15 percent on marketing, 10 percent on administration, 10 percent on carrier communications.

If the owner's goal is $600,000 of new premium in year one, and each closed policy averages $1,500 premium with a 30 percent close rate on qualified quotes, the owner needs 400 policies and about 1,333 quotes, or roughly 26 quotes a week. If each quote takes 45 minutes including follow-up, that is about 19.5 hours a week, more than the 25 percent (10 hours of a 40-hour week) currently available. The arithmetic points to a decision: hire service help, automate routine service, or lower the goal.

# Chapter 7: Intake and Customer Information Security

:::objectives
- Design an intake process that collects only necessary information.
- Describe the main elements of the NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law.
- Apply basic safeguards for personal information.
- Prepare an incident response outline.
:::

Insurance runs on personal information: names, addresses, dates of birth, driver's license numbers, vehicle identification numbers, claims histories, and sometimes health or financial data. That information is valuable to criminals and protected by law.

## Data minimization at intake

Collect what you need for the next step, not everything you might someday need. A first inquiry rarely requires a Social Security number. Ask yourself, for each field: who uses it, when, and what happens if we don't collect it now?

| Stage | Typically needed | Usually not needed yet |
|---|---|---|
| Initial inquiry | Name, contact, line of interest, best time to call, consent | Date of birth, license numbers, financial data |
| Quoting | Information the carrier's rating requires | Unrelated data |
| Application | Full underwriting information | Anything not on the application |
| Service | Policy number, identity verification | Full re-collection of application data |

## The NAIC Insurance Data Security Model Law

In 2017 the NAIC adopted the Insurance Data Security Model Law (#668). It requires licensees to develop a written information security program based on a risk assessment, designate a person responsible for the program, maintain an incident response plan, oversee third-party service providers, and notify the insurance commissioner of cybersecurity events within 72 hours of determination [14]. The NAIC reports that the model has been adopted in many jurisdictions, and it includes an exemption for licensees with fewer than ten employees [14]. Even if your state has not adopted it, or you qualify for an exemption, its elements are a sound framework. Check your state's version, since adopted laws can differ from the model.

## Safeguards every small agency can apply

The FTC's guide Protecting Personal Information organizes safeguards around five principles: take stock (know what you have), scale down (keep only what you need), lock it (protect what you keep), pitch it (dispose of what you no longer need), and plan ahead (prepare for incidents) [15]. In practice:

- Use multi-factor authentication on email, the AMS, and carrier portals.
- Never send unencrypted sensitive data by ordinary email.
- Use secure upload links or carrier portals for documents.
- Restrict access by role and remove access promptly when staff leave.
- Shred paper and wipe devices before disposal.
- Train staff to recognize phishing and verify payment or banking changes by phone using a known number.

:::example Illustrative: reducing exposure by minimizing data
An agency's web form asks every visitor for a date of birth and driver's license number. It receives 1,200 inquiries a year; 35 percent become quotes and 10 percent become customers. Under the old form, the agency stores license numbers for 1,200 people a year; 1,080 of them (90 percent) never become customers.

The redesigned form collects only name, contact information, line of interest, and consent. License numbers are collected only at the quoting stage, for 420 people, and deleted for non-buyers after a documented retention period. Stored license numbers fall by about 65 percent (from 1,200 to 420) in the first year, and to 120 after deletions. Less data stored means less to protect and less harm if something goes wrong.
:::

# Chapter 8: Needs Analysis and the Licensed Handoff

:::objectives
- Conduct a structured needs analysis.
- Distinguish tasks that generally require a license from those that generally do not.
- Design a clean handoff from unlicensed staff or automated intake to a licensed producer.
- Document recommendations and customer decisions.
:::

The heart of an insurance agency is advice: helping a customer understand risks and choose coverage. That work is regulated, and it is where most errors and complaints originate.

## A needs analysis framework

1. **Understand the customer's situation.** Household or business, assets, activities, existing coverage.
2. **Identify exposures.** What could go wrong, and what would it cost?
3. **Review current coverage.** Limits, deductibles, exclusions, and gaps.
4. **Present options.** Explain the trade-offs in plain language, including higher and lower coverage options.
5. **Record the decision.** Document what was offered, what the customer chose, and what they declined.
6. **Follow up.** Deliver documents and confirm the customer understands.

## What requires a license?

Each state defines the activities that require a producer license, typically including selling, soliciting, and negotiating insurance. Many states allow unlicensed staff to perform clerical and administrative tasks under supervision. The boundary differs by state, so treat the following table as a general orientation and confirm with your DOI.

| Generally clerical (often permitted for unlicensed staff) | Generally licensed activity (confirm with your state) |
|---|---|
| Scheduling appointments | Recommending coverage or limits |
| Collecting basic contact information | Explaining policy terms to persuade |
| Sending forms the customer completes | Comparing carriers' products for the customer |
| Accepting documents | Quoting premiums in many states |
| Processing routine changes under direction | Binding coverage |
| Answering factual questions about office hours or status | Soliciting the purchase of insurance |

Automated tools, including chatbots and AI assistants, deserve the same scrutiny. If a tool recommends coverage, explains terms in a persuasive way, or compares products, it may be performing an activity that requires a license. Configure such tools to gather information and route customers to a licensed person.

## Designing the handoff

A clean **licensed handoff** moves a customer from intake to a licensed producer with complete information and no gaps. Good handoffs:

- Use a script that tells the customer a licensed agent will contact them, and when.
- Transfer complete intake data to the producer through the AMS.
- Set a response-time target. Research on online sales leads published in Harvard Business Review found that companies contacting leads within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as those who waited longer [16].
- Record who handled each step.

:::example Illustrative: measuring a handoff
An agency tracks 200 web inquiries in a quarter. Under the current process, the median time from inquiry to licensed contact is 26 hours, and 22 percent of inquiries are never reached. After adding a same-day callback rule and an intake assistant that collects basic information and books a callback slot, the median falls to 3 hours and the unreached share falls to 9 percent.

If 30 percent of reached inquiries become quotes and 30 percent of quotes become policies averaging $1,400 premium:
- Before: 200 × 0.78 × 0.30 × 0.30 = about 14 policies, $19,600 premium.
- After: 200 × 0.91 × 0.30 × 0.30 = about 16 policies, $22,400 premium.

These figures are invented to illustrate the arithmetic. The point is that faster handoffs can be measured and their effect estimated, not that any particular improvement will occur.
:::

## Documentation

Write down the recommendation and the customer's decision every time. If a customer declines a coverage you recommended, such as higher liability limits, note it and, where appropriate, have the customer acknowledge it in writing. Documentation protects the customer, who has a record of the advice, and the agency, which can show it acted reasonably.

:::checklist Licensed handoff
- My state's definition of licensed activity is documented for staff.
- Unlicensed staff and automated tools have written scripts and limits.
- Intake data flows to the licensed producer without re-keying.
- A response-time target is set and measured.
- Every recommendation and decision is documented in the AMS.
- Declined coverage is recorded and acknowledged where appropriate.
:::

:::summary
A structured needs analysis moves from situation to exposures, coverage review, options, decision, and follow-up. States define licensed activity; unlicensed staff and automated tools should stay within clerical tasks and route customers to licensed producers. Fast, complete handoffs and thorough documentation protect customers and the agency.
:::

# Chapter 9: Responsible Marketing

:::objectives
- Apply the main federal telemarketing rules to agency outreach.
- Evaluate purchased leads for consent and quality.
- Write advertising that is truthful and compliant.
- Learn from the CMS response to agent misconduct in the ACA marketplace.
:::

Insurance marketing is regulated at both state and federal levels. State unfair trade practices laws prohibit misrepresentation and deceptive advertising of insurance. Federal rules govern telephone, text, and email outreach. Violations can bring fines, license action, and lasting damage to reputation.

## Telemarketing and the Do Not Call Registry

The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule (TSR) governs many outbound sales calls. Among its requirements, as summarized in the FTC's compliance guide [17]:

- Sellers and telemarketers must access the National Do Not Call Registry and remove registered numbers from call lists, refreshing at least every 31 days.
- Callers must honor company-specific do-not-call requests.
- Calls are permitted only between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time.
- An established business relationship allows calls for 18 months after a purchase or transaction, or 3 months after an inquiry or application, unless the person asks not to be called.
- Civil penalties can reach $53,088 per violation (the amount is adjusted periodically for inflation).
- Several states maintain their own do-not-call lists with additional rules.

Separate rules under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, administered by the Federal Communications Commission, govern autodialed calls, prerecorded messages, and text messages, and generally require prior express written consent for marketing calls and texts using that technology. Because TCPA lawsuits can carry statutory damages per call or text, obtain clear, documented consent before automated outreach and consult counsel on your scripts and tools.

## Email

The CAN-SPAM Act requires that commercial emails not use deceptive headers or subject lines, identify themselves as advertisements where required, include a valid physical postal address, and offer a clear way to opt out, honored within 10 business days [18].

## Buying leads

Many agencies buy leads from aggregators. A lead is only as good as the consent behind it. Before buying:

- Ask how consent was obtained and request the exact consent language and screen.
- Confirm that the consent names your agency or clearly covers it.
- Ask how often each lead is sold, and to how many buyers.
- Keep records of consent for each lead you contact.

## Truthful advertising and reviews

Avoid absolute claims ("lowest rates," "guaranteed savings") unless you can substantiate them. Do not imply affiliation with a government program or carrier you do not represent. The FTC's 2024 rule on consumer reviews and testimonials prohibits fake reviews, buying positive reviews, and certain review-suppression practices, with civil penalties for knowing violations [19].

:::case CMS and unauthorized marketplace enrollments
In 2024, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) reported a surge in complaints about agents and brokers changing consumers' Affordable Care Act marketplace plans or enrolling them without consent. From January through August 2024, CMS received 183,553 complaints about unauthorized enrollments and 90,863 about unauthorized plan switches [20]. CMS suspended the marketplace agreements of 850 agents and brokers between June and October 2024 for suspected misconduct, and from July 19, 2024, it limited plan changes to agents already associated with an enrollment unless a three-way call with the consumer took place. CMS reported that after this change, agent-associated plan changes fell by nearly 70 percent [20]. CMS also emphasized that agents must obtain and document consumer consent [21].

For accuracy, the story has a later chapter: the U.S. Government Accountability Office reported in 2025 that CMS reinstated the 850 suspended agents and brokers in May 2025 on procedural grounds, after concluding it had not followed its own required procedures [22]. The lesson does not depend on any individual outcome. Systems that lack consent controls invite abuse, regulators respond with sweeping restrictions that affect honest agents too, and documented consent is every producer's best protection.
:::

:::example Illustrative: list hygiene costs and risks
An agency plans a 2,000-number outbound campaign to past inquirers. Scrubbing against the national registry and the agency's internal do-not-call list removes 340 numbers. Of the remaining 1,660, 420 inquired more than 3 months ago and never bought, so they fall outside the inquiry-based established relationship and must be removed unless another exception applies. That leaves 1,240 callable numbers.

Skipping the scrub might seem to add 760 calls, but even ten calls to registered numbers expose the agency to penalties far exceeding any likely commission: at the published maximum of $53,088 per violation, ten violations could reach $530,880. The arithmetic makes the compliance decision easy.
:::

:::checklist Marketing compliance
- Call lists are scrubbed against the national and applicable state registries at least every 31 days.
- An internal do-not-call list is maintained and honored.
- Calling hours are restricted to 8 a.m.–9 p.m. recipient time.
- Written consent is obtained and stored before any automated calls or texts.
- Purchased leads have documented consent naming or covering my agency.
- Emails meet CAN-SPAM requirements, including opt-out handling.
- Advertising claims are substantiated and reviewed for state compliance.
- No fake, purchased, or incentivized-without-disclosure reviews.
:::

:::summary
Insurance marketing is governed by state unfair trade practices laws and federal rules on calls, texts, emails, and reviews. The TSR requires registry scrubbing every 31 days, internal do-not-call lists, and restricted calling hours. The TCPA requires consent for automated outreach. The CMS response to marketplace misconduct shows how lack of documented consent harms consumers and leads to industry-wide restrictions.
:::

# Chapter 10: Complaints and Professional Ethics

:::objectives
- Explain how consumers file insurance complaints.
- Build an internal complaint-handling process.
- Use complaint data to improve operations.
- Apply ethical principles to common agency dilemmas.
:::

Complaints are not only a risk; they are information. A professional agency treats every complaint as a chance to fix a problem for one customer and prevent it for others.

## How the regulatory complaint system works

Consumers who are dissatisfied with an insurer or producer can file a complaint with their state department of insurance, which typically investigates and asks the company or producer to respond [23]. The NAIC's Consumer Insurance Search tool lets the public research closed confirmed complaints against insurers, giving consumers a way to compare companies [24]. A regulatory complaint against your agency can lead to inquiries, required responses within set deadlines, and in serious cases license action.

## An internal complaint process

1. **Receive.** Every staff member knows how to recognize and log a complaint, including informal ones.
2. **Acknowledge.** Respond promptly, in writing, with a named contact and expected timeline.
3. **Investigate.** Gather the file, recordings, and documentation.
4. **Resolve.** Fix the issue where possible; involve the carrier when it concerns underwriting or claims.
5. **Record.** Log the root cause and resolution.
6. **Review.** Monthly, examine trends and change processes.

| Root cause category | Example | Typical process fix |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | Customer did not understand a deductible | Plain-language summary at binding |
| Service delay | Certificate not issued on time | Response-time standard and tracking |
| Coverage gap | Exclusion not explained | Needs analysis checklist update |
| Billing | Unexpected premium change | Renewal review call before effective date |
| Conduct | Pushy sales behavior | Coaching, script revision, supervision |

## Ethics in daily practice

The NAIC Uniform Licensing Standards include 3 hours of ethics within each continuing education cycle [5], reflecting regulators' view that ethics is part of competence. Common dilemmas include recommending a carrier because of a higher commission, replacing a policy without a clear customer benefit, and minimizing an exclusion to close a sale. A useful test: would you be comfortable if the customer, the carrier, and the regulator all read your notes?

:::example Illustrative: complaint ratio tracking
An agency serves 1,800 policies and logs 27 complaints in a year, a ratio of 15 per 1,000 policies. Root-cause review shows 12 complaints (44 percent) concern unexpected renewal increases. The agency adds a renewal review call 30 days before effective date for policies with increases above 10 percent. The next year it logs 16 complaints on 2,000 policies (8 per 1,000), with 4 concerning renewals. The improvement is the agency's own measured result in this invented scenario; the method, not the number, is what transfers.
:::

Consumers file insurance complaints with state departments of insurance, and the NAIC publishes complaint information on insurers. Agencies should receive, acknowledge, investigate, resolve, record, and review complaints, using root causes to change processes. # Chapter 11: Retention and Renewals

:::objectives
- Explain why retention drives agency value.
- Design a renewal review process.
- Use cross-selling responsibly to meet real customer needs.
- Measure client and premium retention.
:::

Chapter 5 showed that a book's long-run size depends on retention. Retention also drives the value of an agency if it is ever sold, because buyers pay for durable renewal revenue.

## Measuring retention

**Client (policy) retention** is the share of customers or policies that renew. **Premium retention** is renewing premium as a share of prior premium, which can exceed 100 percent when rates rise. Public companies report both. Goosehead, for example, reported client retention of 85 percent and premium retention of 90 percent as of December 31, 2025 [25]. Those are its figures, not a benchmark for your agency, but they show how the two measures can diverge.

## The renewal review

A renewal review is a scheduled contact before a policy renews:

1. 45–60 days before renewal: review the renewal offer and any rate change.
2. Contact the customer if the premium change exceeds a set threshold or if life or business changes are likely.
3. Re-check coverage needs using the needs analysis framework.
4. Remarket to other carriers when appropriate and permitted.
5. Document the outcome.

## Responsible cross-selling

Customers with more than one policy at an agency often stay longer, and many have genuine gaps, such as a homeowner with no umbrella policy. Cross-selling is responsible when it begins with a need and ends with a documented recommendation; it is not responsible when it is driven by quotas without regard to the customer.

:::example Illustrative: value of a retention improvement
An agency with a $2,000,000 book and 12 percent commission earns $240,000 a year. Raising retention from 84 to 88 percent preserves an extra 4 percent of the book, or $80,000 of premium, worth $9,600 in commission in the first year. Because retained premium renews again, the effect compounds: under a simple model with constant new business, the long-run book ceiling rises by about 33 percent (from N ÷ 0.16 to N ÷ 0.12). This is arithmetic, not a forecast.
:::

| Retention metric | Formula | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Policy retention | Policies renewed ÷ policies up for renewal | Service quality signal |
| Premium retention | Renewing premium ÷ prior premium | Revenue stability |
| Policies per customer | Total policies ÷ customers | Account depth |
| Lost-customer reasons | Count by reason | Process improvement |

:::checklist Renewal process
- Renewal list generated 60 days ahead each month.
- Threshold set for proactive calls on premium increases.
- Needs re-check performed and documented.
- Lost customers asked for a reason, and reasons logged.
- Retention measured monthly by policy and premium.
:::

:::summary
Retention drives both the long-run size and the market value of an agency. Measure policy and premium retention separately. A renewal review 45–60 days ahead, needs re-checks, and responsible cross-selling based on documented needs improve retention while serving customers.
:::

:::case Documenting procedures: Dan Martell's Camcorder Method

If the owner delegates 10 hours a week of such tasks at $18 an hour, the cost is $180 a week, about $9,360 a year (52 weeks). # Glossary

**Agency agreement**: The contract between an insurer and an agency setting authority, commissions, and obligations.

**Agency management system (AMS)**: Software that stores customer, policy, activity, and commission records.

**Appointment**: An insurer's authorization for a licensed producer to represent it.

**Book of business**: The set of policies and customer relationships an agent or agency services and earns commissions on.

**Broker**: A producer who acts on behalf of the customer; the legal meaning varies by state.

**Captive agent**: An agent who represents one insurer or insurer group.

**Comparative rater**: Software that quotes multiple carriers at once.

**Contingent commission**: A bonus commission tied to volume, growth, or underwriting profitability.

**Continuing education (CE)**: Required coursework to renew a producer license.

**Department of insurance (DOI)**: The state agency that regulates insurance and licenses producers.

**Direct writer**: An insurer that sells without outside agents.

**Errors and omissions (E&O) insurance**: Coverage for claims of professional mistakes.

**Established business relationship**: Under the TSR, a relationship that permits calls for a limited period after a purchase or inquiry.

**Fiduciary account**: A separate account holding premium funds owed to insurers or customers.

**Independent agent**: An agent appointed with multiple insurers.

**Licensed handoff**: The transfer of a customer from intake to a licensed producer.

**Line of authority**: A category of insurance a producer is licensed to sell.

**NAIC**: The National Association of Insurance Commissioners, the standard-setting organization of state insurance regulators.

**NIPR**: The National Insurance Producer Registry, which supports electronic licensing and appointments.

**Nonresident license**: A license issued by a state other than the producer's home state.

**Premium retention**: Renewing premium as a share of prior premium.

**Producer**: A person licensed to sell, solicit, or negotiate insurance.

**Retention rate**: The share of policies or customers that renew.

**Runway**: The months of cash available to cover the gap between expenses and revenue.

**TCPA**: The Telephone Consumer Protection Act, which governs automated calls and texts.

**TSR**: The FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule.
