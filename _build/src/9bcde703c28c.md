# How to Use This Book

AI voice agents, software that answers or places telephone calls using synthesized speech and language models, have moved quickly from novelty to a practical option for small businesses. They can answer after hours, capture details from callers, book appointments, and route urgent calls to people. They can also give wrong answers with complete confidence, mishandle emergencies, collect information a business has no reason to keep, and, when used for outbound calls, run afoul of federal telemarketing law.

This workbook is built on a simple premise: the quality of an AI phone agent depends far more on the planning done before it is switched on than on the vendor chosen. A business that knows exactly which calls it receives, which of those an automated agent should handle, what the agent is allowed to say, and when a human must take over will get better results from almost any reputable product than a business that buys first and plans later.

The workbook draws on public sources that bear directly on these decisions: the Federal Communications Commission's 2024 ruling that AI-generated voices are "artificial" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act ([FCC Declaratory Ruling, 2024](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-confirms-tcpa-applies-ai-technologies-generate-human-voices)); a 2024 tribunal decision holding Air Canada responsible for its chatbot's incorrect answer ([Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/)); the Federal Trade Commission's data security principles for business ([FTC, Protecting Personal Information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/protecting-personal-information-guide-business)); and the National Institute of Standards and Technology's AI Risk Management Framework ([NIST AI RMF](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)).

## Who This Book Is For

- Owners and office managers of service businesses that depend on the phone: trades, clinics, salons, law and accounting offices, property managers, and restaurants.
- Teams evaluating AI receptionist or voice agent products.
- Businesses that already use an AI agent and want to make it safer and more accurate.

## What You Will Be Able to Do

- Audit your inbound calls by type, volume, timing, and outcome.
- Decide which calls an AI agent should handle, assist with, or never touch.
- Design an eight-step call flow and write the agent's scripts.
- Build an accurate knowledge sheet that serves as the agent's single source of truth.
- Define human handoff rules, especially for emergencies and upset callers.
- Apply recording-consent, data-minimization, and outbound-calling rules.
- Test the agent systematically before launch and monitor it afterward.

## How the Book Is Organized

Twelve chapters follow the order of a responsible deployment, from the decision to plan through launch and continuous improvement. Each chapter has learning objectives, key definitions, a step-by-step framework, a reference table, a worked illustrative example, a worksheet or checklist, a summary, and review questions. Selected chapters include case studies drawn from public legal and regulatory records. The examples follow Lena, the hypothetical owner of a six-truck plumbing company; Lena and her company are invented to demonstrate the method. Appendices provide templates you can copy: a call audit log, knowledge sheet, test call bank, vendor questions, launch checklist, and weekly review form.

:::warning Disclaimer
This workbook is educational and is not legal advice. Telemarketing, recording-consent, privacy, health-information, and consumer protection laws vary by jurisdiction and change over time; consult a qualified attorney before deploying an AI phone agent, especially for outbound calls or regulated information. Product capabilities differ; confirm features with your vendor. Nothing here guarantees cost savings, revenue, or performance. Case studies describe public legal and regulatory records; the outcomes are those of the parties involved, not TTI's.
:::

# Chapter 1: Why Planning Comes Before Buying

:::objectives
- Explain what an AI phone agent is and what it does.
- Recognize that the business remains responsible for what its agent says.
- Identify the five planning decisions every deployment requires.
- Apply a simple risk framework to AI phone agents.
:::

## What an AI Phone Agent Is

An **AI phone agent** (also called an AI receptionist or voice agent) is software that conducts telephone conversations using speech recognition, a language model that decides what to say, and a synthesized voice. Unlike traditional phone menus ("press 1 for billing"), it can understand natural speech and respond conversationally. Typical capabilities include answering questions, collecting caller details, booking appointments through a calendar integration, sending text confirmations, and transferring calls to staff.

## You Are Responsible for What It Says

The most important principle in this workbook is that an AI agent speaks for your business. In Moffatt v. Air Canada, a British Columbia tribunal considered a customer who relied on the airline's website chatbot, which told him he could apply for a bereavement fare after travel. The airline's actual policy, on another page, did not allow that. The tribunal rejected the argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own actions, writing that "it should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website. It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot," and found that the airline "did not take reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate" ([CBC News](http://www.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.7116416)). A phone agent is no different in principle: what it tells callers is your representation.

## A Risk Framework

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework organizes AI risk work into four functions: Govern (establish a culture, policies, and accountability), Map (understand the context and risks), Measure (test and monitor), and Manage (prioritize and act on risks). NIST notes that after mapping, organizations should have enough context to make "an initial go/no-go decision" about deploying an AI system, and that systems "should be tested before their deployment and regularly while in operation" ([NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)). This workbook applies those functions at small-business scale: Chapter 2 and 3 map, Chapters 4 through 9 design controls, Chapter 10 measures, and Chapter 11 manages and governs.

:::case Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024)
In November 2022, Jake Moffatt asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares after his grandmother died. The chatbot indicated he could buy a regular ticket and apply for a partial refund afterward, while a linked page stated that bereavement requests could not be made after travel ([American Bar Association, Business Law Today](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/)). The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal found negligent misrepresentation and ordered the airline to pay $812.02 (Canadian), including $650.88 in damages, interest, and fees ([Moffatt v. Air Canada decision, 2024 BCCRT 149](https://www.middletemple.org.uk/sites/default/files/civicrm/persist/contribute/files/2024bccrt149.pdf)). The tribunal said customers should not have to double-check one part of a company's website against another. The amount was small; the principle is large. This is a Canadian small-claims decision and not binding in the United States, but it illustrates how a decision-maker may view automated answers. The outcome belongs to the parties, not TTI.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Lena's Starting Point
Lena's plumbing company misses calls during busy mornings and after 6 p.m. A vendor demo impressed her, and she was ready to sign. After reading about the Air Canada decision, she realized she could not say what the agent would tell a caller who asked, "Do you charge extra on weekends?" because the answer depended on who answered the phone. She decided to complete this workbook before choosing a product.
:::

# Chapter 2: The Call Audit

:::objectives
- Collect two weeks of call data.
- Categorize calls by type, timing, and outcome.
- Calculate missed-call patterns.
- Identify the calls with the highest value and highest risk.
:::

## Why Audit First

You cannot design a good agent for calls you have not studied. A **call audit** is a structured record of every inbound call over a defined period, typically two weeks, capturing who called, why, when, and what happened. It reveals which calls are frequent, which are valuable, which are being missed, and which are sensitive. It is the "Map" step in practice.

## Data Sources

- Phone system call logs (time, duration, answered or missed).
- Voicemail transcripts.
- A simple tally sheet kept by whoever answers the phone (Appendix A).
- Booking and job records to connect calls to revenue.

## What to Record for Each Call

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Date and time | Tuesday, 7:42 a.m. |
| Answered, missed, or voicemail | Missed |
| Caller type | New customer, existing customer, vendor, spam |
| Call reason | Emergency leak, quote request, schedule change, billing question |
| Urgency | Emergency, same day, routine |
| Information needed to resolve | Address, problem description, preferred time |
| Outcome | Booked, quoted, transferred, no action |
| Sensitive information involved? | Payment card, health, gate code |

## Analyzing the Audit

After two weeks, summarize:

1. **Volume by reason.** Which five reasons account for most calls?
2. **Timing.** When are calls missed? Early morning, lunch, evenings, weekends?
3. **Value.** Which call types lead to booked jobs?
4. **Risk.** Which involve emergencies, upset callers, or sensitive data?
5. **Resolution needs.** Which require a judgment only staff can make?

:::example Illustrative: Lena's Audit Results
Over two weeks, Lena's company received 412 calls. 23 percent were missed, most before 8 a.m., during lunch, and after 6 p.m. The top reasons were new service requests (31 percent), schedule changes and arrival-time questions (22 percent), quote and price questions (14 percent), emergencies such as active leaks or no water (9 percent), billing (8 percent), vendors and spam (11 percent), and other (5 percent). Emergencies were disproportionately after hours. Several callers offered credit card numbers by phone, and some gave gate or lockbox codes. These figures are hypothetical.

# Chapter 3: Deciding What the Agent Should Do

:::objectives
- Classify call types as green, yellow, or red.
- Define the agent's job description.
- Set boundaries the agent must never cross.
- Choose a starting scope that is small and safe.
:::

## The Traffic-Light Model

Classify every call type from your audit into one of three categories:

| Category | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Green | Agent may handle fully | Business hours, service area, booking routine appointments, taking messages |
| Yellow | Agent may gather information, then a human decides or confirms | Quotes, schedule changes affecting crews, billing disputes, warranty questions |
| Red | Agent must transfer immediately or give a fixed safety message | Emergencies, threats, injuries, legal complaints, very upset callers, regulated advice |

The model forces explicit decisions instead of leaving the agent to improvise.

## The Agent's Job Description

Write a one-paragraph **job description** for the agent, as you would for a new receptionist. Include its purpose, the call types it handles, what it must never do, and to whom it reports problems.

Example: "The agent answers calls when staff are unavailable. It greets callers, identifies the reason for the call, books routine service appointments within our service area, answers questions found in the knowledge sheet, collects details for quotes and billing questions for staff follow-up, and transfers emergencies immediately to the on-call technician. It never quotes prices not listed in the knowledge sheet, never promises arrival times, never collects payment card numbers, and never gives advice about safety beyond the approved emergency script. The office manager reviews its call summaries daily."

## Start Small

Begin with the narrowest useful scope, often after-hours and overflow calls for green call types plus safe routing of red calls. Expand only after testing and several weeks of monitored operation show the agent performs reliably.

:::example Illustrative: Lena's Classification
Green: hours, service area, booking routine drain and fixture appointments, taking messages, sending a text confirmation. Yellow: quotes for water heaters and repiping (collect details, staff call back), schedule changes for the same day, billing questions. Red: active leaks, sewage backups, no water, gas smell (fixed message: leave the building and call the gas utility or 911, then transfer), angry callers, anyone mentioning injury or legal action. Starting scope: after 6 p.m., before 8 a.m., and overflow during lunch.
:::

# Chapter 4: Designing the Call Flow

:::objectives
- Use an eight-step call flow as a design template.
- Define the purpose and exit conditions of each step.
- Map green, yellow, and red paths through the flow.
- Document the flow so staff and vendors share one design.
:::

## The Eight-Step Flow

A **call flow** is the planned sequence of steps a conversation follows, including the branches for different caller needs. Most small-business calls fit an eight-step pattern:

| Step | Purpose | Exit condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Greet and disclose | Identify the business and state that the caller is speaking with an automated assistant | Caller hears disclosure |
| 2. Identify the need | Ask why the caller is calling and classify the reason | Reason matched to green, yellow, or red |
| 3. Safety check | Detect emergency or red-flag words early | Red calls go immediately to handoff |
| 4. Collect information | Gather only the details needed for this call type | Required fields captured and read back |
| 5. Answer from the knowledge sheet | Respond only with approved facts | Question answered, or "I'll have someone confirm" |
| 6. Book or route | Schedule, take a message, or transfer | Action completed |
| 7. Confirm | Summarize what will happen next and when | Caller agrees summary is correct |
| 8. Close and record | End politely; log summary for staff | Summary sent to the responsible person |

## Design Principles

- **Safety before efficiency.** Place the safety check early so that a caller describing a gas smell is not asked for an address before hearing the safety message.
- **Collect the minimum.** Ask only for fields needed for the call type (Chapter 8).
- **Read back critical details.** Names, phone numbers, and addresses are frequently misheard; ask the caller to confirm them.
- **Always offer a human path.** A caller who says "representative," "person," or "operator" should be routed according to your handoff rules.
- **Never dead-end.** Every branch ends in an action: booked, message taken, transferred, or a clear next step.

## Documenting the Flow

Draw the flow as a simple diagram or a numbered outline with branches. For each call type, list the required fields, the knowledge sheet sections the agent may use, the action, and the confirmation script. This document becomes the specification you give the vendor and the basis for tests in Chapter 10.

:::example Illustrative: Lena's Routine Booking Path
Step 1: "Thanks for calling Lena's Plumbing. I'm the company's automated assistant, and I can help schedule service or take a message." Step 2: "What can we help you with today?" Caller: "My kitchen sink drains slowly." Step 3: no red-flag words detected. Step 4: name, callback number, service address (read back), problem description, preferred day. Step 5: caller asks about the trip fee; the agent states the listed fee from the knowledge sheet. Step 6: books the next routine window. Step 7: "You're scheduled for Thursday between 8 and 12. A technician will call 30 minutes before arriving. You'll get a text confirmation." Step 8: close and send summary to the office manager.
:::

# Chapter 5: Writing the Agent's Scripts and Instructions

:::objectives
- Write a clear, honest greeting.
- Draft instructions that define tone, boundaries, and fallback behavior.
- Prepare fixed scripts for sensitive situations.
- Avoid deceptive or overpromising language.
:::

## Two Kinds of Text

AI agents typically use two kinds of text. **Fixed scripts** are exact words the agent says every time, such as the greeting, the emergency message, and the recording notice. **Instructions** (sometimes called a prompt or system prompt) describe how the agent should behave in open conversation: tone, scope, what to do when unsure. Fixed scripts are best for anything legally or safety sensitive; instructions handle the variety of ordinary conversation.

## The Greeting

A good greeting names the business, discloses that the caller is speaking with an automated assistant, states what it can help with, and, if calls are recorded, gives the recording notice. Disclosure supports honesty with callers and reduces the risk of deception claims; for outbound calls using artificial voices, federal rules require identification of the caller (Chapter 9). Example: "Thank you for calling Lena's Plumbing. You've reached our automated assistant. This call may be recorded for quality. I can schedule service, answer common questions, or connect you with our team."

## Writing Instructions

Effective instructions cover:

1. **Role.** "You are the automated phone assistant for Lena's Plumbing."
2. **Scope.** The green, yellow, and red categories in plain language.
3. **Knowledge rule.** "Answer only from the knowledge sheet. If the answer is not there, say you will have a team member confirm, and take a message."
4. **Prohibitions.** Never quote prices not listed, promise arrival times, diagnose problems, give legal or medical advice, or collect payment card numbers.
5. **Tone.** Friendly, brief, plain language; no slang; one question at a time.
6. **Handoff triggers.** When to transfer (Chapter 7).
7. **Fallback.** What to say after two failed attempts to understand the caller.

## Fixed Scripts to Prepare

| Situation | Script purpose |
|---|---|
| Emergency | State safety instructions, then transfer to on-call |
| Life-threatening situation | Direct the caller to hang up and call 911 |
| Request for a human | Acknowledge and transfer or take an urgent message |
| Unknown answer | Promise a confirmed answer from staff with a timeframe |
| Recording notice | State that the call may be recorded |
| Out-of-scope request | Politely explain and take a message |

## Language to Avoid

- Claims the agent is a person, or evasive answers when asked "Am I talking to a robot?"
- Guarantees ("We'll definitely fix it today").
- Unlisted prices or discounts.
- Diagnoses ("That's probably your water heater").
- Pressure tactics.

:::example Illustrative: Lena's Unknown-Answer Script
A caller asks whether the company installs tankless water heaters from a specific brand. The knowledge sheet does not say. The agent responds: "That's a good question, and I want to make sure you get an accurate answer. I'll have our office manager call you back by noon tomorrow. Can I confirm the best number to reach you?" The office manager later adds the answer to the knowledge sheet.
:::

# Chapter 6: The Knowledge Sheet

:::objectives
- Build a single source of truth for the agent.
- Organize facts so the agent can retrieve them reliably.
- Assign ownership and update rules.
- Prevent contradictions like the one in the Air Canada case.
:::

## Single Source of Truth

A **knowledge sheet** (or knowledge base) is the approved set of facts the agent may state: hours, service area, services offered and not offered, listed prices and fees, policies, and frequently asked questions. It should be the single source of truth, meaning the same facts appear on your website, in staff training, and in the agent. The Air Canada case turned on a contradiction between what the chatbot said and what another page stated ([ABA Business Law Today](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/)); a single maintained source reduces that risk.

## Recommended Sections

| Section | Contents |
|---|---|
| Business basics | Legal name, address, hours, holidays, phone, website |
| Service area | Cities or ZIP codes served and not served |
| Services | What you do, what you do not do |
| Pricing | Only prices you are willing to state; trip fees; after-hours fees; "quotes required" items |
| Policies | Cancellation, payment methods, warranties, licensing |
| Scheduling | Appointment windows, lead times, arrival-call policy |
| FAQ | Exact answers to the 20 most common questions from your audit |
| Do not say | Topics the agent must defer to staff |

## Writing Facts the Agent Can Use

- Write short, unambiguous statements: "Our after-hours fee is $X, added to the standard rate, for calls between 6 p.m. and 7 a.m."
- Include the conditions and exceptions in the same entry.
- Avoid "usually" and "typically" unless you want the agent to repeat that uncertainty.
- Date the sheet and record who approved each change.

## Ownership and Updates

Assign one owner. Update the sheet whenever prices, hours, policies, or services change, and before holidays. Review it monthly against the website. Add answers to questions the agent could not answer (from call summaries).

:::example Illustrative: Lena Finds Contradictions
While building her sheet, Lena found that her website listed a trip fee different from the amount her dispatcher quoted, and that the website said "24/7 service" while the company actually offered only emergency service overnight. She corrected the website, wrote one approved entry for each, and told staff. Without this step, the agent would have learned conflicting facts.
:::

:::worksheet Knowledge Sheet Audit

Prices the agent may state: ___
Items that always require a staff quote: ___
Top 20 questions answered? Yes / No

Update triggers (price changes, holidays, new services): ___
:::

:::summary
The knowledge sheet is the single source of approved facts the agent may state. Organize it into basics, service area, services, pricing, policies, scheduling, FAQs, and "do not say" topics. Write short, conditional statements, assign one owner, and update it on every change. Reconciling contradictions protects against the kind of error at issue in the Air Canada case.
:::

# Chapter 7: Human Handoff and Escalation

:::objectives
- Define the triggers that require a human.
- Design warm transfers and urgent messages.
- Build an escalation matrix with response times.
- Plan for when no human is available.
:::

## Why Handoff Matters Most

The handoff design determines how the agent behaves in the calls that matter most: emergencies, frustrated customers, and complex or high-value situations. A **handoff** is the transfer of a call, or its details, from the agent to a person. A **warm transfer** connects the caller to a person and passes along a summary; a **cold transfer** connects without context; an **urgent message** notifies a person to call back within a set time.

## Handoff Triggers

- Safety words: fire, smoke, gas smell, flooding, sparks, injury, unconscious, threat.
- Explicit requests for a person.
- Strong frustration or repeated complaints.
- Mentions of lawyers, lawsuits, regulators, or reviews threatening reputational harm.
- Billing disputes or refunds above a threshold.
- Two consecutive failures to understand the caller.
- Any red call type from Chapter 3.

## The Escalation Matrix

| Trigger | Agent action | Destination | Target response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Life-threatening danger | 911 script, then end or transfer | Caller calls 911 | Immediate |
| Property emergency | Safety script, warm transfer | On-call technician | Immediate; urgent text if unanswered |
| Request for a person (business hours) | Warm transfer | Office | Immediate |
| Request for a person (after hours) | Urgent message | Office manager | Next business morning by 9 a.m. |
| Upset caller or complaint | Acknowledge, urgent message | Owner | Same day |
| Legal or regulatory mention | Take message, no comment | Owner | Same day |

## When No One Answers

Plan for the transfer that fails. The agent should tell the caller what will happen ("I wasn't able to reach the technician directly. I've sent an urgent alert, and they will call you back within 15 minutes."), send the alert by text and email, and, for safety situations, repeat the safety instructions. Test this path explicitly; it is the one most often overlooked.

:::example Illustrative: Lena's Overnight Leak
At 2 a.m., a caller says water is pouring from a ceiling. The agent hears "water pouring," skips the routine questions, and says: "This sounds urgent. If you can do so safely, turn off your main water valve. If there's any electrical danger or someone is hurt, hang up and call 911. I'm connecting you to our on-call technician now." The technician does not answer. The agent collects the callback number and address, sends an urgent text and email, tells the caller to expect a callback within 15 minutes, and triggers a second alert to Lena after 10 minutes.
:::

# Chapter 8: Privacy, Recording Consent, and Data Minimization

:::objectives
- Apply the FTC's five data security principles to phone calls.
- Understand one-party and all-party recording consent.
- Decide what the agent should and should not collect.
- Set retention and access rules for recordings and transcripts.
:::

## Calls Create Records

An AI phone agent typically produces recordings, transcripts, and summaries, and it may store caller names, numbers, addresses, and problem descriptions. Each is **personal information** that must be protected. The FTC notes that businesses receive personal information through many channels, including call centers, and recommends five principles: Take Stock (know what you have), Scale Down (keep only what you need), Lock It (protect what you keep), Pitch It (properly dispose of what you no longer need), and Plan Ahead (create a plan to respond to incidents) ([FTC, Protecting Personal Information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/protecting-personal-information-guide-business)).

## Applying the Five Principles

| Principle | Application to an AI phone agent |
|---|---|
| Take Stock | List every data element the agent collects and where it is stored (vendor platform, CRM, email, text) |
| Scale Down | Collect only required fields; do not collect card numbers, Social Security numbers, or unnecessary health details |
| Lock It | Use strong passwords and multi-factor authentication on the vendor dashboard; limit who can access recordings |
| Pitch It | Set retention periods for recordings and transcripts; confirm the vendor deletes data on schedule |
| Plan Ahead | Know whom to call and what to do if the vendor account or data is compromised |

## Recording Consent

Many AI agents record calls by default. United States law on recording varies. Federal law and most states follow a **one-party consent** rule, meaning a recording is lawful if one party to the conversation consents. A number of states require **all-party consent**; the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press identifies about eleven states, including California, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, and Washington, as primarily all-party, with additional nuances in states such as Connecticut and Nevada for phone calls ([RCFP Reporter's Recording Guide](https://www.rcfp.org/introduction-to-reporters-recording-guide/)). Because callers may be in any state, a common practice is to give a clear recording notice at the start of every call. Confirm the right approach with counsel.

## Sensitive Information

- **Payment cards.** Avoid having the agent collect card numbers by voice. Send a secure payment link instead, or route payment to staff using a compliant process.
- **Health information.** Clinics and other health-care providers may have obligations under health privacy laws; consult counsel and confirm vendor agreements before handling patient information.
- **Access codes.** Gate and lockbox codes should be stored only where technicians need them and removed after the job.

:::example Illustrative: Lena Scales Down
Lena's original intake form asked for 11 fields. She reduced the agent's required fields to name, callback number, service address, problem description, and preferred time. She configured the agent to say, "For your security, we don't take card numbers over the phone; we'll text you a secure payment link," and set transcript retention to 90 days after confirming her vendor supported automatic deletion.
:::

:::checklist Privacy and Consent
- Data inventory lists every field, recording, and storage location.
- Required fields are limited to what each call type needs.
- Agent does not collect payment card or Social Security numbers.
- Recording notice is in the greeting (if recording is on).
- Dashboard uses multi-factor authentication; access limited by role.
- Retention periods set and confirmed with vendor.
- Incident response contacts documented.
- Counsel consulted on recording and any regulated data.
:::

:::summary
Recordings, transcripts, and summaries are personal information. Apply the FTC's Take Stock, Scale Down, Lock It, Pitch It, and Plan Ahead principles. Recording consent varies by state, with some states requiring all-party consent, so many businesses give a recording notice on every call. Avoid collecting card numbers and other sensitive data through the agent.

# Chapter 9: Outbound Calls and the TCPA

:::objectives
- Explain how the TCPA applies to AI-generated voices.
- Distinguish informational from telemarketing calls.
- Identify consent, identification, and opt-out requirements.
- Decide whether to use outbound AI calling at all.
:::

## The FCC's 2024 Ruling

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) restricts calls using an "artificial or prerecorded voice." In February 2024, the Federal Communications Commission issued a declaratory ruling confirming that this restriction covers current AI technologies that generate human voices ([FCC Declaratory Ruling, FCC 24-17](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-confirms-tcpa-applies-ai-technologies-generate-human-voices)). As a result, callers using AI-generated voices must have the prior express consent of the called party unless an emergency purpose or exemption applies, and must meet identification and disclosure requirements; for telemarketing, the FCC noted that prior express written consent is required ([FCC News Release, February 8, 2024](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-400393A1.pdf)).

## Key Terms

- **Prior express consent:** the called party's permission to receive the call, typically given by providing their number for a related purpose.
- **Prior express written consent:** a signed written agreement, including electronic signatures, authorizing telemarketing calls using an artificial or prerecorded voice to a specific number.
- **Telemarketing:** calls encouraging the purchase of goods or services.
- **Informational call:** a call conveying information, such as an appointment reminder, without a sales message.

## Outbound Risk Levels

| Outbound use | Risk level | Planning note |
|---|---|---|
| Returning a call the customer just requested | Lower | Document the request |
| Appointment reminders to existing customers | Moderate | Confirm consent; include identification and opt-out |
| Follow-ups on old quotes with a sales pitch | Higher | Likely telemarketing; written consent needed |
| Cold calls to purchased lists | Highest | Avoid |

## A Conservative Rule for Small Businesses

Start with inbound only. If you add outbound AI calls, limit them to informational calls to customers who have given consent, identify the business at the start, offer an easy opt-out, honor opt-outs promptly, and have counsel review your consent language and scripts. Text messages carry their own TCPA and carrier requirements; review them separately.

:::case FCC Enforcement Against Deepfake Robocalls (2024)
In January 2024, voters in New Hampshire received robocalls using an AI-generated voice imitating President Biden, urging them not to vote in the primary. In September 2024 the FCC finalized a $6 million fine against political consultant Steve Kramer for the spoofed calls, under the Truth in Caller ID Act ([FCC, "FCC Issues $6M Fine for NH Robocalls"](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-issues-6m-fine-nh-robocalls)). Lingo Telecom, the carrier that transmitted the calls, agreed to a $1 million settlement ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/fcc-finalizes-6-million-fine-over-ai-generated-biden-robocalls-2024-09-26/)). This was a deliberate deception case, far from a plumber's appointment reminder, but it shows that regulators actively police AI voice calls and that carriers are also held accountable. The outcome belongs to the parties, not TTI.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Lena Declines Outbound Sales Calls
A vendor offered an add-on to call past customers with water heater promotions. Lena recognized that this would likely be telemarketing requiring written consent she did not have. She limited outbound use to text appointment reminders already covered by her consent process (reviewed by her attorney) and kept all AI voice calling inbound.
:::

:::checklist Outbound Readiness
- Outbound use case defined as informational or telemarketing.
- Consent type documented for every number called.
- Script identifies the business at the start.
- Opt-out offered and honored promptly.
- Do-not-call list maintained.
- Counsel reviewed consent language and scripts.
- Calling hours set to reasonable local times.
:::

:::summary
The FCC confirmed in 2024 that AI-generated voices are "artificial" under the TCPA, so consent, identification, and opt-out requirements apply, with written consent needed for telemarketing. Enforcement against deepfake robocalls shows active oversight. Small businesses should start inbound only and add outbound informational calls only with documented consent and legal review.
:::

# Chapter 10: Choosing a Vendor and Testing Before Launch

:::objectives
- Evaluate vendors against your plan rather than their demo.
- Build a test call bank from your audit.
- Score test calls against clear criteria.
- Make a documented go/no-go decision.
:::

## Evaluate Against Your Plan

Demos show the best case. Evaluate vendors on whether they can implement your call flow, knowledge sheet, escalation matrix, and privacy settings. Key questions (Appendix D) include: Can the agent be restricted to approved knowledge? How are transfers handled when no one answers? Where is data stored, for how long, and who can access it? Does the dashboard support multi-factor authentication? Can recordings be turned off or retention shortened? What happens when the system is down? Can you export call logs?

## Building a Test Call Bank

A **test call bank** is a set of scripted scenarios used to evaluate the agent before launch and after every significant change. Build it from your audit:

| Test type | Examples | Minimum count |
|---|---|---|
| Routine green | Booking, hours, service area | 15 |
| Yellow | Quote request, schedule change | 10 |
| Red | Emergencies, gas smell, injury | 10 |
| Edge cases | Accents, background noise, mumbling, interruptions | 10 |
| Adversarial | Requests for unlisted discounts, attempts to make the agent promise things, "ignore your instructions" | 5 |
| Failure paths | Transfer unanswered, calendar full, system confusion | 5 |

## Scoring

For each test call, score: correct classification (green, yellow, red); accurate information (only knowledge sheet facts); required fields captured and correct; correct action; correct handoff; disclosure and recording notice given; tone appropriate. Treat any failure on a red call as a launch blocker.

## The Go/No-Go Decision

NIST describes using context mapping to inform an initial go/no-go decision and testing before deployment ([NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)). Set criteria in advance, for example: 100 percent of red calls handled correctly; at least 95 percent of green calls correct; no instances of invented prices or policies; all failure paths behave as designed. If criteria are not met, fix and retest.

:::case FTC Action Against DoNotPay (2024)
In September 2024, the Federal Trade Commission announced Operation AI Comply, a set of enforcement actions involving deceptive AI claims. One action targeted DoNotPay, which marketed "the world's first robot lawyer." According to the FTC's complaint, the company did not conduct testing to determine whether its chatbot's output matched the level of a human lawyer and did not hire or retain attorneys. DoNotPay agreed to a proposed order requiring it to pay $193,000, notify subscribers about the service's limitations, and stop claiming it could substitute for a professional service without evidence ([FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes)). FTC Chair Lina Khan stated that "there is no AI exemption from the laws on the books." For a buyer, the lesson is to ask vendors for evidence and run your own tests rather than relying on marketing claims. The outcome belongs to the parties, not TTI.
:::

:::example Illustrative: Lena's Test Results
Lena and her office manager placed 55 test calls. The agent handled all routine bookings but quoted a "senior discount" that did not exist when a tester asked for one, and on one emergency call it asked for an email address before giving safety instructions. Both were launch blockers. The vendor tightened the knowledge restriction and reordered the safety check. A retest of all 55 calls passed.

# Chapter 11: Launch, Monitoring, and Continuous Improvement

:::objectives
- Launch in stages with a rollback plan.
- Track a small set of operating metrics.
- Run a weekly review using call summaries and samples.
- Govern changes so accuracy holds over time.
:::

## Staged Launch

1. **Soft launch.** Enable the agent for one limited window, such as after hours, with staff reviewing every call summary the next morning.
2. **Expand.** Add overflow or additional hours after two to four weeks of acceptable performance.
3. **Rollback plan.** Know exactly how to forward calls back to staff or voicemail within minutes if something goes wrong, and test it.

Inform staff before launch: what the agent does, how handoffs reach them, and how to report problems.

## Operating Metrics

| Metric | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Answer rate | Share of calls answered by a person or agent | Original problem being solved |
| Containment by category | Share of green calls resolved without staff | Efficiency, within scope |
| Handoff success | Share of transfers or urgent messages reaching a person within target time | Safety and service |
| Accuracy errors | Incorrect statements found in review | Liability and trust |
| Caller abandonment | Callers hanging up during the agent conversation | Experience problems |
| Booking conversion | Share of new service calls that book | Business value |

Avoid maximizing containment for its own sake; a high containment rate achieved by keeping callers away from people is a failure.

## The Weekly Review

Each week, the owner or accountable manager spends 30 to 45 minutes: reviewing metrics, listening to or reading a sample of calls (all red calls, plus a random sample of others), logging errors, updating the knowledge sheet, and deciding on one improvement. This applies the Plan-Do-Study-Act improvement cycle widely used in quality improvement ([Institute for Healthcare Improvement, Model for Improvement](https://www.ihi.org/library/model-for-improvement)). Retest affected scenarios from the test bank after any change.

## Governance

NIST's Govern function emphasizes accountability and policies ([NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)). For a small business, governance means: one named owner, a change log for scripts and knowledge, a rule that changes are tested before going live, a quarterly review of scope and compliance, and a clear path for staff and customers to report problems.

:::example Illustrative: Lena's First Month
In the first month, after-hours calls were all answered, and the weekly reviews caught two issues: callers asking about drain cleaning "specials" from an old flyer, and a handoff text that went to a technician who had left the company. Lena added an entry explaining the flyer had expired and updated the on-call list, adding "update on-call contacts" to her staff-departure checklist. These results are hypothetical.
:::

Knowledge sheet updates: ___

:::summary
Launch in stages with a tested rollback plan. Track answer rate, containment by category, handoff success, accuracy errors, abandonment, and booking conversion, without treating containment as a goal in itself. Run a weekly Plan-Do-Study-Act review and govern changes with an owner, change log, and retesting.

# Chapter 12: Adapting the Plan to Your Industry

:::objectives

## Same Method, Different Risks

The method in this workbook (audit, classify, design, script, ground in knowledge, escalate, protect, test, and monitor) applies to every business that answers the phone. What changes from one industry to another is the mix of calls, the definition of an emergency, the kinds of sensitive information callers share, and the professional rules that limit what anyone, human or machine, may say. This chapter summarizes common patterns so you can adapt the earlier chapters. Treat these as starting points to verify against your own audit and with your professional advisers, not as rules.

## Industry Patterns

| Industry | Common green calls | Typical red calls | Sensitive data to minimize |
|---|---|---|---|
| Home services (plumbing, HVAC, electrical) | Booking, service area, hours, fees | Gas smell, flooding, no heat in freezing weather, sparks | Gate codes, payment cards |
| Dental and medical clinics | Hours, location, appointment requests, insurance accepted (general) | Severe pain, bleeding, breathing problems, medication reactions | Health details, insurance numbers, birth dates |
| Salons and spas | Booking, services menu, prices, cancellation policy | Allergic reactions, injury complaints | Payment cards |
| Law and accounting offices | Hours, practice areas, booking consultations | Deadlines, court dates, threats, conflicts | Case facts, tax identifiers |
| Property management | Office hours, rent portal directions, showing requests | Fire, flooding, no heat, security issues, lockouts | Unit access, tenant identity data |
| Restaurants | Hours, reservations, menu, directions | Allergic reaction reports, food safety complaints | Payment cards |

## Professional and Regulatory Boundaries

Several industries have boundaries an agent must respect. Health-care providers should keep the agent away from clinical advice and confirm their obligations for patient information before the agent handles it. Law offices must avoid anything that sounds like legal advice and should be careful about forming an attorney-client relationship or receiving confidential facts through an automated intake; a common approach is to collect only contact details and the general subject of the matter. Property managers should be aware that fair housing laws apply to how they communicate with prospective tenants; instruct the agent to give the same factual information to every caller and avoid screening questions not approved by counsel. In every case, the knowledge sheet should state what the agent must defer to a licensed professional.

2. Ask a licensed professional in your field to review the red list.
3. Add a "never discuss" list specific to your profession.
4. Shrink the required fields for any call type that involves regulated information.
5. Write a fixed script for every red call type, and test each one.

The practice classifies booking and rescheduling as green, insurance and billing questions as yellow (the agent collects the caller's name and callback number, and staff return the call), and reports of facial swelling, uncontrolled bleeding, or difficulty breathing as red, with a fixed script directing the caller to emergency care when appropriate and an urgent alert to the on-call dentist. The agent does not ask for a description of symptoms beyond what is needed to route the call, and the practice reviews its patient information obligations with counsel before launch.

The following sample scripts illustrate the tone and structure recommended in Chapter 5. Adapt the wording to your business, and have counsel review any script involving recording notices, outbound calls, or regulated information.

**Transfer failed.** "I wasn't able to reach our on-call team member directly. I've sent an urgent alert with your details, and someone will call you back within [time]. If the situation becomes dangerous, please call 911."

**Request for a person after hours.** "Our office is closed right now. I can take a detailed message and mark it for a callback first thing tomorrow morning, or if this is an emergency, I can connect you with our on-call team. Which would you like?"

**Upset caller.** "I'm sorry this has been frustrating. I want to make sure the right person hears this. May I take down what happened so our [owner/manager] can call you back today?"

**Unknown answer.** "I want to give you an accurate answer, so I'll have a team member confirm and call you back by [time]. What's the best number to reach you?"

**Price not listed.** "That job needs a quote from one of our technicians so the price is accurate. I can set up a time for an estimate or have someone call you. Which works better?"

**Payment request.** "For your security, we don't take card numbers over the phone. We'll text you a secure payment link."

**Misunderstanding fallback (after two attempts).** "I'm sorry, I'm having trouble understanding. Let me take your name and number so a team member can call you back," or transfer during business hours.

**Read-back confirmation.** "Let me make sure I have this right: [name], at [phone number], service at [address], for [problem]. Is that correct?"

**Close.** "You're all set. [Summary of next step and time]. You'll receive a text confirmation. Thank you for calling [Business]."

**Outbound informational reminder (only with documented consent and counsel review).** "Hello, this is an automated reminder from [Business] about your appointment on [date] between [window]. To confirm, say 'confirm.' To reschedule, say 'reschedule.' To stop receiving these calls, say 'stop.'"

# Appendix I: Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does planning take?** Most small businesses can complete the audit in two weeks and the remaining planning steps in another two to three weeks of part-time work. Testing usually takes several days, plus time for fixes and retesting. Rushing these steps shifts the work to after launch, when errors reach real callers.

**Do I need to tell callers they are talking to an automated assistant?** This workbook recommends disclosing automation in every greeting. Disclosure supports honest dealing with callers, and for outbound calls using artificial voices, federal rules require identification of the caller. Ask counsel about any additional state requirements that apply to your business.

**Should the agent ever handle payments?** The conservative approach is no. Sending a secure payment link or routing payment to staff keeps card numbers out of recordings and transcripts, which reduces both risk and compliance burden.

**What if callers prefer a person?** Always offer a human path. Some callers will choose a person every time, and that is acceptable. The goal is to answer every call well, not to keep callers away from staff.

**Can the agent learn on its own from calls?** Some products adjust behavior based on past calls or use call data to improve models. Ask your vendor exactly how call data is used, whether you can opt out, and how changes to agent behavior are reviewed. In your own operation, treat every change to scripts and knowledge as a controlled change that is tested before it goes live.

**How do I know whether it is working?** Compare your weekly metrics with your audit baseline: answer rate, handoff success, accuracy errors, abandonment, and booking conversion. Combine numbers with listening to real calls; metrics alone can hide poor experiences.

**When should I expand the scope?** Expand after several weeks in which the agent meets your go/no-go criteria in live operation, red calls are handled correctly, and the weekly review finds few accuracy errors. Expand one call type or time window at a time, and retest before each expansion.

**What if something goes wrong?** Use your rollback procedure to forward calls to staff or voicemail, contact affected callers if needed, document what happened in the change log, fix and retest, and only then re-enable the agent. If personal information may have been exposed, follow your incident plan and seek legal advice.

# Glossary

**AI phone agent:** Software that conducts telephone conversations using speech recognition, a language model, and a synthesized voice.

**All-party consent:** A legal rule requiring every party to a conversation to consent to recording.

**Call audit:** A structured record of inbound calls over a set period.

**Call flow:** The planned sequence of conversation steps and branches.

**Cold transfer:** Connecting a caller to a person without passing context.

**Containment:** The share of calls resolved without staff involvement.

**Escalation matrix:** A table mapping triggers to actions, destinations, and response times.

**Fixed script:** Exact words the agent says every time in a defined situation.

**Handoff:** Transfer of a call or its details from the agent to a person.

**Informational call:** A call conveying information without a sales message.

**Instructions:** Text that guides an agent's behavior in open conversation.

**Knowledge sheet:** The approved set of facts the agent may state.

**One-party consent:** A legal rule allowing recording when one party consents.

**Prior express written consent:** Signed written authorization for telemarketing calls using an artificial or prerecorded voice.

**TCPA:** The Telephone Consumer Protection Act, a federal law restricting certain calls and texts.

**Telemarketing:** Calls encouraging the purchase of goods or services.

**Test call bank:** A set of scripted scenarios used to evaluate an agent.

**Traffic-light model:** Classification of call types as green, yellow, or red.

**Warm transfer:** Connecting a caller to a person while passing a summary.
